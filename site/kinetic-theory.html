1 Basics

Ideal-gas model and equation of state

An ideal gas models molecules as negligibly small particles with negligible interactions except elastic collisions. It approximates dilute gases away from condensation. Temperature must be absolute and pressure must be absolute rather than gauge.

The mole form counts amount of substance, while the particle form counts molecules. Boyle’s, Charles’s and pressure–temperature laws follow by holding the appropriate variable fixed. Real gases can depart from this model at high density or low temperature.

Ideal-gas model and equation of state
\[ pV=nRT=Nk_BT,\qquad N=nN_A,\qquad R=N_Ak_B \]
Symbols and assumptions: p pressure (Pa); V volume (m³); n amount (mol); R gas constant (J/mol K); N molecule count; k_B Boltzmann constant (J/K); T absolute temperature (K); N_A Avogadro constant (mol⁻¹). Ideal thermal equilibrium is assumed.
Other useful formulas
Density form of the gas equation
\[ P=\frac{\rho RT}{M}\quad\Rightarrow\quad \rho=\frac{PM}{RT} \]
Symbols and assumptions: P absolute pressure (Pa); ρ gas density (kg/m³); R = 8.31 J/mol K; T absolute temperature (K); M molar mass in kg/mol, not g/mol. Ideal gas.
Number density
\[ n_N=\frac{N}{V}=\frac{P}{k_BT} \]
Symbols and assumptions: n_N molecules per unit volume (m⁻³); N number of molecules; V volume (m³); P pressure (Pa); k_B = 1.38×10⁻²³ J/K; T absolute temperature (K). Same for all ideal gases at given P and T.
Watch the assumption

Use the same particle definition when counting N; dissociating molecules changes the number of particles.

Worked example

At fixed amount and volume, an ideal gas warms from 300 to 450 K. Find the pressure ratio.

Show solution

p∝T under these constraints, so p₂/p₁=450/300=1.5.

Worked example · Numerical

Find the density of oxygen gas (M = 32 g/mol) at 27 °C and 1.013×10⁵ Pa. Take R = 8.31 J/mol K.

Show solution
  1. Convert units: M = 0.032 kg/mol and T = 27 + 273 = 300 K.
  2. Use \(\rho=PM/(RT)\).
  3. \(\rho=\dfrac{1.013\times10^5\times0.032}{8.31\times300}=\dfrac{3241.6}{2493}\approx1.30\) kg/m³.
  4. Check: air (M ≈ 29 g/mol) has density about 1.2 kg/m³ at room conditions, so a slightly heavier gas at about 1.3 kg/m³ is sensible.

Answer: About 1.30 kg/m³.

More solved problem patterns
Worked example · Numerical

An air bubble rises from the bottom of a lake to the surface and its volume becomes three times larger. Temperature stays constant. Atmospheric pressure is 10⁵ Pa, water density is 1000 kg/m³ and g = 10 m/s². How deep is the lake?

Show solution
  1. Temperature and the amount of air are fixed, so Boyle’s law applies: \(P_1V_1=P_2V_2\).
  2. At the surface P₂ = P₀ and V₂ = 3V₁. So the pressure at the bottom is \(P_1=3P_0\).
  3. At depth h, \(P_1=P_0+\rho gh\). So \(\rho gh=2P_0\).
  4. \(h=\dfrac{2\times10^5}{1000\times10}=20\) m.

Answer: 20 m.

Worked example · Graph

A fixed mass of ideal gas goes from state A (300 K, 1.0 atm) to state B (600 K, 1.5 atm). On a P–T graph, which state lies on the steeper line drawn from the origin, and does the volume increase or decrease?

Show solution
  1. For each state, the line from the origin has slope \(P/T=nR/V\).
  2. Slope at A = 1.0/300 = 1/300. Slope at B = 1.5/600 = 1/400.
  3. A lies on the steeper line, so A has the smaller volume.
  4. Check directly: \(V\propto T/P\). \(V_B/V_A=(600/1.5)/(300/1.0)=400/300=4/3\).

Answer: A is on the steeper line. The volume increases by a factor 4/3 from A to B.

Assumptions of the kinetic theory

Open a bottle of perfume in one corner of a room and you smell it in the far corner a little later. Gas molecules are always moving, and they spread out to fill whatever space they get. Kinetic theory turns that picture into numbers. It works because it makes a few simple assumptions about the molecules:

  1. A gas is made of a very large number of identical molecules. Each one behaves like a tiny hard sphere.
  2. The molecules themselves take up negligible volume compared with the container.
  3. They move randomly in all directions and obey Newton’s laws.
  4. There are no forces between molecules except during collisions, so they move in straight lines between collisions. Gravity on them is neglected.
  5. Collisions with each other and with the walls are perfectly elastic, so kinetic energy is conserved.
  6. A collision lasts a negligible time compared with the time between collisions.

A gas that obeys these assumptions exactly is an ideal gas. Real gases come close at low pressure and high temperature, where molecules are far apart and fast.

The gas laws are special cases of PV = nRT

Hold two of the four quantities (n, P, V, T) fixed and the equation of state gives one of the named laws.

  • Boyle’s law (n, T fixed): \(PV=\) constant. The P–V graph is a rectangular hyperbola. A hotter isotherm lies farther from the origin. A graph of PV against P is a horizontal line at height nRT.
  • Charles’s law (n, P fixed): \(V\propto T\). The V–T graph is a straight line through the origin, with slope nR/P. A steeper line means a lower pressure.
  • Gay-Lussac’s (pressure) law (n, V fixed): \(P\propto T\). The P–T graph is a straight line through the origin, with slope nR/V. A steeper line means a smaller volume.
  • Avogadro’s law (P, T fixed): \(V\propto n\). Equal volumes of all ideal gases at the same P and T hold the same number of molecules. One mole occupies about 22.4 L at 0 °C and 1 atm.

If T is plotted in °C instead of K, the V–T and P–T lines no longer pass through the origin. Extended backwards, they all meet the temperature axis at −273.15 °C.

To compare two states on any graph, draw lines from the origin. On a P–T graph, the state on the steeper line from O has the smaller volume, because \(V=nR\,(T/P)\).

Useful rearrangements and real gases

Write \(n=m/M\) to bring in mass and density: \(P=\dfrac{\rho RT}{M}\). At fixed P and T, gas density is proportional to molar mass. Write \(N=PV/(k_BT)\) to count molecules: the number density is \(P/(k_BT)\), the same for every ideal gas at the same P and T.

Real gases depart from PV = nRT at high pressure and low temperature. Molecules then occupy a noticeable part of the volume and attract each other. The van der Waals equation \(\left(P+\dfrac{an^2}{V^2}\right)(V-nb)=nRT\) corrects for both effects: b for molecular volume and a for attraction. For NEET you only need the idea: a real gas behaves most ideally at low pressure and high temperature.

Question patterns
  • “Two isochores (or isobars) make angles θ₁ and θ₂ with the T axis. Find V₁/V₂ (or P₁/P₂).” Use slope = tan θ ∝ 1/V or 1/P.
  • “A gas leaks from a cylinder and both P and T fall. What fraction of gas escaped?” Use m ∝ PV/T at fixed V.
  • “An air bubble rises from the bottom of a lake and its volume becomes k times. Find the depth.”
  • Graph shape questions: PV against P at constant T, P against 1/V, V against T in °C.
  • Density of a gas at given P and T, or comparing densities of two gases (ρ ∝ PM/T).
Another common mistake

Proportionality uses kelvin. Heating a gas from 27 °C to 54 °C at constant volume does not double its pressure. The ratio is 327/300 = 1.09.

Another common mistake

On a P–T or V–T graph, a steeper line through the origin means a smaller volume (P–T) or a smaller pressure (V–T). Students often read steeper as larger.

2 Core

Pressure from molecular momentum transfer

Gas pressure comes from molecules changing momentum when they collide with container walls. In equilibrium their random directions are isotropic, so the average squared velocity component in each direction is one third of the total.

Bulk motion of the whole container does not add to pressure: collisions depend on velocities relative to the walls. Derivation assumes elastic wall impacts and many molecules, giving a smooth time-averaged force.

Pressure from molecular momentum transfer
\[ p=\frac13\rho\langle v^2\rangle=\frac13\rho v_{rms}^2 \]
Symbols and assumptions: p pressure (Pa); ρ gas mass density (kg/m³); ⟨v²⟩ mean squared molecular speed relative to the gas bulk (m²/s²); v_rms its square root (m/s). Assume equilibrium isotropic random motion and ideal elastic collisions.
Other useful formulas
Pressure from molecular quantities
\[ P=\frac13\,\frac{mN}{V}\,v_{rms}^2=\frac13\,m\,n_N\,v_{rms}^2 \]
Symbols and assumptions: P pressure (Pa); m mass of one molecule (kg); N number of molecules; V volume (m³); n_N = N/V number density (m⁻³); v_rms root-mean-square speed (m/s). Ideal gas, random isotropic motion, elastic collisions.
Pressure and kinetic energy
\[ PV=\frac23E,\qquad P=\frac23\,\frac{E}{V},\qquad \frac12mv_{rms}^2=\frac32k_BT \]
Symbols and assumptions: E total translational kinetic energy of the gas (J); E/V translational KE per unit volume (J/m³); k_B = 1.38×10⁻²³ J/K; T absolute temperature (K). Only translational energy enters, even for diatomic gases.
Watch the assumption

Mean vector velocity can be zero while mean squared speed is positive.

Worked example

A gas has density 1.2 kg/m³ and rms speed 500 m/s. Find pressure.

Show solution

p=(1/3)×1.2×500²=100000 Pa. The molecular speeds are random relative to the gas, not a common drift velocity.

Worked example · Numerical

A 2.0 L container holds an ideal gas at 1.0×10⁵ Pa. Find the total translational kinetic energy of the molecules.

Show solution
  1. Use \(PV=\frac23E\), so \(E=\frac32PV\).
  2. Convert volume: 2.0 L = 2.0×10⁻³ m³.
  3. \(E=1.5\times1.0\times10^5\times2.0\times10^{-3}=300\) J.
  4. The answer does not depend on the type of gas, because only translational energy is involved.

Answer: 300 J.

Worked example · Ratio

In a gas, the mass of each molecule is halved and its rms speed is doubled. The number of molecules per unit volume is unchanged. By what factor does the pressure change?

Show solution
  1. \(P=\frac13mn_Nv_{rms}^2\), so \(P\propto mv_{rms}^2\) at fixed \(n_N\).
  2. New factor = \(\frac12\times2^2=2\).
  3. The pressure doubles. Each impact delivers less momentum (half the mass, double the speed gives the same momentum per hit) but impacts happen twice as often.

Answer: The pressure doubles.

Worked example · Concept

Five molecules have speeds 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5 km/s. Find their mean speed and rms speed. Which one decides the pressure?

Show solution
  1. Mean speed = (1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 5)/5 = 3 km/s.
  2. Mean of squares = (1 + 4 + 9 + 16 + 25)/5 = 11 (km/s)².
  3. \(v_{rms}=\sqrt{11}\approx3.32\) km/s, larger than the mean speed.
  4. Pressure depends on momentum transfer per hit (∝ v) times hit rate (∝ v), so it depends on ⟨v²⟩, that is on \(v_{rms}\).

Answer: Mean speed 3 km/s, rms speed about 3.32 km/s. Pressure depends on the rms speed.

Derivation: pressure from molecular collisions

Take a cube of side L containing N molecules, each of mass m. Look at one molecule with velocity components \((v_x, v_y, v_z)\) and follow its motion along x.

  1. It hits the right wall (wall A, area L²) and bounces back elastically. Its x-momentum changes from \(+mv_x\) to \(-mv_x\). The change is \(-2mv_x\), so the wall receives an impulse \(2mv_x\). The y and z components do not change.
  2. Before it hits wall A again it must cross to the far wall and come back, a distance 2L. Time between hits on wall A: \(\Delta t=2L/v_x\).
  3. Average force on wall A from this molecule: \(\dfrac{2mv_x}{2L/v_x}=\dfrac{mv_x^2}{L}\).
  4. Add all N molecules: \(F=\dfrac{m}{L}\sum v_x^2=\dfrac{mN}{L}\langle v_x^2\rangle\).
  5. Pressure: \(P=\dfrac{F}{L^2}=\dfrac{mN}{V}\langle v_x^2\rangle\).
  6. Motion is random, so no direction is special: \(\langle v_x^2\rangle=\langle v_y^2\rangle=\langle v_z^2\rangle\). Since \(v^2=v_x^2+v_y^2+v_z^2\), each equals \(\langle v^2\rangle/3\).
  7. Therefore \(P=\dfrac13\dfrac{mN}{V}\langle v^2\rangle=\dfrac13\rho v_{rms}^2\), with \(\rho=mN/V\).

Collisions between molecules do not spoil the result. Momentum is conserved in each collision, so on average the momentum delivered to the wall is unchanged. The shape of the container does not matter either; the cube just keeps the algebra simple.

Pressure, kinetic energy and the meaning of temperature

Multiply \(P=\frac13\frac{mN}{V}v_{rms}^2\) by V: \(PV=\frac13Nmv_{rms}^2=\frac23\left(\frac12Nmv_{rms}^2\right)=\frac23E\), where E is the total translational kinetic energy. So pressure equals two-thirds of the translational kinetic energy per unit volume.

Now compare with the ideal gas law \(PV=Nk_BT\). Then \(\frac23E=Nk_BT\), so the average translational kinetic energy of one molecule is \(\frac12mv_{rms}^2=\frac32k_BT\).

This is the kinetic meaning of temperature: absolute temperature measures the average translational kinetic energy of the molecules. It does not depend on the mass of the molecule, the pressure or the kind of gas. A hydrogen molecule and an oxygen molecule in the same room have the same average translational kinetic energy.

The “rms” in \(v_{rms}\) means root of the mean of the squares. For speeds 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5 km/s, the mean is 3 km/s but \(v_{rms}=\sqrt{(1+4+9+16+25)/5}=\sqrt{11}\approx3.32\) km/s. Squaring gives more weight to the fast molecules, so \(v_{rms}\) is always at least the mean speed.

Question patterns
  • “Find the pressure from density and rms speed”, or the reverse, using P = ρv²ᵣₘₛ/3.
  • “The kinetic energy per unit volume of a gas at pressure P is …” Answer: 3P/2.
  • Steps of the derivation: impulse per collision (2mvₓ), time between collisions with the same wall (2L/vₓ).
  • Ratio questions: what happens to P if the mass of each molecule halves and the speed doubles at fixed number density.
  • Assertion–reason on why pressure is the same on all walls, or why a moving container does not change gas pressure.
Another common mistake

The factor 1/3 comes from sharing ⟨v²⟩ equally among three directions. It does not come from the cube having three pairs of walls.

Another common mistake

PV = (2/3)E uses only translational kinetic energy. For a diatomic gas the total internal energy is larger, so PV ≠ (2/3)U for O₂ or N₂.

Check the idea

Molecular energy and characteristic speeds

Absolute temperature measures average translational kinetic energy per molecule in an ideal gas. Equal temperatures give equal mean translational energies for different species, but lighter molecules need higher speeds to carry that energy.

Rms speed, mean speed and most probable speed are different summaries of the speed distribution. For an equilibrium ideal gas they follow a fixed ordering. Use molecular mass with k_B or molar mass with R, without mixing the two.

Molecular energy and characteristic speeds
\[ \langle K_{tr}\rangle=\tfrac32k_BT,\quad v_{rms}=\sqrt{\frac{3RT}{M}},\quad \bar v=\sqrt{\frac{8RT}{\pi M}},\quad v_{mp}=\sqrt{\frac{2RT}{M}} \]
Symbols and assumptions: K_tr mean translational energy per molecule (J); k_B constant (J/K); T temperature (K); R gas constant; M molar mass (kg/mol); v_rms/mean/most-probable speeds in m/s. Assume Maxwell equilibrium distribution.
Other useful formulas
Other forms of the rms speed
\[ v_{rms}=\sqrt{\frac{3k_BT}{m}}=\sqrt{\frac{3RT}{M}}=\sqrt{\frac{3P}{\rho}} \]
Symbols and assumptions: k_B = 1.38×10⁻²³ J/K; m mass of one molecule (kg); R = 8.31 J/mol K; M molar mass (kg/mol); T absolute temperature (K); P pressure (Pa); ρ density (kg/m³). Ideal gas in equilibrium.
Ratio of the three speeds
\[ v_{rms}:\bar v:v_{mp}=\sqrt3:\sqrt{\tfrac{8}{\pi}}:\sqrt2\approx1.73:1.60:1.41 \]
Symbols and assumptions: v_rms rms speed, v̄ mean speed, v_mp most probable speed, all in m/s for the same gas at the same temperature. Holds for any ideal gas with a Maxwell distribution.
Speed of sound and rms speed
\[ v_s=\sqrt{\frac{\gamma RT}{M}}=\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{3}}\;v_{rms} \]
Symbols and assumptions: v_s speed of sound in the gas (m/s); γ = C_P/C_V (no unit); R = 8.31 J/mol K; T absolute temperature (K); M molar mass (kg/mol). Ideal gas, adiabatic sound waves.
Watch the assumption

Molar mass in g/mol must be converted to kg/mol when using SI R.

Worked example

At the same temperature, compare rms speeds of gases with molar masses 4 and 16 g/mol.

Show solution

v₄/v₁₆=√(16/4)=2. The lighter species is twice as fast, while both have the same average translational energy per molecule.

Worked example · Numerical

Find the rms speed of nitrogen molecules (M = 28 g/mol) at 27 °C. Take R = 8.31 J/mol K.

Show solution
  1. Convert: M = 0.028 kg/mol, T = 300 K.
  2. \(v_{rms}=\sqrt{\dfrac{3RT}{M}}=\sqrt{\dfrac{3\times8.31\times300}{0.028}}\).
  3. \(=\sqrt{2.671\times10^5}\approx517\) m/s.
  4. If you forget to convert M and use 28, you get about 16 m/s, which is far too small for a gas at room temperature.

Answer: About 517 m/s.

Worked example · Ratio

At what temperature will the rms speed of hydrogen (M = 2 g/mol) equal the rms speed of oxygen (M = 32 g/mol) at 47 °C?

Show solution
  1. Equal rms speeds need equal T/M.
  2. Oxygen: T = 47 + 273 = 320 K, so T/M = 320/32 = 10 K mol/g.
  3. Hydrogen: T = 10 × 2 = 20 K.
  4. Hydrogen is 16 times lighter, so it needs a 16 times lower kelvin temperature to move as fast.

Answer: 20 K (about −253 °C).

Worked example · Graph

Maxwell speed distributions of H₂ and O₂ are drawn at the same temperature. One curve peaks at a low speed and is tall and narrow. The other peaks at a high speed and is low and broad. Which is which, and how far apart are the peaks?

Show solution
  1. At fixed T, \(v_{mp}=\sqrt{2RT/M}\propto1/\sqrt M\).
  2. Oxygen is heavier, so its peak is at a lower speed. The total area is the same, so a curve squeezed into a narrower speed range must be taller.
  3. The tall, narrow, low-speed curve is O₂. The low, broad curve is H₂.
  4. Ratio of peak speeds: \(v_{mp,H_2}/v_{mp,O_2}=\sqrt{32/2}=4\).

Answer: Tall narrow curve: O₂. Low broad curve: H₂. The H₂ peak is at 4 times the speed of the O₂ peak.

Worked example · Numerical

The rms speed of the molecules of a diatomic gas (γ = 1.4) is 500 m/s at some temperature. Find the speed of sound in the gas at that temperature.

Show solution
  1. \(v_s/v_{rms}=\sqrt{\gamma/3}=\sqrt{1.4/3}=\sqrt{0.467}\approx0.683\).
  2. \(v_s\approx0.683\times500\approx342\) m/s.
  3. Sound is a pressure disturbance carried by the molecules, so it cannot travel faster than the molecules themselves.

Answer: About 342 m/s.

Kinetic energy per molecule and per mole

From the pressure derivation, every ideal-gas molecule has average translational kinetic energy \(\frac32k_BT\), whatever its mass. At 300 K this is \(1.5\times1.38\times10^{-23}\times300\approx6.2\times10^{-21}\) J. One mole contains \(N_A\) molecules, so its translational kinetic energy is \(\frac32RT\).

Since \(\frac12mv_{rms}^2=\frac32k_BT\), we get \(v_{rms}=\sqrt{3k_BT/m}=\sqrt{3RT/M}\). Using \(P=\rho RT/M\) this is also \(v_{rms}=\sqrt{3P/\rho}\).

  • At a fixed temperature, \(v_{rms}\propto1/\sqrt{M}\): lighter gases move faster.
  • For a given gas, \(v_{rms}\propto\sqrt{T}\). Changing pressure at constant temperature does not change \(v_{rms}\), because density changes in the same ratio as pressure.
  • Classically, all molecular motion would stop at T = 0 K. That is the kinetic meaning of absolute zero.

Three speeds and their fixed ratio

The molecules in a gas do not all move at the same speed. Three summaries of the distribution are used:

  • Most probable speed \(v_{mp}=\sqrt{2RT/M}\): the speed at the peak of the distribution.
  • Average (mean) speed \(\bar v=\sqrt{8RT/(\pi M)}\): the ordinary average of the speeds.
  • Rms speed \(v_{rms}=\sqrt{3RT/M}\): the square root of the average of v².

All three have the form \(\sqrt{\text{number}\times RT/M}\), so their ratio is fixed for every gas at every temperature:

\[v_{rms}:\bar v:v_{mp}=\sqrt3:\sqrt{8/\pi}:\sqrt2\approx1.73:1.60:1.41\]

So \(v_{rms}>\bar v>v_{mp}\). The mean vector velocity is zero, because molecules move equally in all directions. Never use it in place of a speed.

Maxwell speed distribution, escape of light gases and sound

The Maxwell distribution gives the fraction of molecules per unit speed interval. Its shape:

  • It starts at zero (very few molecules are almost at rest), rises to a peak at \(v_{mp}\), then falls with a long tail of fast molecules.
  • The area under the curve is the total number (or fraction) of molecules, so it stays the same when T changes.
  • At higher T the peak moves to a higher speed and becomes lower, and the curve spreads out. The area stays the same.
  • At the same T, a heavier gas has a narrower, taller curve peaked at a lower speed. Raising M has the same effect as lowering T, because only T/M appears.

Why Earth has almost no hydrogen. Escape speed from Earth is 11.2 km/s. At 300 K, \(v_{rms}\) of H₂ is only about 1.9 km/s. But the long tail always contains some molecules faster than the escape speed, and for light molecules this fraction is much larger. Over millions of years H₂ and He leak away, while N₂ and O₂ stay. The Moon’s escape speed is only about 2.4 km/s, so it has lost almost all its atmosphere.

Sound versus molecules. The speed of sound in a gas is \(v_s=\sqrt{\gamma RT/M}\). Dividing, \(v_s/v_{rms}=\sqrt{\gamma/3}\). Since γ ≤ 5/3, sound is always slower than the rms speed. For air (γ = 1.4) the ratio is about 0.68.

Question patterns
  • “At what temperature will v_rms of gas X equal that of gas Y at T?” Use T/M = constant.
  • “v_rms becomes double (or half) when the temperature changes from 27 °C to …”
  • Ratio v_rms : v̄ : v_mp, or which speed is largest.
  • Maxwell graph questions: identify the higher temperature or the lighter gas; does the area change; where the peak moves.
  • “Why is hydrogen rare in Earth’s atmosphere?” or “Why has the Moon no atmosphere?”
  • Ratio of speed of sound to v_rms for a given γ.
Another common mistake

At constant temperature, doubling the pressure leaves \(v_{rms}\) unchanged. The formula \(\sqrt{3P/\rho}\) does not mean \(v_{rms}\propto\sqrt P\), because ρ doubles too.

Another common mistake

To double \(v_{rms}\) you must multiply the kelvin temperature by 4. From 27 °C (300 K) that means 1200 K, which is 927 °C, not 108 °C or 1200 °C.

Another common mistake

(3/2)k_BT is the translational kinetic energy per molecule. For a diatomic gas the total kinetic energy per molecule is larger, (5/2)k_BT, because rotation adds energy.

Degrees of freedom and heat capacities

Each active quadratic degree of freedom contributes k_BT/2 per molecule on average in the classical model. A monatomic gas has three translational degrees; a diatomic gas at ordinary temperatures often has three translational and two rotational ones.

Vibrational modes can become active at higher temperatures and are suppressed at lower temperatures by quantum effects. Heat capacities therefore depend on which modes are active; do not apply a single diatomic value at every temperature.

Degrees of freedom and heat capacities
\[ U=\tfrac f2nRT,\quad C_V=\tfrac f2R,\quad C_P=C_V+R,\quad \gamma=\frac{C_P}{C_V}=\frac{f+2}{f} \]
Symbols and assumptions: U internal energy (J); f number of active quadratic degrees of freedom per molecule; n amount (mol); T temperature (K); C_V/C_P molar capacities (J/mol K); γ ratio. Ideal gas with temperature-independent f over the interval is assumed.
Other useful formulas
Average energy from equipartition
\[ \bar\varepsilon=\frac f2k_BT\ \text{per molecule},\qquad U=\frac f2nRT,\qquad f=3N-k \]
Symbols and assumptions: ε̄ average energy per molecule (J); f active degrees of freedom; k_B = 1.38×10⁻²³ J/K; T absolute temperature (K); U internal energy of n moles of ideal gas (J); R = 8.31 J/mol K; N atoms per molecule; k number of independent constraints (rigid bonds). Classical equilibrium.
Watch the assumption

Each harmonic vibrational mode adds two quadratic contributions, one kinetic and one potential.

Worked example

For a monatomic ideal gas, find C_V, C_P and γ.

Show solution

f=3 gives C_V=3R/2, C_P=5R/2 and γ=5/3. Internal energy includes translational kinetic energy in this ideal model.

Worked example · Numerical

Find the total internal energy of 2 mol of oxygen (rigid diatomic) at 27 °C. Split it into translational and rotational parts. Take R = 8.31 J/mol K.

Show solution
  1. Rigid diatomic: f = 5 (3 translational + 2 rotational). T = 300 K.
  2. \(U=\frac52nRT=2.5\times2\times8.31\times300=12465\) J ≈ 12.5 kJ.
  3. Translational part: \(\frac32nRT=7479\) J.
  4. Rotational part: \(\frac22nRT=nRT=4986\) J. The parts add up to the total.

Answer: U ≈ 12.5 kJ: about 7.48 kJ translational and 4.99 kJ rotational.

More solved problem patterns
Worked example · Concept

Count the degrees of freedom of rigid molecules of CO₂ (linear, O=C=O), H₂O (bent) and NH₃ (pyramidal) at ordinary temperatures.

Show solution
  1. Each molecule has 3 translational degrees of freedom.
  2. CO₂ is linear. Rotation about its own axis stores no energy, so it has 2 rotational: f = 5.
  3. H₂O is bent and NH₃ is pyramidal. They are non-linear, so they rotate about all three axes: f = 3 + 3 = 6.
  4. Check H₂O with \(f=3N-k\): N = 3 atoms and k = 3 fixed distances (two O–H bonds and the H–H distance), so f = 9 − 3 = 6.

Answer: CO₂: 5. H₂O: 6. NH₃: 6.

Worked example · Concept

An ideal gas has an internal energy of 3RT per mole at moderate temperatures. What kind of molecule could it be?

Show solution
  1. \(U=\frac f2RT=3RT\) per mole, so f = 6.
  2. f = 6 = 3 translational + 3 rotational with no vibration.
  3. Three rotational degrees of freedom need a non-linear molecule.

Answer: A rigid non-linear polyatomic molecule, such as CH₄ or NH₃.

The law of equipartition of energy

A molecule can store energy in several independent ways. Each way that adds a squared term to the energy is called a degree of freedom. Examples: \(\frac12mv_x^2\) for motion along x, \(\frac12I\omega^2\) for rotation about one axis, and \(\frac12kx^2\) for the stretch of a vibrating bond.

The law of equipartition says that in thermal equilibrium at temperature T, each such squared term has the same average energy \(\frac12k_BT\) per molecule.

  • Translation: three terms (x, y, z), so \(\frac32k_BT\). This matches the kinetic theory result for every gas.
  • If a molecule has f degrees of freedom, its average energy is \(\frac f2k_BT\). One mole has \(\frac f2RT\), and n moles have \(U=\frac f2nRT\).
  • Ideal-gas molecules do not attract each other, so there is no intermolecular potential energy. U depends only on T, not on P or V.

Counting degrees of freedom

A point atom moves in three directions, so N free atoms have 3N coordinates. Every rigid link between atoms removes one. A useful rule is \(f=3N-k\), where k is the number of independent constraints.

MoleculeTranslationalRotationalVibrationalf
Monatomic (He, Ne, Ar)3003
Diatomic, rigid (O₂, N₂, H₂ near room T)3205
Diatomic, vibrating (high T)322 (one mode)7
Linear triatomic, rigid (CO₂)3205
Non-linear, rigid (H₂O, NH₃, CH₄)3306

A diatomic molecule has only two rotational degrees of freedom. Its moment of inertia about the bond axis is almost zero, so rotation about that axis stores no energy. The same is true for any linear molecule. A non-linear molecule can rotate about all three axes.

One vibrational mode counts twice: once for the kinetic energy of the vibrating atoms and once for the potential energy of the stretched bond.

Where equipartition breaks down

Equipartition is a classical result. Quantum mechanics allows rotational and vibrational energy only in steps. When \(k_BT\) is much smaller than the step size, that mode cannot be excited and contributes nothing: it is “frozen”. Vibrational steps are large, so diatomic vibrations are frozen at room temperature, and f = 5 is the right value there. The next section, on specific heats, shows how this makes heat capacities depend on temperature.

Question patterns
  • “The degrees of freedom of a rigid diatomic (or triatomic, linear or non-linear) molecule are …”
  • “Find the total (or rotational) kinetic energy of n moles of O₂ at temperature T.”
  • “What fraction of the energy of a diatomic gas is rotational?” Answer: 2/5.
  • Match the gas with its f value, often mixed with C_V or γ.
  • Assertion–reason comparing internal energies of monatomic and diatomic gases at the same temperature.
Another common mistake

Translational KE per mole is (3/2)RT for every gas. Total internal energy (f/2)RT depends on the molecule. Read whether a question asks for translational KE, rotational KE or total energy.

Another common mistake

Specific heats of gases and Mayer’s relation

A gas has no single heat capacity. The heat needed to warm it by 1 K depends on what happens to its volume. At constant volume, all the heat goes into internal energy. At constant pressure, the gas also expands and pushes on its surroundings, so more heat is needed for the same rise in temperature.

At constant volume, supplied heat increases internal energy. At constant pressure, some heat also supplies expansion work, so Cₚ is larger. Check whether the question uses molar heat capacity or heat capacity per kilogram.

Specific heats of gases and Mayer’s relation
\[ C_P-C_V=R,\qquad c_p-c_v=\frac{R}{M},\qquad \gamma=\frac{C_P}{C_V}=1+\frac{2}{f} \]
Symbols and assumptions: C_P, C_V molar heat capacities at constant pressure and constant volume (J/mol K); c_p, c_v specific heats (J/kg K); R = 8.31 J/mol K; M molar mass (kg/mol); γ ratio of heat capacities (no unit); f active degrees of freedom. Ideal gas.
Other useful formulas
C_V and C_P from γ
\[ C_V=\frac{R}{\gamma-1},\qquad C_P=\frac{\gamma R}{\gamma-1} \]
Symbols and assumptions: C_V, C_P molar heat capacities (J/mol K); R = 8.31 J/mol K; γ = C_P/C_V (no unit). Ideal gas; follows from Mayer’s relation.
Sharing of heat at constant pressure
\[ \frac{\Delta U}{Q}=\frac1\gamma,\qquad \frac{W}{Q}=1-\frac1\gamma=\frac{2}{f+2} \]
Symbols and assumptions: Q heat supplied at constant pressure (J); ΔU rise in internal energy (J); W work done by the gas (J); γ = C_P/C_V; f degrees of freedom. Ideal gas, isobaric process.
Dulong–Petit law for solids
\[ C\approx3R\approx25\ \text{J mol}^{-1}\text{K}^{-1} \]
Symbols and assumptions: C molar heat capacity of a solid element (J/mol K); R = 8.31 J/mol K. Each atom is a 3D oscillator with 6 squared energy terms. Valid at ordinary and high temperatures; fails at low temperatures.
Watch the assumption

Mayer’s relation is per mole. Per kilogram the difference is R/M, which is different for every gas.

Worked example

For an ideal gas γ = 1.4. Find C_V and C_P, taking R = 8.31 J/mol K.

Show solution

Why a gas has many heat capacities

Heat capacity is \(C=\dfrac{Q}{n\Delta T}\), and Q depends on the process. Two processes are standard:

  • Constant volume: no work is done, so \(Q=\Delta U=nC_V\Delta T\). For an ideal gas, \(C_V=\dfrac f2R\).
  • Constant pressure: the gas expands and does work, so \(C_P>C_V\).

Other processes give other values. In an isothermal process heat flows in but ΔT = 0, so C is infinite. In an adiabatic process Q = 0 while T changes, so C = 0. A process can even have a negative heat capacity. Per-mole and per-kilogram values are related by \(C=Mc\).

Derivation of Mayer’s relation

  1. Heat n moles of ideal gas at constant volume through ΔT: \(Q_V=nC_V\Delta T=\Delta U\).
  2. Heat the same gas through the same ΔT at constant pressure: \(Q_P=nC_P\Delta T=\Delta U+P\Delta V\).
  3. ΔU depends only on ΔT for an ideal gas, so it is the same in both cases: \(\Delta U=nC_V\Delta T\).
  4. At constant P, \(P\Delta V=nR\Delta T\) from PV = nRT.
  5. So \(nC_P\Delta T=nC_V\Delta T+nR\Delta T\), giving \(C_P-C_V=R\).

The same steps show how heat supplied at constant pressure is shared: the fraction going into internal energy is \(\dfrac{\Delta U}{Q}=\dfrac{C_V}{C_P}=\dfrac1\gamma\), and the fraction turned into work is \(\dfrac{W}{Q}=1-\dfrac1\gamma=\dfrac{2}{f+2}\). For a diatomic gas these are 5/7 and 2/7.

Standard values

Gas typefC_VC_Pγ
Monatomic (He, Ar)33R/25R/25/3 ≈ 1.67
Diatomic, rigid (O₂, N₂ at room T); linear CO₂, rigid55R/27R/27/5 = 1.40
Non-linear polyatomic, rigid (CH₄, NH₃)63R4R4/3 ≈ 1.33
Diatomic with vibration (high T)77R/29R/29/7 ≈ 1.29

Since \(\gamma=1+2/f\), more degrees of freedom mean a smaller γ. γ always lies between 1 and 5/3 for an ideal gas. Useful inverses: \(C_V=\dfrac{R}{\gamma-1}\) and \(C_P=\dfrac{\gamma R}{\gamma-1}\).

Mixtures in one line. C_V values add with mole weights, so \(\dfrac{n_1+n_2}{\gamma_{mix}-1}=\dfrac{n_1}{\gamma_1-1}+\dfrac{n_2}{\gamma_2-1}\). The mixtures section derives this; never average γ directly.

Temperature dependence and solids

Heat capacities are not fixed numbers. For hydrogen gas, C_V is about 3R/2 at very low temperature, because rotations are frozen. It rises to 5R/2 near room temperature when rotations switch on, and towards 7R/2 at a few thousand kelvin when vibration switches on. Classical equipartition cannot explain these steps; quantum theory does.

Solids (Dulong–Petit law). Each atom in a solid vibrates about a fixed point in three directions. Each direction has a kinetic and a potential energy term, so there are 6 squared terms and an energy of \(3k_BT\) per atom. One mole has \(U=3RT\), so \(C=3R\approx25\) J/mol K. For a solid, C_P ≈ C_V because its expansion work is tiny. This value holds at ordinary and high temperatures; at low temperatures the heat capacity falls towards zero.

  • “For a gas γ = … Find C_V, C_P, or the degrees of freedom.”

  • Given c_p and c_v in J/kg K, find the molar mass and identify the gas.
  • γ of a mixture of a monatomic and a diatomic gas.
  • Statement questions on how C_V changes with temperature, or the Dulong–Petit value 3R for solids.
Another common mistake

Another common mistake

Gas mixtures and partial pressure

For an ideal nonreacting mixture, each species contributes a partial pressure according to its molecule count at the common temperature and volume. The total pressure is the sum of these partial pressures.

The common temperature means equal average translational energy per molecule, not equal rms speeds. If a chemical process changes molecule count, recompute total amount before comparing pressures.

Gas mixtures and partial pressure
\[ p_{\rm total}=\sum p_i,\qquad p_i=\frac{n_iRT}{V},\qquad \frac{p_i}{p_{\rm total}}=\frac{n_i}{\sum n_j} \]
Symbols and assumptions: p_i partial pressure (Pa); n_i species amount (mol); R gas constant; T common temperature (K); V shared volume (m³); i/j species indices. Dalton’s law assumes an ideal nonreacting mixture at equilibrium.
Other useful formulas
Average molar mass of a mixture
\[ M_{mix}=\frac{n_1M_1+n_2M_2}{n_1+n_2}=\frac{m_1+m_2}{\dfrac{m_1}{M_1}+\dfrac{m_2}{M_2}} \]
Symbols and assumptions: M_mix effective molar mass (kg/mol or g/mol, consistently); n₁, n₂ moles of each gas; M₁, M₂ molar masses; m₁, m₂ masses of each gas. Ideal non-reacting mixture.
Temperature after insulated mixing
\[ T=\frac{f_1n_1T_1+f_2n_2T_2}{f_1n_1+f_2n_2} \]
Symbols and assumptions: T final common temperature (K); f₁, f₂ degrees of freedom; n₁, n₂ moles; T₁, T₂ initial temperatures (K). No heat exchange with the surroundings and no external work; ideal gases.
γ of a mixture
\[ \frac{n_1+n_2}{\gamma_{mix}-1}=\frac{n_1}{\gamma_1-1}+\frac{n_2}{\gamma_2-1} \]
Symbols and assumptions: γ_mix, γ₁, γ₂ ratios C_P/C_V (no unit); n₁, n₂ moles of each gas. Follows from adding the C_V values; ideal non-reacting gases.
Watch the assumption

Mole fraction, not mass fraction, directly sets ideal partial-pressure fraction.

Worked example

Show solution

Dalton’s law from kinetic theory

In an ideal gas the molecules do not exert forces on each other between collisions. So each gas in a mixture hits the walls exactly as it would if it were alone in the container. Its pressure is \(P_i=\frac13\rho_iv_{rms,i}^2=n_iRT/V\), and the total pressure is the sum of these partial pressures.

All the gases share one temperature, so they share the same average translational kinetic energy per molecule, \(\frac32k_BT\). Their rms speeds still differ: the lighter gas moves faster.

The partial pressure fraction equals the mole fraction. If masses are given, divide each by its molar mass first.

Molar mass and density of a mixture

A mixture behaves like a single ideal gas with an average molar mass, total mass divided by total moles:

\[M_{mix}=\frac{n_1M_1+n_2M_2}{n_1+n_2}\]

Example: dry air is roughly 79% N₂ and 21% O₂ by moles, so \(M_{mix}\approx0.79\times28+0.21\times32\approx28.8\) g/mol. The mixture density is then \(\rho=PM_{mix}/(RT)\).

Mixing gases from two containers

Two containers are joined by a thin tube and no gas escapes. Moles are conserved: \(\dfrac{PV_{total}}{RT}=\dfrac{P_1V_1}{RT_1}+\dfrac{P_2V_2}{RT_2}\).

  • If both start at the same temperature and it stays fixed, the final pressure is \(P=\dfrac{P_1V_1+P_2V_2}{V_1+V_2}\).
  • If the system is insulated and does no work, the total internal energy is conserved: \(\frac{f_1}{2}n_1RT_1+\frac{f_2}{2}n_2RT_2=\left(\frac{f_1}{2}n_1+\frac{f_2}{2}n_2\right)RT\). So \(T=\dfrac{f_1n_1T_1+f_2n_2T_2}{f_1n_1+f_2n_2}\). For the same kind of gas this becomes \(T=\dfrac{n_1T_1+n_2T_2}{n_1+n_2}\).

Energy and heat capacity of a mixture

Internal energies add, so the mixture has \(U=\frac{f_1}{2}n_1RT+\frac{f_2}{2}n_2RT\). Writing this as \(\frac{f_{mix}}{2}(n_1+n_2)RT\) gives

\[f_{mix}=\frac{n_1f_1+n_2f_2}{n_1+n_2},\qquad C_{V,mix}=\frac{n_1C_{V1}+n_2C_{V2}}{n_1+n_2},\qquad C_{P,mix}=C_{V,mix}+R\]

Since \(C_V=R/(\gamma-1)\), the same weighting gives \(\dfrac{n_1+n_2}{\gamma_{mix}-1}=\dfrac{n_1}{\gamma_1-1}+\dfrac{n_2}{\gamma_2-1}\). Never average the γ values directly. The specific heats section has a worked example.

Mean free path and collisions

Molecules travel between collisions along approximately straight segments. Mean free path is the average distance between collisions, not the average distance between neighbouring molecules. Higher number density makes collisions more frequent.

For the hard-sphere model the collision cross section depends on diameter squared. The √2 factor accounts for relative molecular motion. Combining number density with the ideal gas law shows that at fixed pressure the path grows with temperature.

Mean free path and collisions
\[ \ell_{mfp}=\frac1{\sqrt2\pi d^2 n_N}=\frac{k_BT}{\sqrt2\pi d^2p} \]
Symbols and assumptions: ℓ_mfp mean free path (m); d effective molecular diameter (m); n_N=N/V number density (m⁻³), not moles; k_B constant; T temperature (K); p pressure (Pa). Dilute identical hard spheres and an equilibrium ideal gas are assumed.
Other useful formulas
Collision frequency and mean free time
\[ \nu=\frac{\bar v}{\lambda}=\sqrt2\,\pi d^2n_N\bar v,\qquad \tau=\frac1\nu=\frac{\lambda}{\bar v} \]
Symbols and assumptions: ν collisions per second for one molecule (s⁻¹); v̄ mean speed (m/s); λ mean free path (m); d molecular diameter (m); n_N number density (m⁻³); τ mean time between collisions (s). Hard-sphere ideal gas.
Watch the assumption

At fixed volume and particle count, raising temperature does not change this hard-sphere mean free path, although collision frequency changes.

Worked example

At fixed temperature and molecular diameter, pressure triples. What happens to mean free path?

Show solution

ℓ_mfp is inversely proportional to p, so it becomes one third. Number density has tripled.

Worked example · Numerical

Derivation: the collision cylinder

Perfume molecules move at hundreds of metres per second, yet the smell takes many seconds to cross a room. The reason is collisions: each molecule zig-zags, changing direction billions of times every second.

  1. Treat molecules as hard spheres of diameter d. Two molecules collide if their centres come within a distance d of each other.
  2. Let one molecule move with average speed \(\bar v\) and imagine the others at rest. In time t it sweeps out a cylinder of radius d and length \(\bar vt\). Its volume is \(\pi d^2\bar vt\).
  3. Every molecule whose centre lies inside this cylinder is hit. With \(n_N\) molecules per unit volume, the number of collisions is \(n_N\pi d^2\bar vt\).
  4. Mean free path = distance travelled ÷ number of collisions = \(\dfrac{\bar vt}{n_N\pi d^2\bar vt}=\dfrac{1}{\pi d^2n_N}\).
  5. The other molecules are moving too. The average relative speed is \(\sqrt2\,\bar v\), so there are \(\sqrt2\) times more collisions. This gives \(\lambda=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt2\pi d^2n_N}\).

The quantity \(\pi d^2\) is the collision cross-section. It uses the diameter, not the radius. If a question gives radius r, use \(\pi d^2=4\pi r^2\).

How λ and collision frequency depend on T and P

Using \(n_N=P/(k_BT)\), \(\lambda=\dfrac{k_BT}{\sqrt2\pi d^2P}\). The collision frequency (collisions per second for one molecule) is \(\nu=\bar v/\lambda=\sqrt2\pi d^2n_N\bar v\), and the mean time between collisions is \(\tau=1/\nu\).

For air at room conditions, λ is about 10⁻⁷ m. This is roughly 300 molecular diameters and about 30 times the average spacing between molecules. A molecule collides about 5×10⁹ times per second.

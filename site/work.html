Work, Energy & Power

Use work to track energy changes, recognise conservative forces and analyse collisions. Every energy equation starts with a clearly chosen system.

1 Core

Work by constant and variable forces

Work measures energy transfer by a force through displacement of its point of application. Only the force component along the displacement contributes. A force perpendicular to motion can change direction without changing kinetic energy.

For a varying one-dimensional force, accumulate small force-times-displacement contributions. The signed area under an F–x graph gives work, including negative contributions where the force opposes displacement.

Work by constant and variable forces
\[ W=\vec F\cdot\vec s=Fs\cos\theta,\qquad W=\int_{x_i}^{x_f}F_x\,dx \]
Symbols and assumptions: W is work (J); F is constant force magnitude (N); s displacement magnitude (m); θ force–displacement angle; Fₓ is the signed force component along x; xᵢ/x_f are integration limits (m). The first formula requires constant force.
Watch the assumption

A large force does zero work if its application point does not move.

Worked example

A 10 N force acts at 60° to a 4 m displacement. Find work.

Show solution

W=10×4×cos60°=20 J. The perpendicular component does no work through that displacement.

Check the idea

2 Core

Kinetic energy and the work–energy theorem

Kinetic energy measures the energy associated with motion in the chosen frame. The work–energy theorem follows by integrating net force along the motion. Net work changes kinetic energy, even when individual forces do different amounts of work.

The theorem can avoid finding time or acceleration when speed and displacement are given. Include every force doing work. If the net work is negative, kinetic energy decreases but the final speed must still be found from its square.

Kinetic energy and the work–energy theorem
\[ K=\tfrac12mv^2,\qquad W_{\rm net}=K_f-K_i \]
Symbols and assumptions: K is translational kinetic energy (J); m mass (kg); v speed (m/s); W_net is the sum of works by all forces (J); i/f mean initial/final. The particle form does not include a separate rotational energy.
Watch the assumption

Kinetic energy is frame dependent and nonnegative; momentum has direction.

Worked example

A 2 kg particle speeds up from 3 to 5 m/s. Find net work.

Show solution

ΔK=½×2×(25−9)=16 J. The change is based on the difference of squares, not the square of the speed difference.

Check the idea

3 Core

Conservative forces and potential energy

A conservative force does work depending only on the endpoints. It allows a potential-energy function whose decrease equals the work done by that force. Near Earth, raising a mass increases gravitational potential energy; stretching a spring stores elastic energy.

The zero of potential energy is a chosen reference. Only differences affect the dynamics. In one dimension a negative slope of U(x) means a positive force, so the body tends to move toward lower potential.

Conservative forces and potential energy
\[ W_c=-\Delta U,\quad F_x=-\frac{dU}{dx},\quad U_g=mgh,\quad U_s=\tfrac12kx^2 \]
Symbols and assumptions: U is potential energy (J); W_c conservative work (J); Fₓ force (N); m mass (kg); g constant gravity; h height above a reference (m); k spring constant (N/m); x spring displacement (m). Spring law assumes Hookean behaviour.
Watch the assumption

Potential energy can be negative depending on the reference; that is not a negative kinetic energy.

Worked example

A 200 N/m spring is stretched by 0.10 m. Find stored energy.

Show solution

U=½×200×0.10²=1 J. Doubling this extension would store 4 J.

Check the idea

4 Core

Mechanical energy and dissipative work

With only conservative forces doing work, kinetic plus potential energy remains constant. Gravity can convert potential energy to kinetic energy without changing their sum. Friction reduces mechanical energy while increasing internal energy of the wider system.

Choose the system and identify external work before writing an energy balance. A change of mechanical energy equals nonconservative work when conservative interactions are represented by U. Total energy includes the internal energy produced by dissipation.

Mechanical energy and dissipative work
\[ K_i+U_i+W_{nc}=K_f+U_f \]
Symbols and assumptions: K and U are kinetic and potential energies (J); W_nc is work of forces not represented by U (J); i/f are initial/final. Use a consistent reference for potential and include any rotational kinetic energy required by the model.
Watch the assumption

Mechanical-energy loss does not mean total energy disappears.

Worked example

A 2 kg body starts from rest 5 m above ground. Friction dissipates 20 J before arrival. Use g=10. Find final speed.

Show solution

Initial potential is 100 J. Final kinetic is 100−20=80 J, so v=√(2×80/2)=√80≈8.94 m/s.

Check the idea

5 Core

Power and efficiency

Power is the rate of energy transfer. Average power divides work by the time taken; instantaneous power uses the force and velocity at that instant. A machine can exert a high force at low speed while delivering modest power.

Efficiency compares useful output with energy or power supplied. In a constant-power acceleration, force is not constant: with a force parallel to motion, F=P/v decreases as speed rises.

Power and efficiency
\[ P_{\rm av}=\frac{W}{\Delta t},\quad P=\vec F\cdot\vec v,\quad \eta=\frac{P_{\rm useful}}{P_{\rm input}} \]
Symbols and assumptions: P is power (W = J/s); W in the numerator is work (J), not the watt symbol; Δt time (s); F force (N); v velocity (m/s); η is dimensionless efficiency, often expressed as a percentage.
Watch the assumption

Power is a rate, not the amount of energy transferred.

Worked example

A motor raises 20 kg by 3 m in 2 s. Use g=10 and efficiency 75%. Find useful and input powers.

Show solution

Useful work is 600 J, giving 300 W. Input power is 300/0.75=400 W.

Check the idea

6 Core

Elastic and inelastic collisions

During a short collision with negligible external impulse, total momentum is conserved. An elastic collision also conserves total kinetic energy. In a completely inelastic collision, the objects stick together; their final common velocity follows from momentum.

The coefficient of restitution describes the relative separation speed compared with approach speed along the collision axis. A zero value means no relative separation immediately after impact, but does not by itself describe every later interaction.

Elastic and inelastic collisions
\[ m_1u_1+m_2u_2=m_1v_1+m_2v_2,\quad e=\frac{v_2-v_1}{u_1-u_2},\quad V_{\rm stick}=\frac{m_1u_1+m_2u_2}{m_1+m_2} \]
Symbols and assumptions: m₁/m₂ are masses (kg); u₁/u₂ initial and v₁/v₂ final signed velocities (m/s); e is dimensionless restitution for a one-dimensional approach with u₁>u₂; V_stick is common velocity. Momentum requires negligible external impulse.
Watch the assumption

Conserved momentum does not imply conserved kinetic energy.

Worked example

A 2 kg cart at 6 m/s sticks to a stationary 1 kg cart. Find final speed and kinetic-energy loss.

Show solution

Common speed is (2×6)/3=4 m/s. Initial K=36 J and final K=½×3×16=24 J, so 12 J becomes internal energy.

Check the idea

7 Core

Vertical circles and minimum launch energy

A body on a light string in a vertical circle must have enough speed at the top to keep the string taut. At the limiting case, gravity alone supplies the inward acceleration and tension just becomes zero.

Between bottom and top the body rises by 2r, so kinetic energy decreases by 2mgr. Combining this energy change with the top contact condition gives the minimum bottom speed. A rigid rod can push as well as pull and has a different constraint.

Vertical circles and minimum launch energy
\[ v_{\rm top,min}=\sqrt{gr},\qquad v_{\rm bottom,min}=\sqrt{5gr} \]
Symbols and assumptions: v is speed (m/s); g gravity (m/s²); r circle radius (m). These limits apply to a point mass on a taut light string making a complete vertical circle without dissipative losses; the bottom result assumes energy conservation.
Watch the assumption

Do not use the light-string minimum for a rigid rod or a track that can maintain contact differently.

Worked example

A string circle has radius 0.8 m and g=10. Find the minimum bottom speed.

Show solution

v=√(5×10×0.8)=√40≈6.32 m/s. At the top, minimum speed is √8≈2.83 m/s.

Check the idea

8 Core

One-dimensional elastic collision results

Elastic head-on collisions conserve both momentum and kinetic energy. Solving the two equations gives final velocities or, equivalently, conservation of momentum plus reversal of relative velocity.

The unequal-mass formulas reveal useful limits: a light projectile reflecting from a very heavy stationary target nearly reverses its velocity; equal masses exchange velocities. Keep signed velocities when both objects initially move.

One-dimensional elastic collision results
\[ v_1=\frac{(m_1-m_2)u_1+2m_2u_2}{m_1+m_2},\quad v_2=\frac{2m_1u_1+(m_2-m_1)u_2}{m_1+m_2} \]
Symbols and assumptions: m₁/m₂ masses (kg); u₁/u₂ initial and v₁/v₂ final signed velocities (m/s). Assumptions: one-dimensional perfectly elastic collision and negligible external impulse; denominator is positive total mass.
Watch the assumption

These formulas are not valid for sticking or energy-losing collisions.

Worked example

A 1 kg cart at 6 m/s hits a stationary 2 kg cart elastically. Find both velocities.

Show solution

v₁=(1−2)6/3=−2 m/s and v₂=2×1×6/3=4 m/s. Momentum is −2+8=6 kg m/s; energy is 2+16=18 J, matching the initial values.

Check the idea

1 Basics

Work by constant and variable forces

Work measures energy transfer by a force through displacement of its point of application. Only the force component along the displacement contributes. A force perpendicular to motion can change direction without changing kinetic energy.

For a varying one-dimensional force, accumulate small force-times-displacement contributions. The signed area under an F–x graph gives work, including negative contributions where the force opposes displacement.

Work by constant and variable forces
\[ W=\vec F\cdot\vec s=Fs\cos\theta,\qquad W=\int_{x_i}^{x_f}F_x\,dx \]
Symbols and assumptions: W is work (J); F is constant force magnitude (N); s displacement magnitude (m); θ force–displacement angle; Fₓ is the signed force component along x; xᵢ/x_f are integration limits (m). The first formula requires constant force.
Other useful formulas
Work by a constant force in components
\[ W=\vec F\cdot\Delta\vec r=F_x\Delta x+F_y\Delta y+F_z\Delta z \]
Symbols and assumptions: Fₓ, F_y, F_z components of the constant force (N); Δx, Δy, Δz components of the displacement (m); W work (J).
Energy unit conversions
\[ 1\ \text{J}=10^7\ \text{erg},\quad 1\ \text{kWh}=3.6\times10^6\ \text{J},\quad 1\ \text{eV}=1.6\times10^{-19}\ \text{J} \]
Symbols and assumptions: erg is the CGS unit (dyne cm); kWh is the commercial "unit" of electrical energy; eV is the energy gained by an electron through 1 volt.
Watch the assumption

A large force does zero work if its application point does not move.

Worked example

A 10 N force acts at 60° to a 4 m displacement. Find work.

Show solution

W=10×4×cos60°=20 J. The perpendicular component does no work through that displacement.

Worked example · Numerical

A constant force F = (3î + 4ĵ − 2k̂) N moves a particle from (1, 0, 2) m to (3, 2, 1) m. Find the work done.

Show solution
  1. Displacement Δr = (3 − 1)î + (2 − 0)ĵ + (1 − 2)k̂ = 2î + 2ĵ − k̂ m.
  2. W = F·Δr = 3 × 2 + 4 × 2 + (−2)(−1).
  3. W = 6 + 8 + 2 = 16 J.

Answer: 16 J

Worked example · Graph

Use the F–x graph in the figure: F rises from 0 to 10 N over the first 2 m, stays at 10 N up to 4 m, then falls linearly to −10 N at 6 m. Find the work done from x = 0 to x = 6 m.

Show solution
  1. 0 to 2 m: triangle ½ × 2 × 10 = 10 J.
  2. 2 to 4 m: rectangle 2 × 10 = 20 J.
  3. The line from (4, 10) to (6, −10) crosses zero at x = 5 m. From 4 to 5 m: triangle ½ × 1 × 10 = +5 J.
  4. From 5 to 6 m: triangle below the axis, −5 J.
  5. Net work = 10 + 20 + 5 − 5 = 30 J.

Worked example · Numerical

A force F = (3x² − 2x) N acts along the x-axis. Find the work it does as the particle moves from x = 1 m to x = 3 m.

Show solution
  1. W = ∫ from 1 to 3 of (3x² − 2x) dx = [x³ − x²] from 1 to 3.
  2. At 3: 27 − 9 = 18. At 1: 1 − 1 = 0.
  3. W = 18 J.

Answer: 18 J

Sign of work and the zero-work cases

W = Fs cosθ, where θ is the angle between the force and the displacement.

  • Positive (0 ≤ θ < 90°): the force helps the motion. Gravity on a falling ball, the pull on a dragged box.
  • Zero (θ = 90°, or no displacement): centripetal force in uniform circular motion, tension in a swinging pendulum string, the normal force on a block sliding on a fixed surface, a porter holding a load still or carrying it horizontally at steady speed (his upward force is perpendicular to the motion).
  • Negative (90° < θ ≤ 180°): the force opposes the motion. Friction on a block sliding over a fixed floor, gravity on a ball going up, brakes.

Work is a scalar, measured in joules: 1 J = 1 N m = 10⁷ erg. Other units: 1 kWh = 3.6 × 10⁶ J and 1 eV = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ J.

Variable forces and the F–x graph

When F changes with position, split the path into tiny steps dx. On each step the force is nearly constant, so dW = F dx. Adding them gives W = ∫F dx, which is the area under the F–x graph.

  • Area above the x-axis counts as positive work; area below counts as negative.
  • Break the graph into triangles, rectangles and trapeziums and add the signed areas.
  • In vector form, \(W=\int(F_x\,dx+F_y\,dy+F_z\,dz)\). For a constant force this is simply \(F_x\Delta x+F_y\Delta y+F_z\Delta z\).

Work done by gravity depends only on the vertical drop: \(W_g=mgh\) for a fall through h, along any path.

Question patterns
  • "F = (… î + … ĵ) N moves a body from point A to point B. Find the work." Use the dot product with the displacement.
  • F–x graph with parts above and below the axis: find the net work or the speed at the end.
  • F = f(x) given as a polynomial: integrate between limits.
  • "Work done by the centripetal force / tension / normal force is …" (zero).
Another common mistake

When a graph dips below the x-axis, subtract that area. Adding all areas as positive overestimates the work.

Another common mistake

Work done by a person holding a heavy bag still is zero in physics, even though the person gets tired. Muscles use energy internally, but the bag does not move.

2 Core

Kinetic energy and the work–energy theorem

Kinetic energy measures the energy associated with motion in the chosen frame. The work–energy theorem follows by integrating net force along the motion. Net work changes kinetic energy, even when individual forces do different amounts of work.

The theorem can avoid finding time or acceleration when speed and displacement are given. Include every force doing work. If the net work is negative, kinetic energy decreases but the final speed must still be found from its square.

Kinetic energy and the work–energy theorem
\[ K=\tfrac12mv^2,\qquad W_{\rm net}=K_f-K_i \]
Symbols and assumptions: K is translational kinetic energy (J); m mass (kg); v speed (m/s); W_net is the sum of works by all forces (J); i/f mean initial/final. The particle form does not include a separate rotational energy.
Other useful formulas
Kinetic energy and momentum
\[ K=\frac{p^2}{2m},\qquad p=\sqrt{2mK} \]
Symbols and assumptions: K kinetic energy (J); p momentum magnitude (kg m/s); m mass (kg).
Work–energy theorem for a variable force
\[ \int_{x_i}^{x_f}F_{\rm net}\,dx=\tfrac12mv^2-\tfrac12mu^2 \]
Symbols and assumptions: F_net net force along the path (N); u, v initial and final speeds (m/s); m mass (kg). Valid in an inertial frame for any forces.
Watch the assumption

Kinetic energy is frame dependent and nonnegative; momentum has direction.

Worked example

A 2 kg particle speeds up from 3 to 5 m/s. Find net work.

Show solution

ΔK=½×2×(25−9)=16 J. The change is based on the difference of squares, not the square of the speed difference.

Worked example · Numerical

A bullet loses half its speed after penetrating 3 cm into a wooden block. Assuming constant resistance, how much farther will it penetrate?

Show solution
  1. Half the speed means K becomes ¼ of the original. So ¾K is lost in 3 cm.
  2. Constant resistance F: F × 3 cm = ¾K, so F = K/(4 cm).
  3. The remaining ¼K needs a distance (¼K)/F = 1 cm.

Answer: 1 cm more (4 cm in total)

More solved problem patterns
Worked example · Ratio

The kinetic energy of a body increases by 300%. By what percentage does its momentum increase?

Show solution
  1. An increase of 300% means K becomes K + 3K = 4K.
  2. p = √(2mK), so p becomes √4 = 2 times its value.
  3. An increase from p to 2p is a 100% increase.

Answer: 100%

Worked example · Numerical

A 2 kg stone falls from rest through 5 m into sand and stops after sinking 5 cm. Find the average resistive force of the sand.

Show solution
  1. Initial and final kinetic energies are both zero, so W_net = 0.
  2. Gravity acts through the whole 5.05 m: W_g = 20 × 5.05 = 101 J.
  3. Sand acts through 0.05 m: W_sand = −F × 0.05.
  4. 0 = 101 − 0.05F, so F = 2020 N (about 100 times the weight).

Answer: About 2020 N

Derivation from Newton’s second law

  1. For a particle moving along x under a net force F: \(F=ma=m\dfrac{dv}{dt}\).
  2. Use the chain rule: \(\dfrac{dv}{dt}=\dfrac{dv}{dx}\dfrac{dx}{dt}=v\dfrac{dv}{dx}\). So \(F=mv\dfrac{dv}{dx}\).
  3. Multiply by dx and integrate: \(\displaystyle\int_{x_i}^{x_f}F\,dx=\int_{u}^{v}mv\,dv=\tfrac12mv^2-\tfrac12mu^2\).
  4. The left side is the net work. So W_net = ΔK.

Nothing in the derivation assumed F was constant or conservative. The theorem holds for any forces, as long as you include all of them and work in an inertial frame.

Kinetic energy and momentum

Since p = mv, \(K=\dfrac{p^2}{2m}\) and \(p=\sqrt{2mK}\). This link drives most ratio questions:

  • Same momentum: K ∝ 1/m. The lighter body has more kinetic energy.
  • Same kinetic energy: p ∝ √m. The heavier body has more momentum.
  • Percentage changes: if p rises by 20%, K rises by a factor 1.2² = 1.44, i.e. 44%. If K rises by 300% (becomes 4K), p doubles (rises by 100%).
  • For small changes only: ΔK/K ≈ 2Δp/p (so a 1% rise in p gives about 2% rise in K).

Stopping problems: bullets and planks

A resistive force that is constant removes kinetic energy in proportion to distance. If a bullet loses half its speed after penetrating a distance d, it has lost ¾ of its kinetic energy. The remaining ¼ needs only d/3 more. In general, the number of identical planks a bullet can cross is proportional to its initial kinetic energy.

Question patterns
  • "If the momentum of a body is increased by n%, its kinetic energy increases by …"
  • Two bodies with equal kinetic energy (or equal momentum): ratio of momenta (or of kinetic energies).
  • Bullet into a block or planks: further penetration after losing part of its speed.
  • A body dropped into sand: average resistive force from h and the penetration depth.
  • Graphs: K against v (parabola), K against p² (straight line), √K against v (straight line).
Another common mistake

A 20% increase in speed does not mean a 20% increase in kinetic energy. K ∝ v², so it rises by 44%.

Another common mistake

The work–energy theorem is not limited to conservative forces. Friction, air drag and muscle forces all enter W_net.

Check the idea

3 Core

Conservative forces and potential energy

A conservative force does work depending only on the endpoints. It allows a potential-energy function whose decrease equals the work done by that force. Near Earth, raising a mass increases gravitational potential energy; stretching a spring stores elastic energy.

The zero of potential energy is a chosen reference. Only differences affect the dynamics. In one dimension a negative slope of U(x) means a positive force, so the body tends to move toward lower potential.

Conservative forces and potential energy
\[ W_c=-\Delta U,\quad F_x=-\frac{dU}{dx},\quad U_g=mgh,\quad U_s=\tfrac12kx^2 \]
Symbols and assumptions: U is potential energy (J); W_c conservative work (J); Fₓ force (N); m mass (kg); g constant gravity; h height above a reference (m); k spring constant (N/m); x spring displacement (m). Spring law assumes Hookean behaviour.
Other useful formulas
Equilibrium from U(x)
\[ \frac{dU}{dx}=0;\quad \frac{d^2U}{dx^2}>0\ \text{stable},\quad <0\ \text{unstable},\quad =0\ \text{neutral} \]
Symbols and assumptions: U potential energy (J) as a function of position x (m). The second-derivative test classifies each equilibrium point.
Kinetic energy from the energy line
\[ K(x)=E-U(x)\ge 0 \]
Symbols and assumptions: E total mechanical energy (J), constant when only conservative forces act; U(x) potential energy (J); K kinetic energy (J). Turning points are where U(x) = E.
Watch the assumption

Potential energy can be negative depending on the reference; that is not a negative kinetic energy.

Worked example

A 200 N/m spring is stretched by 0.10 m. Find stored energy.

Show solution

U=½×200×0.10²=1 J. Doubling this extension would store 4 J.

Worked example · Numerical

U(x) = x³/3 − 4x (SI units). Find the equilibrium points and classify them.

Show solution
  1. dU/dx = x² − 4 = 0, so x = +2 m or x = −2 m.
  2. d²U/dx² = 2x.
  3. At x = +2: d²U/dx² = 4 > 0, a minimum, so stable.
  4. At x = −2: d²U/dx² = −4 < 0, a maximum, so unstable.

Answer: x = 2 m stable; x = −2 m unstable

More solved problem patterns
Worked example · Numerical

The potential energy between two atoms is U(r) = a/r¹² − b/r⁶, with a, b > 0. Find the equilibrium separation.

Show solution
  1. F = −dU/dr = 12a/r¹³ − 6b/r⁷.
  2. Equilibrium: 12a/r¹³ = 6b/r⁷, so r⁶ = 2a/b.
  3. r₀ = (2a/b)^(1/6). This is a minimum of U, so the bond is stable.

Answer: r₀ = (2a/b)^(1/6)

Worked example · Graph

In the figure, the particle has total energy E (dashed line). Where is it fastest, where does it turn back, and which way does the force push it just to the right of A?

Show solution
  1. K = E − U is largest where U is smallest, at A. So it is fastest at A.
  2. It turns back at the two points where the curve meets the energy line (E = U, K = 0).
  3. Just right of A the curve rises (positive slope), so F = −dU/dx is negative: the force points back toward A.

Answer: Fastest at A; turns at the two marked points; force points back toward A

Testing whether a force is conservative

A force is conservative if any one of these equivalent statements holds:

  • The work it does between two points does not depend on the path.
  • The work it does around any closed path is zero.
  • It can be written as \(F_x=-dU/dx\) for some potential-energy function U.

Gravity, the spring force and the electrostatic force are conservative. Friction, air drag and viscous forces are not: their work around a closed loop is always negative, because they oppose the motion everywhere.

Near Earth’s surface U = mgh is valid only for h much smaller than Earth’s radius; for large heights use the gravitation chapter’s −GMm/r.

Reading a potential-energy curve

Use F = −dU/dx: the force is minus the slope. A particle is pushed "downhill" on the U–x graph.

  • Equilibrium: F = 0, so dU/dx = 0 (flat points of the curve).
  • Stable (point A, a minimum, d²U/dx² > 0): a small displacement brings a restoring force back toward A. The particle oscillates.
  • Unstable (point B, a maximum, d²U/dx² < 0): a small displacement gives a force pushing it further away.
  • Neutral (region C, flat, d²U/dx² = 0): displaced, it stays put.

With total energy E, the kinetic energy at any x is K = E − U(x). Motion is possible only where E ≥ U. The points where E = U are turning points: the particle stops there and turns back. K (and speed) is largest where U is smallest.

Question patterns
  • "U(x) = … Find the equilibrium position(s) and state whether stable or unstable."
  • Molecular potential U = a/r¹² − b/r⁶: equilibrium separation r = (2a/b)^(1/6).
  • U–x graph with an energy line: find turning points, region of motion, where speed is maximum, or the force direction at a point.
  • Assertion–reason on conservative forces and closed-path work.
Another common mistake

Where U is zero, the force is not necessarily zero. Force depends on the slope of U, not its value.

Another common mistake

"Equilibrium" means zero net force, not zero speed. A particle can pass through a stable equilibrium point at its maximum speed.

Check the idea

Spring force, spring energy and spring combinations

An ideal spring pulls back with a force proportional to its extension or compression: F = −kx. Because the force changes with x, the work it does must be found from the area under the F–x graph, which is a triangle.

Measure extension from the spring’s natural length. Stored energy depends on extension squared; the work done by the spring is the negative change in that energy. For a maximum-compression question, the moving mass is instantaneously at rest.

Spring force, spring energy and spring combinations
\[ F=-kx,\qquad W_{\rm spring}=-\tfrac12k\left(x_2^2-x_1^2\right),\qquad U=\tfrac12kx^2=\frac{F^2}{2k} \]
Symbols and assumptions: k spring constant (N/m); x extension or compression from natural length (m); x₁, x₂ initial and final deformations (m); W_spring work done by the spring (J); U stored energy (J); F spring force magnitude (N). Ideal massless spring obeying Hooke’s law.
Other useful formulas
Combining and cutting springs
\[ \frac1{k_s}=\frac1{k_1}+\frac1{k_2},\qquad k_p=k_1+k_2,\qquad kL=\text{constant} \]
Symbols and assumptions: k₁, k₂ individual spring constants (N/m); k_s series and k_p parallel equivalents (N/m); L natural length (m). Ideal massless springs.
Block hitting a spring
\[ x_{\max}=v\sqrt{\frac mk}\ \ (\text{smooth}),\qquad \tfrac12mv^2=\tfrac12kx^2+\mu mgx\ \ (\text{rough}) \]
Symbols and assumptions: m block mass (kg); v speed when it touches the spring (m/s); k spring constant (N/m); x compression (m); μ kinetic friction coefficient; g = 10 m/s².
Watch the assumption

Work to stretch from x₁ to x₂ is ½k(x₂² − x₁²), not ½k(x₂ − x₁)². The spring is already resisting at x₁, so the second centimetre costs more than the first.

Worked example

A spring with k = 800 N/m is stretched from 5 cm to 15 cm. How much work is needed?

Show solution

W = ½ × 800 × (0.15² − 0.05²) = 400 × (0.0225 − 0.0025) = 400 × 0.02 = 8 J. Using ½k(0.10)² would give only 4 J.

Worked example · Numerical

A 2 kg block moving at 4 m/s on a rough floor (μₖ = 0.5) hits a spring with k = 700 N/m. Find the maximum compression.

Show solution
  1. Initial kinetic energy = ½ × 2 × 16 = 16 J. Friction = 0.5 × 20 = 10 N.
  2. Energy: 16 = ½ × 700 × x² + 10x, so 350x² + 10x − 16 = 0.
  3. x = [−10 + √(100 + 22 400)]/700 = (−10 + 150)/700 = 0.2 m.
  4. Check: spring energy 14 J + friction work 2 J = 16 J.

Answer: 0.2 m

Worked example · Ratio

A spring with k = 120 N/m is cut into three pieces whose lengths are in the ratio 1 : 2 : 3. Find their spring constants.

Show solution
  1. kL is constant. The pieces have lengths L/6, 2L/6 and 3L/6.
  2. k₁ = 120 × 6 = 720 N/m; k₂ = 120 × 3 = 360 N/m; k₃ = 120 × 2 = 240 N/m.
  3. Check: in series, 1/720 + 1/360 + 1/240 = 6/720 = 1/120. They rebuild the original spring.

Answer: 720, 360 and 240 N/m

Worked example · Ratio

Spring A has twice the spring constant of spring B. Find U_A : U_B when they are stretched (a) by the same force, (b) by the same extension.

Show solution
  1. (a) Same force: U = F²/2k ∝ 1/k. U_A : U_B = k_B : k_A = 1 : 2.
  2. (b) Same extension: U = ½kx² ∝ k. U_A : U_B = 2 : 1.

Answer: (a) 1 : 2 (b) 2 : 1

Derivation: spring work from the F–x graph

  1. To stretch the spring slowly, you apply F_ext = +kx (equal and opposite to the spring force).
  2. Work by you from 0 to x: \(\int_0^x kx\,dx=\tfrac12kx^2\). This is the triangle under the F–x line (base x, height kx).
  3. From x₁ to x₂: \(\tfrac12k(x_2^2-x_1^2)\), the area of a trapezium.
  4. The spring force is the opposite, so its work is \(-\tfrac12k(x_2^2-x_1^2)\). Moving back toward natural length, the spring does positive work.

Since U = ½kx² and F = kx, the stored energy can also be written U = F²/(2k) = ½Fx. These forms are handy when the force, not the extension, is given.

Block meets spring, with and without friction

  • Smooth floor: ½mv² = ½kx_max², so x_max = v√(m/k).
  • Rough floor: friction acts during compression: ½mv² = ½kx² + μmgx. Solve this quadratic for x.
  • Dropped from height h onto a vertical spring: the block falls a total h + x, so mg(h + x) = ½kx².
  • Two blocks joined by a spring on a smooth floor: at maximum compression they move with the same velocity. Use momentum for that velocity and the kinetic energy difference for ½kx².

Cutting and combining springs

  • Cutting: k is inversely proportional to the natural length (kL = constant). Cut into n equal parts, each has spring constant nk. Cut in the ratio of lengths L₁ : L₂, the parts have k₁ = k(L/L₁) and k₂ = k(L/L₂).
  • Series (end to end): same force in each, extensions add. \(\dfrac{1}{k_s}=\dfrac{1}{k_1}+\dfrac{1}{k_2}\).
  • Parallel (side by side, same extension): forces add. \(k_p=k_1+k_2\).
  • Energy sharing: in series (same F), U = F²/2k, so the softer spring stores more. In parallel (same x), U = ½kx², so the stiffer spring stores more.
Question patterns
  • "Work done to stretch a spring from x to 2x" (3 times the work from 0 to x).
  • Block on a smooth or rough floor hitting a spring: maximum compression.
  • Spring cut into pieces, or pieces combined in series and parallel: new k.
  • Ratio of energies in two springs stretched by the same force, or by the same extension.
  • F–x graph of a spring: find k from the slope and energy from the triangle.
Another common mistake

Cutting a spring in half doubles its spring constant; it does not halve it. Shorter springs are stiffer.

Another common mistake

For springs in series with the same force, the softer spring stores more energy. Students often assume the stiffer spring always stores more.

Mechanical energy and dissipative work

With only conservative forces doing work, kinetic plus potential energy remains constant. Gravity can convert potential energy to kinetic energy without changing their sum. Friction reduces mechanical energy while increasing internal energy of the wider system.

Choose the system and identify external work before writing an energy balance. A change of mechanical energy equals nonconservative work when conservative interactions are represented by U. Total energy includes the internal energy produced by dissipation.

Mechanical energy and dissipative work
\[ K_i+U_i+W_{nc}=K_f+U_f \]
Symbols and assumptions: K and U are kinetic and potential energies (J); W_nc is work of forces not represented by U (J); i/f are initial/final. Use a consistent reference for potential and include any rotational kinetic energy required by the model.
Other useful formulas
Pendulum released from angle θ₀
\[ v_{\rm bottom}=\sqrt{2gL(1-\cos\theta_0)},\qquad T_{\rm bottom}=mg(3-2\cos\theta_0) \]
Symbols and assumptions: L string length (m); θ₀ release angle from the vertical; g = 10 m/s²; m bob mass (kg); v speed (m/s); T tension (N). Light string, no air resistance.
Pulling a hanging part of a chain onto a table
\[ W=\frac{MgL}{2n^2} \]
Symbols and assumptions: M total mass (kg); L total length (m); 1/n fraction hanging; W work (J) needed to pull it back slowly onto a smooth table.
Watch the assumption

Mechanical-energy loss does not mean total energy disappears.

Worked example

A 2 kg body starts from rest 5 m above ground. Friction dissipates 20 J before arrival. Use g=10. Find final speed.

Show solution

Initial potential is 100 J. Final kinetic is 100−20=80 J, so v=√(2×80/2)=√80≈8.94 m/s.

Worked example · Numerical

A pendulum of length 2 m is released from rest at 60° to the vertical. Find the speed at the lowest point and the tension there in terms of mg.

Show solution
  1. Drop in height: L(1 − cos60°) = 2 × 0.5 = 1 m.
  2. v = √(2 × 10 × 1) = √20 ≈ 4.47 m/s.
  3. T = mg(3 − 2cos60°) = mg(3 − 1) = 2mg.

Answer: v ≈ 4.47 m/s, T = 2mg

Worked example · Numerical

A uniform chain of mass 4 kg and length 2 m lies on a smooth table with one third of its length hanging over the edge. Find the work needed to pull it slowly back onto the table.

Show solution
  1. Hanging mass = 4/3 kg; its centre is (2/3)/2 = 1/3 m below the table.
  2. W = (4/3) × 10 × (1/3) = 40/9 ≈ 4.44 J.
  3. Check with the formula: MgL/(2n²) = 4 × 10 × 2/(2 × 9) = 4.44 J.

Answer: ≈ 4.44 J

Worked example · Numerical

A 2 kg block slides from rest down a 5 m long incline at 37° (sin 37° = 0.6). μₖ = 0.25. Find its speed at the bottom.

Show solution
  1. Height h = 5 × 0.6 = 3 m, so U_i = 2 × 10 × 3 = 60 J.
  2. Friction = μmg cos37° = 0.25 × 20 × 0.8 = 4 N; work = −4 × 5 = −20 J.
  3. K_f = 60 − 20 = 40 J = ½ × 2 × v², so v² = 40.
  4. v ≈ 6.32 m/s.

Answer: √40 ≈ 6.32 m/s

Setting up an energy equation

  1. Choose the system and the start and end states.
  2. Write K + U for each state, using one fixed reference level for potential energy.
  3. Add the work of all forces not written as potential energy (friction, applied pushes, air drag): K_i + U_i + W_nc = K_f + U_f.
  4. Friction makes W_nc negative; a motor or a person pushing can make it positive.

On any smooth track, the speed after falling through height h is √(2gh) whatever the shape of the track. The time taken does depend on the shape.

Simple pendulum: speed and tension

A bob on a string of length L is released from rest at angle θ₀ from the vertical. It falls a height L(1 − cosθ₀) to the lowest point.

  • Speed at the bottom: \(v=\sqrt{2gL(1-\cos\theta_0)}\).
  • Tension at the bottom: T − mg = mv²/L gives \(T=mg(3-2\cos\theta_0)\).
  • Released from the horizontal (θ₀ = 90°): v = √(2gL) and T = 3mg.

Hanging chains: work and centre of mass

For a uniform chain of mass M and length L with a fraction 1/n hanging over the edge of a smooth table, the hanging part has mass M/n and its centre of mass is L/(2n) below the table. Pulling it back on the table needs

\[W=\frac{M}{n}\,g\,\frac{L}{2n}=\frac{MgL}{2n^2}.\]

Use the centre of mass of each piece whenever an extended body changes height.

Question patterns
  • Ball thrown from a height with speed u: speed on hitting the ground is √(u² + 2gh) in every direction of throw.
  • Pendulum: speed and tension at the lowest point for a given release angle.
  • Work against air resistance from the observed final speed.
  • Chain hanging over a table: work to pull it up, or speed as it slides off.
  • Rough incline: speed at the bottom using K_i + U_i + W_friction = K_f + U_f.
Another common mistake

For an extended object, use the rise of its centre of mass, not the rise of its end or top.

Another common mistake

"Mechanical energy is conserved" needs only that non-conservative forces do no work. A normal force that is always perpendicular to the motion does not spoil it.

6 Core

Power and efficiency

Power is the rate of energy transfer. Average power divides work by the time taken; instantaneous power uses the force and velocity at that instant. A machine can exert a high force at low speed while delivering modest power.

Efficiency compares useful output with energy or power supplied. In a constant-power acceleration, force is not constant: with a force parallel to motion, F=P/v decreases as speed rises.

Power and efficiency
\[ P_{\rm av}=\frac{W}{\Delta t},\quad P=\vec F\cdot\vec v,\quad \eta=\frac{P_{\rm useful}}{P_{\rm input}} \]
Symbols and assumptions: P is power (W = J/s); W in the numerator is work (J), not the watt symbol; Δt time (s); F force (N); v velocity (m/s); η is dimensionless efficiency, often expressed as a percentage.
Other useful formulas
Motion under constant power from rest
\[ v=\sqrt{\frac{2Pt}{m}}\propto t^{1/2},\qquad x\propto t^{3/2},\qquad F=\frac{P}{v}\propto t^{-1/2} \]
Symbols and assumptions: P constant power delivered to the body (W); m mass (kg); t time (s); v speed (m/s); x distance (m); F force (N). No losses, motion along a straight line.
Vehicle power
\[ P=(mg\sin\theta+f)\,v,\qquad v_{\max}=\frac{P}{f}\ \ (\text{level road}) \]
Symbols and assumptions: m mass (kg); g = 10 m/s²; θ incline angle (0 on level ground); f resistive force (N); v constant speed (m/s); P engine power (W). 1 hp = 746 W.
Watch the assumption

Power is a rate, not the amount of energy transferred.

Worked example

A motor raises 20 kg by 3 m in 2 s. Use g=10 and efficiency 75%. Find useful and input powers.

Show solution

Useful work is 600 J, giving 300 W. Input power is 300/0.75=400 W.

Worked example · Numerical

A pump lifts 600 kg of water per minute to a tank 20 m high. Find the useful power and the input power if the pump is 80% efficient.

Show solution
  1. Work per minute = mgh = 600 × 10 × 20 = 1.2 × 10⁵ J.
  2. Useful power = 1.2 × 10⁵/60 = 2000 W.
  3. Input power = 2000/0.8 = 2500 W = 2.5 kW.

Answer: 2 kW useful; 2.5 kW input

More solved problem patterns
Worked example · Ratio

Show solution

  2. x ∝ t^(3/2): x(4)/x(1) = 4^(3/2) = 8.
  3. Under constant force instead, v would scale as t (4×) and x as t² (16×).

Constant force versus constant power

Constant force from rest: a = F/m is constant, v = at, so P = Fv = (F²/m)t. Power grows linearly with time.

Constant power from rest (e.g. an engine at full throttle, no losses):

  1. The work done equals the kinetic energy gained: Pt = ½mv², so \(v=\sqrt{2Pt/m}\propto t^{1/2}\).
  2. Distance: \(x=\int v\,dt\propto t^{3/2}\).
  3. Force F = P/v ∝ t^(−1/2), so the force falls as the body speeds up.

Pumps, cranes and vehicles

  • Lifting water: power = (mass per second) × g × h. If the water also leaves with speed v, add (mass per second) × ½v².
  • Vehicle at constant speed on a level road: engine force = resistance f, so P = fv. The top speed with maximum power is v_max = P/f.
  • Up an incline: P = (mg sinθ + f)v.
  • Efficiency: input power = useful power/η. The difference becomes heat.

Units: 1 W = 1 J/s; 1 horsepower (hp) = 746 W.

Question patterns
  • Pump lifting water to a tank: find power, then input power for a given efficiency.
  • Machine gun firing n bullets per minute: power = (n/60) × ½mv².
  • Body under constant power: how v or x depends on t.
  • Car on a level road or an incline: power at a speed, or maximum speed for a given power.
  • F = f(t) on a body from rest: instantaneous power at time t.
Another common mistake

Under constant power the force is not constant. Using v = u + at with a fixed acceleration gives wrong answers.

Another common mistake

Kilowatt-hour is a unit of energy, not power. 1 kWh = 3.6 × 10⁶ J.

Elastic and inelastic collisions

During a short collision with negligible external impulse, total momentum is conserved. An elastic collision also conserves total kinetic energy. In a completely inelastic collision, the objects stick together; their final common velocity follows from momentum.

The coefficient of restitution describes the relative separation speed compared with approach speed along the collision axis. A zero value means no relative separation immediately after impact, but does not by itself describe every later interaction.

Elastic and inelastic collisions
\[ m_1u_1+m_2u_2=m_1v_1+m_2v_2,\quad e=\frac{v_2-v_1}{u_1-u_2},\quad V_{\rm stick}=\frac{m_1u_1+m_2u_2}{m_1+m_2} \]
Symbols and assumptions: m₁/m₂ are masses (kg); u₁/u₂ initial and v₁/v₂ final signed velocities (m/s); e is dimensionless restitution for a one-dimensional approach with u₁>u₂; V_stick is common velocity. Momentum requires negligible external impulse.
Other useful formulas
Kinetic energy lost when bodies stick
\[ \Delta K=\frac{m_1m_2\,(u_1-u_2)^2}{2(m_1+m_2)},\qquad \frac{\Delta K}{K_i}=\frac{m_2}{m_1+m_2}\ (u_2=0) \]
Symbols and assumptions: m₁, m₂ masses (kg); u₁, u₂ velocities before the collision (m/s); K_i initial kinetic energy (J); ΔK kinetic energy converted to other forms (J).
Ballistic pendulum
\[ v=\frac{m+M}{m}\sqrt{2gh} \]
Symbols and assumptions: m bullet mass and M block mass (kg); h height the block rises after the bullet embeds (m); v bullet speed (m/s); g = 10 m/s².
Body at rest splitting into two pieces
\[ m_1v_1=m_2v_2,\qquad \frac{K_1}{K_2}=\frac{m_2}{m_1} \]
Symbols and assumptions: m₁, m₂ masses of the pieces (kg); v₁, v₂ their speeds (m/s), in opposite directions; K₁, K₂ their kinetic energies (J).
Watch the assumption

Conserved momentum does not imply conserved kinetic energy.

Worked example

A 2 kg cart at 6 m/s sticks to a stationary 1 kg cart. Find final speed and kinetic-energy loss.

Show solution

Common speed is (2×6)/3=4 m/s. Initial K=36 J and final K=½×3×16=24 J, so 12 J becomes internal energy.

Worked example · Numerical

A 10 g bullet embeds in a 990 g block hanging on a string. The block rises 20 cm. Find the bullet’s speed and the fraction of kinetic energy lost.

Show solution

  2. Momentum in the impact: 0.01v = 1.0 × 2, so v = 200 m/s.
  3. Fraction of KE lost = M/(m + M) = 990/1000 = 0.99, i.e. 99%.

Answer: 200 m/s; 99% of the kinetic energy is lost

Types of collision: what is conserved

TypeMomentumKinetic energyeExample
ElasticConservedConserved1Collisions of atoms, nearly for steel balls
InelasticConservedPartly lost0 to 1Most real collisions
Perfectly inelasticConservedMaximum loss0Bodies stick: bullet in a block, mud on a wall

Total energy is always conserved; "lost" kinetic energy becomes heat, sound and deformation. Momentum is conserved because the collision forces are internal and act for a very short time. External forces such as gravity or friction are finite, so their impulse during the short impact is negligible (the impulse approximation).

Perfectly inelastic collisions and the ballistic pendulum

When the bodies stick, the common velocity follows from momentum alone, and the kinetic energy lost is

\[\Delta K=\frac12\,\frac{m_1m_2}{m_1+m_2}\,(u_1-u_2)^2.\]

With the target at rest, the fraction lost is m₂/(m₁ + m₂). Two equal masses lose half their kinetic energy. Not all kinetic energy can be lost: the centre of mass keeps moving, and its kinetic energy (P²/2M) must remain.

Ballistic pendulum: a bullet m embeds in a hanging block M, and the pair rises a height h. Use momentum for the collision, then energy for the swing: \(v=\dfrac{m+M}{m}\sqrt{2gh}\). Do not use energy conservation across the collision itself.

In two dimensions, add the momenta as vectors: the combined body moves along the direction of the total momentum.

Explosions and recoil

An explosion is a collision run backward. A body at rest that splits into two pieces has zero total momentum, so the pieces move in opposite directions with m₁v₁ = m₂v₂. Their kinetic energies share as K₁/K₂ = m₂/m₁: the lighter piece carries more energy. A gun and its bullet are the standard example: the gun recoils slowly but the bullet gets almost all the kinetic energy.

Question patterns
  • Bullet embeds in a block: common velocity, height risen, energy lost.
  • "Fraction of kinetic energy lost when a moving body sticks to an identical body at rest" (½).
  • Two bodies moving at right angles stick together: speed and direction of the combination.
  • A body at rest explodes into two pieces in a given mass ratio: ratio of speeds or kinetic energies.
  • Assertion–reason on whether momentum or kinetic energy is conserved in each type of collision.
Another common mistake

Energy is not conserved across a perfectly inelastic collision. In the ballistic pendulum, use momentum for the impact and energy only for the swing afterwards.

Another common mistake

"Perfectly inelastic" does not mean all kinetic energy is lost. Only the part associated with relative motion is lost; the centre of mass keeps its kinetic energy.

Coefficient of restitution, bouncing and oblique collisions

Real collisions lie between perfectly elastic and perfectly inelastic. The coefficient of restitution e measures how "bouncy" a collision is: it compares how fast the bodies separate with how fast they approached, along the line joining them at impact.

Use restitution along the impact direction and momentum conservation for the isolated pair. In a bounce from a fixed floor, rebound speed is e times arrival speed and rebound height is e² times the previous height.

Coefficient of restitution, bouncing and oblique collisions
\[ e=\frac{v_2-v_1}{u_1-u_2},\qquad h_n=e^{2n}h,\qquad v_n=e^n\sqrt{2gh} \]
Symbols and assumptions: e coefficient of restitution (no unit, 0 to 1); u₁, u₂ velocities before and v₁, v₂ after, along the line of impact (m/s); h drop height (m); hₙ height after the nth bounce (m); vₙ rebound speed after the nth bounce (m/s). Floor fixed, no air resistance.
Other useful formulas
Bouncing ball totals
\[ H_{\rm total}=h\,\frac{1+e^2}{1-e^2},\qquad T_{\rm total}=\sqrt{\frac{2h}{g}}\;\frac{1+e}{1-e} \]
Symbols and assumptions: h initial drop height (m); e coefficient of restitution with the floor; g = 10 m/s²; H_total total distance travelled (m); T_total total time until it stops (s). No air resistance.
Kinetic energy lost in a 1-D collision
\[ \Delta K=\frac{m_1m_2}{2(m_1+m_2)}\,(1-e^2)\,(u_1-u_2)^2 \]
Symbols and assumptions: m₁, m₂ masses (kg); u₁, u₂ approach velocities (m/s); e coefficient of restitution; ΔK kinetic energy converted to heat, sound and deformation (J).
Oblique impact on a smooth wall
\[ v_t'=v_t,\quad v_n'=e\,v_n,\quad \tan\theta'=\frac{\tan\theta}{e} \]
Symbols and assumptions: v_t, v_n tangential and normal velocity components before impact (m/s); primes mean after impact; θ, θ′ angles of incidence and rebound measured from the normal.
Watch the assumption

e applies to the components along the line of impact (the normal). In an oblique impact with a smooth surface, the component along the surface does not change.

Worked example

A ball is dropped from 10 m onto a floor with e = 0.5. How high does it rise after the first bounce?

Show solution

It arrives at √(2 × 10 × 10) = √200 m/s and leaves at 0.5√200 m/s. The height is e²h = 0.25 × 10 = 2.5 m.

Worked example · Numerical

A ball dropped from 9 m rises to 4 m after one bounce. Find e and the fraction of kinetic energy lost.

Show solution
  1. e = √(h₁/h) = √(4/9) = 2/3.
  2. Fraction kept = e² = 4/9, so fraction lost = 1 − 4/9 = 5/9 ≈ 56%.
  3. Check with heights: energy just before impact ∝ 9, just after ∝ 4; lost 5 out of 9.

Answer: e = 2/3; 5/9 of the kinetic energy is lost

Worked example · Numerical

A ball is dropped from 5 m on a floor with e = 0.5. Find the total distance travelled and the total time before it comes to rest.

Show solution

  2. First fall time t₀ = √(2 × 5/10) = 1 s.
  3. Total time = t₀(1 + e)/(1 − e) = 1 × 1.5/0.5 = 3 s.

A 1 kg ball moving at 6 m/s hits a 2 kg ball at rest head-on with e = 0.5. Find the final velocities and the kinetic energy lost.

General 1-D collision with restitution

Momentum conservation plus the definition of e give, for a head-on collision,

\[v_1=\frac{(m_1-em_2)u_1+(1+e)m_2u_2}{m_1+m_2},\qquad v_2=\frac{(m_2-em_1)u_2+(1+e)m_1u_1}{m_1+m_2}\]

Putting e = 1 recovers the elastic formulas; e = 0 gives the common velocity. The kinetic energy lost is

\[\Delta K=\frac12\,\frac{m_1m_2}{m_1+m_2}\,(1-e^2)(u_1-u_2)^2.\]

For equal masses with one at rest: v₁ = u(1 − e)/2 and v₂ = u(1 + e)/2.

Bouncing ball: heights, total distance and total time

  • Each bounce multiplies the speed by e, so the height is multiplied by e²: h₁ = e²h, h₂ = e⁴h, hₙ = e²ⁿh.
  • e can be measured from two heights: e = √(h₁/h).
  • Fraction of kinetic energy lost in each bounce = 1 − e².
  • Total distance before it stops: h + 2(e²h + e⁴h + …) = \(h\dfrac{1+e^2}{1-e^2}\).
  • Total time: with t₀ = √(2h/g), each bounce takes 2eⁿt₀, so T = \(t_0\dfrac{1+e}{1-e}\).

Oblique collisions

Ball hitting a smooth fixed wall or floor: resolve the velocity into a normal part and a tangential part. The tangential part is unchanged (no friction). The normal part reverses and is multiplied by e. If the ball arrives at angle θ to the normal, it leaves at θ′ with tanθ′ = tanθ/e. For e < 1 the rebound is flatter (θ′ > θ).

Two equal smooth spheres, one at rest, elastic oblique collision: after the collision they move at right angles to each other (θ₁ + θ₂ = 90°). This follows from momentum (a vector triangle) and energy (Pythagoras) together.

Question patterns
  • "A ball dropped from h rebounds to h′. Find e" (e = √(h′/h)), or the height after n bounces.
  • Total distance or total time before the ball stops.
  • Percentage of kinetic energy lost in a bounce.
  • Two bodies with a given e: final velocities and energy loss.
  • Ball striking a smooth floor at an angle: speed and angle after impact.
Another common mistake

Height ratio is e², not e. If a ball rises to one quarter of its drop height, e = 1/2.

Another common mistake

The fraction of kinetic energy lost per bounce is 1 − e², not 1 − e.

Vertical circles and minimum launch energy

A body on a light string in a vertical circle must have enough speed at the top to keep the string taut. At the limiting case, gravity alone supplies the inward acceleration and tension just becomes zero.

Between bottom and top the body rises by 2r, so kinetic energy decreases by 2mgr. Combining this energy change with the top contact condition gives the minimum bottom speed. A rigid rod can push as well as pull and has a different constraint.

Vertical circles and minimum launch energy
\[ v_{\rm top,min}=\sqrt{gr},\qquad v_{\rm bottom,min}=\sqrt{5gr} \]
Symbols and assumptions: v is speed (m/s); g gravity (m/s²); r circle radius (m). These limits apply to a point mass on a taut light string making a complete vertical circle without dissipative losses; the bottom result assumes energy conservation.
Other useful formulas
Tension at angle θ from the lowest point
\[ T=\frac{mu^2}{r}+mg(3\cos\theta-2),\qquad T_{\rm bottom}-T_{\rm top}=6mg \]
Symbols and assumptions: m mass (kg); u speed at the lowest point (m/s); r radius (m); θ angle from the lowest point; g = 10 m/s²; T tension (N). Light string, no losses.
Minimum conditions in other set-ups
\[ u_{\rm rod}=\sqrt{4gr},\qquad h_{\rm loop}=\tfrac52R,\qquad \cos\theta_{\rm leave}=\tfrac23\ (\text{sphere}) \]
Symbols and assumptions: u_rod minimum bottom speed for a light rigid rod (m/s); h_loop minimum release height above the bottom of a smooth loop of radius R (m); θ_leave angle from the vertical at which a body starting at rest on top of a smooth sphere leaves it.
Watch the assumption

Do not use the light-string minimum for a rigid rod or a track that can maintain contact differently.

Worked example

A string circle has radius 0.8 m and g=10. Find the minimum bottom speed.

Show solution

v=√(5×10×0.8)=√40≈6.32 m/s. At the top, minimum speed is √8≈2.83 m/s.

Worked example · Numerical

A 0.5 kg stone on a 1 m string is whirled in a vertical circle. Find the minimum speed at the bottom and the tension there in that case.

Show solution
  1. u_min = √(5gr) = √50 ≈ 7.07 m/s.
  2. At the bottom, T − mg = mu²/r = 0.5 × 50/1 = 25 N.
  3. T = 25 + 5 = 30 N = 6mg.

Answer: ≈ 7.07 m/s; T = 30 N

Derivation: tension at any point and the minimum speeds

  1. Let the bob have speed u at the bottom and be at angle θ measured from the lowest point. It has risen r(1 − cosθ), so \(v^2=u^2-2gr(1-\cos\theta)\).
  2. Radial equation (toward the centre): T − mg cosθ = mv²/r.
  3. Combine: \(T=\dfrac{mu^2}{r}+mg(3\cos\theta-2)\).
  4. At the top (θ = 180°): T_top = mu²/r − 5mg. The string stays taut if T_top ≥ 0, so u² ≥ 5gr and v_top² ≥ gr.
  5. T_bottom − T_top = (mu²/r + mg) − (mu²/r − 5mg) = 6mg, for any u that completes the circle.

In the limiting case (u = √(5gr)): bottom T = 6mg; side (θ = 90°) v = √(3gr), T = 3mg; top v = √(gr), T = 0. The kinetic energy at the bottom is 5 times that at the top.

What happens for smaller launch speeds

  • u ≤ √(2gr): the bob does not rise above the centre level. It swings back and forth like a pendulum; the string stays taut.
  • √(2gr) < u < √(5gr): the bob rises above the centre, but the tension becomes zero somewhere in the upper half. The string slackens and the bob follows a parabola.
  • u ≥ √(5gr): complete circles.

Rod, track and sphere variations

  • Light rigid rod (or a bead on a ring): the rod can push, so the bob only needs to reach the top with speed ≥ 0. Minimum bottom speed is √(4gr) = 2√(gr).
  • Block released on a smooth loop-the-loop track: it needs v_bottom² ≥ 5gR, so the release height must be at least 5R/2.
  • Body sliding off the top of a smooth sphere (from rest at the top): it leaves the surface when cosθ = 2/3, i.e. at a height 2R/3 above the centre (R/3 below the top).
Question patterns
  • "Minimum speed at the lowest point to complete the circle" for a string (√(5gr)) or a rod (√(4gr)).
  • Ratio of maximum to minimum tension, or T_bottom − T_top = 6mg.
  • Ratio of kinetic energies at the bottom and top in the limiting case (5 : 1).
  • Minimum release height on a loop-the-loop track (5R/2).
  • Body sliding from the top of a smooth hemisphere: height at which it leaves.
Another common mistake

At the top, the minimum speed is √(gr), not zero. Zero is the rod (or bead) answer.

Another common mistake

Release height for a loop-the-loop is 5R/2 above the lowest point, not 2R. Reaching the top needs speed as well as height.

One-dimensional elastic collision results

Elastic head-on collisions conserve both momentum and kinetic energy. Solving the two equations gives final velocities or, equivalently, conservation of momentum plus reversal of relative velocity.

The unequal-mass formulas reveal useful limits: a light projectile reflecting from a very heavy stationary target nearly reverses its velocity; equal masses exchange velocities. Keep signed velocities when both objects initially move.

One-dimensional elastic collision results
\[ v_1=\frac{(m_1-m_2)u_1+2m_2u_2}{m_1+m_2},\quad v_2=\frac{2m_1u_1+(m_2-m_1)u_2}{m_1+m_2} \]
Symbols and assumptions: m₁/m₂ masses (kg); u₁/u₂ initial and v₁/v₂ final signed velocities (m/s). Assumptions: one-dimensional perfectly elastic collision and negligible external impulse; denominator is positive total mass.
Other useful formulas
Energy transferred to a target at rest (elastic)
\[ \frac{K_2}{K_1}=\frac{4m_1m_2}{(m_1+m_2)^2},\qquad \frac{K_1^{\prime}}{K_1}=\left(\frac{m_1-m_2}{m_1+m_2}\right)^2 \]
Symbols and assumptions: m₁ moving body and m₂ target (kg); K₁ initial kinetic energy of m₁ (J); K₂ energy given to the target and K₁′ energy kept by m₁ (J). Head-on elastic collision with u₂ = 0.
Relative velocity reverses
\[ v_2-v_1=-(u_2-u_1) \]
Symbols and assumptions: u, v signed velocities before and after (m/s). Valid for every head-on elastic collision; together with momentum conservation it fixes both final velocities.
Watch the assumption

These formulas are not valid for sticking or energy-losing collisions.

Worked example

A 1 kg cart at 6 m/s hits a stationary 2 kg cart elastically. Find both velocities.

Show solution

v₁=(1−2)6/3=−2 m/s and v₂=2×1×6/3=4 m/s. Momentum is −2+8=6 kg m/s; energy is 2+16=18 J, matching the initial values.

Worked example · Numerical

A 2 kg ball moving at 6 m/s collides head-on and elastically with a 1 kg ball at rest. Find both final velocities.

Show solution
  1. v₁ = (2 − 1)/(2 + 1) × 6 = 2 m/s.
  2. v₂ = 2 × 2/(2 + 1) × 6 = 8 m/s.
  3. Check momentum: 12 = 2 × 2 + 1 × 8. Check energy: 36 J = 4 J + 32 J.

Answer: 2 m/s and 8 m/s, both forward

More solved problem patterns
Worked example · Ratio

A neutron (mass 1 u) hits a stationary carbon-12 nucleus head-on and elastically. What fraction of its kinetic energy does it lose?

Show solution
  1. Fraction transferred = 4m₁m₂/(m₁ + m₂)² = 4 × 1 × 12/13² = 48/169.
  2. 48/169 ≈ 0.28, so about 28% is lost.
  3. Fraction kept = ((1 − 12)/13)² = 121/169 ≈ 0.72. The two add to 1.

Answer: 48/169 ≈ 28%

Worked example · Numerical

A 1 kg ball moving at +4 m/s collides head-on and elastically with a 3 kg ball moving at −2 m/s. Find the final velocities.

Show solution
  1. v₁ = [(1 − 3)(4) + 2 × 3 × (−2)]/4 = (−8 − 12)/4 = −5 m/s.
  2. v₂ = [(3 − 1)(−2) + 2 × 1 × 4]/4 = (−4 + 8)/4 = +1 m/s.
  3. Check: momentum 4 − 6 = −2 before and −5 + 3 = −2 after. Separation 1 − (−5) = 6 = approach 4 − (−2).
  4. Energy: 8 + 6 = 14 J before; 12.5 + 1.5 = 14 J after.

Answer: v₁ = −5 m/s (rebounds), v₂ = +1 m/s

Go deeper · extra explanation and derivations

Derivation: final velocities in a 1-D elastic collision

  1. Momentum: m₁(u₁ − v₁) = m₂(v₂ − u₂).
  2. Kinetic energy: m₁(u₁² − v₁²) = m₂(v₂² − u₂²), i.e. m₁(u₁ − v₁)(u₁ + v₁) = m₂(v₂ − u₂)(v₂ + u₂).
  3. Divide the second by the first: u₁ + v₁ = v₂ + u₂, so v₂ − v₁ = u₁ − u₂. The relative velocity just reverses (e = 1).
  4. Solve the two linear equations to get the boxed results in the formula card above.

Shortcut in practice: write momentum conservation and "separation speed = approach speed". Two linear equations are easier than squaring.

Fraction of kinetic energy transferred to the target: \(\dfrac{4m_1m_2}{(m_1+m_2)^2}\). It is 100% only for equal masses. This is why materials rich in light nuclei (water, heavy water, graphite) are used to slow neutrons in reactors.

Moving wall: a ball hitting a massive wall that approaches at speed V rebounds with speed u + 2V (relative velocity reverses in the wall’s frame).

Another common mistake

During an elastic collision the kinetic energy dips while the bodies are compressed. It is equal before and after the impact, not at every instant.

Another common mistake

Keep signs. When the bodies approach each other, one initial velocity is negative; using speeds without signs gives wrong final velocities.

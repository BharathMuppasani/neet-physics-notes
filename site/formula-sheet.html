Mathematical Review & Physical World

Explanation and example →

Models, laws and sensible approximations
\[ \text{prediction}=\text{model}(\text{measured inputs}) \]
Symbols and assumptions: Prediction means the observable being calculated; model means a stated physical relationship; measured inputs carry units and uncertainty. This is a workflow, not an algebraic force law.

Explanation and example →

Proportionality, powers and scaling
\[ Q=Cx^a y^b,\qquad \frac{Q_2}{Q_1}=\left(\frac{x_2}{x_1}\right)^a\left(\frac{y_2}{y_1}\right)^b \]
Symbols and assumptions: Q is the dependent quantity; C is a constant with the required units; x and y are inputs; a and b are dimensionless exponents. Subscripts 1 and 2 denote two cases with the same C.

Explanation and example →

Trigonometry and small angles
\[ \sin\theta=\frac{\text{opposite}}{\text{hypotenuse}},\quad \cos\theta=\frac{\text{adjacent}}{\text{hypotenuse}},\quad \theta_{\rm rad}=\theta_{\rm deg}\frac{\pi}{180} \]
Symbols and assumptions: θ is the angle; triangle lengths must use the same unit; trigonometric ratios are dimensionless. For |θ| ≪ 1 rad, sin θ ≈ tan θ ≈ θ and cos θ ≈ 1−θ²/2.

Explanation and example →

Graphs: slope and intercept
\[ y=mx+b,\qquad m=\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} \]
Symbols and assumptions: x and y are the plotted quantities; m is slope with units [y]/[x]; b is the y-intercept with the same units as y; Δ denotes a difference. Use distinct points on a straight line.

Explanation and example →

Rates: differentiation
\[ \frac{d}{dt}(Ct^n)=nCt^{n-1},\qquad v=\frac{dx}{dt},\qquad a=\frac{dv}{dt} \]
Symbols and assumptions: x is position (m), t is time (s), v is velocity (m/s), a is acceleration (m/s²); C has units making Ctⁿ a position or the stated quantity; n is a dimensionless power.

Explanation and example →

Accumulation: integration and area
\[ \int_{t_1}^{t_2}Ct^n\,dt=\frac{C}{n+1}(t_2^{n+1}-t_1^{n+1}),\qquad \Delta x=\int v\,dt \]
Symbols and assumptions: C and n describe the rate; n ≠ −1 for the displayed power rule; t₁ and t₂ are limits (s); v is velocity (m/s); Δx is displacement (m). A velocity–time area therefore has units m.

Explanation and example →

Exponentials and logarithms
\[ \ln(ab)=\ln a+\ln b,\quad \ln(a/b)=\ln a-\ln b,\quad \ln(e^x)=x \]
Symbols and assumptions: a and b are positive dimensionless numbers; x is a dimensionless exponent; ln is the natural logarithm. The argument of an exponential must also be dimensionless, for example a cooling constant times time.

Units and Measurements

Explanation and example →

Physical quantities, SI units and conversion
\[ 1\,\mathrm{N}=1\,\mathrm{kg\,m\,s^{-2}},\quad 1\,\mathrm{J}=1\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2\,s^{-2}},\quad 1\,\mathrm{km\,h^{-1}}=\frac5{18}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}} \]
Symbols and assumptions: N is newton (force); J is joule (energy); kg, m and s are SI mass, length and time units. Kelvin, ampere, mole and candela are the other base units.

Explanation and example →

Dimensions and dimensional consistency
\[ [F]=MLT^{-2},\quad [E]=ML^2T^{-2},\quad [G]=M^{-1}L^3T^{-2} \]
Symbols and assumptions: Square brackets denote dimensions; M, L and T represent mass, length and time; F is force, E is energy and G is the gravitational constant. Dimensional exponents are independent of the SI unit choice.

Explanation and example →

Constructing a dependence using dimensions
\[ T_p=C\ell^a g^b\ \Longrightarrow\ a=\tfrac12,\ b=-\tfrac12,\quad T_p=C\sqrt{\ell/g} \]
Symbols and assumptions: Tₚ is pendulum period (s); ℓ is length (m); g is gravitational acceleration (m/s²); C is dimensionless; a and b are exponents. This analysis assumes no other relevant dimensional input.

Explanation and example →

Significant figures and rounding
\[ 0.00450=4.50\times10^{-3}\quad(3\ \text{significant figures}) \]
Symbols and assumptions: 4.50 is the measured coefficient and the power of ten fixes scale. Exact counts and defined conversion factors do not limit significant figures; measurement precision does.

Explanation and example →

Accuracy, precision and error estimates
\[ \bar x=\frac1N\sum x_i,\quad \Delta x=\frac1N\sum|x_i-\bar x|,\quad \text{percentage uncertainty}=100\frac{\Delta x}{|\bar x|} \]
Symbols and assumptions: xᵢ are readings; N is their count; x̄ is the mean; Δx is the mean absolute deviation, in the unit of x. The percentage formula assumes a nonzero mean and is not a universal statistical confidence interval.

Explanation and example →

Propagation of measurement uncertainty
\[ z=x\pm y:\ \Delta z\leq\Delta x+\Delta y;\qquad z=Cx^a y^b:\ \frac{\Delta z}{|z|}\lesssim |a|\frac{\Delta x}{|x|}+|b|\frac{\Delta y}{|y|} \]
Symbols and assumptions: x, y and z are measured/calculated quantities; Δ denotes an absolute uncertainty; C is exact here; a and b are powers. The product formula is a first-order worst-case estimate for small relative errors and nonzero x, y.

Explanation and example →

Vernier calipers, screw gauge and zero correction
\[ LC_v=1\,MSD-1\,VSD,\quad LC_s=\frac{p}{N},\quad x_{\rm true}=x_{\rm observed}-e_0 \]
Symbols and assumptions: LCᵥ is direct-vernier least count; MSD and VSD are scale division lengths; LCₛ is screw-gauge least count; p is pitch (advance per full turn); N is divisions per turn; e₀ is signed zero error. All lengths use consistent units.

Motion in a Straight Line

Explanation and example →

Position, displacement and distance
\[ \Delta x=x_f-x_i,\qquad d=\sum_j|\Delta x_j| \]
Symbols and assumptions: xᵢ and x_f are initial and final positions (m); Δx is displacement (m); d is total distance (m); j labels path segments without reversal. Coordinates depend on the chosen origin.

Explanation and example →

Average and instantaneous velocity and speed
\[ \bar v=\frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t},\quad \bar s=\frac{d}{\Delta t},\quad \bar s_{\rm equal\ distances}=\frac{2uv}{u+v} \]
Symbols and assumptions: v̄ is average signed velocity and s̄ is average speed (m/s); Δt is positive elapsed time (s); d is distance (m); u and v are positive speeds for two equal-distance legs. The last formula is not for equal-time legs.

Explanation and example →

Acceleration and whether speed increases
\[ a=\frac{dv}{dt},\qquad \frac{d(v^2)}{dt}=2va \]
Symbols and assumptions: v is signed velocity (m/s); a is acceleration (m/s²); t is time (s). The sign of va determines whether speed squared increases or decreases; the speed derivative at v=0 needs separate care.

Explanation and example →

Constant-acceleration equations
\[ v=u+at,\quad s=ut+\tfrac12at^2,\quad v^2=u^2+2as,\quad s=\tfrac12(u+v)t \]
Symbols and assumptions: u and v are initial/final velocities (m/s); a is constant acceleration (m/s²); t is elapsed time (s); s is signed displacement (m). All velocities, displacements and accelerations use the same chosen positive direction.

Explanation and example →

Reading x–t, v–t and a–t graphs
\[ v=\text{slope of }x(t),\quad a=\text{slope of }v(t),\quad \Delta x=\int v\,dt,\quad \Delta v=\int a\,dt \]
Symbols and assumptions: x is position (m), v velocity (m/s), a acceleration (m/s²), t time (s). Slopes and areas use labelled numerical axes, not the visual angle or pixel area.

Explanation and example →

Vertical motion and free fall
\[ v=u-gt,\quad y-y_0=ut-\tfrac12gt^2,\quad H=\frac{u^2}{2g},\quad T=\frac{2u}{g} \]
Symbols and assumptions: u is upward launch speed (m/s); v is signed vertical velocity (m/s); g is positive gravity magnitude (m/s²); y and y₀ are heights (m); H is maximum rise above launch; T is time back to launch height. g is assumed constant.

Explanation and example →

Relative motion and meeting conditions
\[ x_{A/B}=x_A-x_B,\quad v_{A/B}=v_A-v_B,\quad a_{A/B}=a_A-a_B \]
Symbols and assumptions: A and B label objects; x is signed position (m), v velocity (m/s), a acceleration (m/s²); A/B means A as seen from B. Use one shared coordinate axis before subtracting.

Vectors

Explanation and example →

Scalars, vectors and components
\[ \vec A=A_x\hat i+A_y\hat j,\quad A_x=A\cos\theta,\quad A_y=A\sin\theta,\quad A=\sqrt{A_x^2+A_y^2} \]
Symbols and assumptions: A is nonnegative magnitude; Aₓ and Aᵧ are signed components in the unit of A; î and ĵ are dimensionless unit vectors along x and y; θ is measured from +x. Orthogonal Cartesian axes are assumed.

Explanation and example →

Adding and subtracting vectors
\[ \vec R=\vec A+\vec B,\qquad R^2=A^2+B^2+2AB\cos\theta \]
Symbols and assumptions: R, A and B are magnitudes in the same unit; θ is the angle between A and B when drawn from a common tail; vector components add algebraically. For subtraction, replace B with −B.

Explanation and example →

Dot product and projection
\[ \vec A\cdot\vec B=AB\cos\theta=A_xB_x+A_yB_y+A_zB_z \]
Symbols and assumptions: A and B are magnitudes; θ is their included angle; Cartesian component products sum to the dot product. Its unit is the product of the units of A and B; the projection of A along B is A cosθ for B ≠ 0.

Explanation and example →

Cross product and the right-hand rule
\[ \vec A\times\vec B=AB\sin\theta\,\hat n,\qquad \hat i\times\hat j=\hat k,\quad \vec B\times\vec A=-\vec A\times\vec B \]
Symbols and assumptions: θ is the included angle, n̂ is the right-hand unit normal, î/ĵ/k̂ are x/y/z unit vectors; magnitude has the product unit of A and B. The cross product is zero for parallel nonzero vectors.

Explanation and example →

Resolving several vectors and equilibrium
\[ \sum\vec F=0\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \sum F_x=0,\ \sum F_y=0,\ \sum F_z=0 \]
Symbols and assumptions: F is force (N); x, y and z denote orthogonal axes. The equivalence states translational equilibrium in an inertial frame; rigid-body rotational equilibrium additionally requires zero net torque.

Motion in a Plane

Explanation and example →

Two-dimensional position, velocity and acceleration
\[ \vec r=\vec r_0+\vec u t+\tfrac12\vec a t^2,\qquad \vec v=\vec u+\vec a t \]
Symbols and assumptions: r and r₀ are final/initial positions (m); u and v are velocities (m/s); a is constant acceleration (m/s²); t is elapsed time (s). Each vector equation holds component by component in an inertial frame.

Explanation and example →

Projectile motion from component independence
\[ x=u\cos\theta\,t,\quad y=u\sin\theta\,t-\tfrac12gt^2,\quad T=\frac{2u\sin\theta}{g},\quad H=\frac{u^2\sin^2\theta}{2g},\quad R=\frac{u^2\sin2\theta}{g} \]
Symbols and assumptions: u is launch speed (m/s); θ is launch angle above horizontal; x and y are displacement from launch (m); g is downward gravity magnitude (m/s²); T, H and R are same-level flight time (s), maximum rise (m) and range (m). Assume constant g and no drag.

Explanation and example →

Horizontal launch and targets at another height
\[ t=\sqrt{\frac{2h}{g}},\qquad R=u_x\sqrt{\frac{2h}{g}},\qquad v_y=-gt \]
Symbols and assumptions: h is launch height above the landing plane (m); uₓ is horizontal launch speed (m/s); t is landing time (s); R is horizontal range (m); vᵧ is vertical velocity with upward positive. These formulas assume horizontal launch, constant g and no drag.

Explanation and example →

Relative velocity: rain and river crossings
\[ \vec v_{A/G}=\vec v_{A/B}+\vec v_{B/G},\quad t_{\min}=\frac{d}{u},\quad t_{\rm opposite}=\frac{d}{\sqrt{u^2-w^2}} \]
Symbols and assumptions: A is swimmer, B water, G ground; d is river width (m); u is swimmer speed relative to water (m/s); w is current speed (m/s). The directly-opposite formula requires u>w and an upstream component equal to w.

Explanation and example →

Uniform circular motion and centripetal acceleration
\[ v=\omega r,\quad \omega=\frac{2\pi}{T},\quad a_c=\frac{v^2}{r}=\omega^2r \]
Symbols and assumptions: v is tangential speed (m/s); ω is angular speed (rad/s); r is radius (m); T is period (s); a_c is inward acceleration (m/s²). Uniform motion has no tangential acceleration.

Explanation and example →

Nonuniform circular motion
\[ a_t=\frac{dv}{dt}=r\alpha,\quad a_r=\frac{v^2}{r},\quad a=\sqrt{a_t^2+a_r^2} \]
Symbols and assumptions: a_t and a_r are tangential and inward accelerations (m/s²); r is fixed radius (m); α is angular acceleration (rad/s²); v is speed (m/s); a is total magnitude. The radial and tangential unit directions change with position.

Laws of Motion · Part 1

Explanation and example →

Inertia and Newton’s three laws
\[ \vec F_{\rm net}=\frac{d\vec p}{dt}=m\vec a\quad(\text{constant }m),\qquad \vec p=m\vec v \]
Symbols and assumptions: F_net is sum of external forces (N); p is momentum (kg m/s); m is mass (kg); a acceleration (m/s²); v velocity (m/s). These equations are used in an inertial reference frame.

Explanation and example →

Free-body diagrams and contact forces
\[ W=mg,\qquad W_{\parallel}=mg\sin\theta,\qquad W_{\perp}=mg\cos\theta \]
Symbols and assumptions: W is gravitational force (N); m mass (kg); g gravity magnitude (m/s²); θ incline angle from horizontal. Parallel weight points downhill; perpendicular weight points into the plane. These are components of one force.

Explanation and example →

Connected masses, pulleys and string constraints
\[ a=\frac{(m_2-m_1)g}{m_1+m_2},\qquad T=\frac{2m_1m_2g}{m_1+m_2} \]
Symbols and assumptions: m₁ and m₂ are masses (kg) with m₂>m₁; a is simple Atwood acceleration magnitude (m/s²); T is string tension (N); g gravity (m/s²). Assumptions: light taut string, fixed smooth massless pulley and negligible drag.

Explanation and example →

Apparent weight and accelerated frames
\[ N=m(g+a)\ \text{(upward acceleration)},\quad N=m(g-a)\ \text{(downward acceleration)},\quad \vec F_{\rm pseudo}=-m\vec a_{\rm frame} \]
Symbols and assumptions: N is scale force (N); m mass (kg); a is the positive acceleration magnitude (m/s²); g gravity magnitude. Downward contact formula assumes a≤g and maintained contact. Pseudo force belongs only to the specified non-inertial frame.

Explanation and example →

Impulse and momentum conservation
\[ \vec J=\int\vec F\,dt=\Delta\vec p,\qquad \vec F_{\rm av}=\frac{\Delta\vec p}{\Delta t},\qquad \vec P_f=\vec P_i\quad(J_{\rm ext}=0) \]
Symbols and assumptions: J is impulse (N s); F force (N); p momentum (kg m/s); Δt interaction duration (s); P is total system momentum. Conservation applies to the chosen system when external impulse is zero or negligible.

Explanation and example →

Forces in circular motion and banking
\[ \sum F_r=\frac{mv^2}{r},\qquad \tan\theta=\frac{v^2}{rg}\quad\text{(frictionless bank)} \]
Symbols and assumptions: m is mass (kg); v speed (m/s); r turn radius (m); F_r actual force components directed inward (N); θ road-bank angle; g gravity (m/s²). The banking relation assumes no vertical acceleration and no friction.

Laws of Motion · Part 2: Friction

Explanation and example →

Static friction is adjustable
\[ 0\leq |f_s|\leq\mu_sN,\qquad f_{s,\max}=\mu_sN \]
Symbols and assumptions: fₛ is signed static friction along the contact (N); μₛ is dimensionless static coefficient; N is normal force (N). Friction opposes the relative sliding tendency, not necessarily the centre-of-mass velocity.

Explanation and example →

Kinetic friction and work
\[ f_k=\mu_kN,\qquad W_f=-f_ks\quad\text{(fixed surface, opposite sliding)} \]
Symbols and assumptions: fₖ is kinetic friction magnitude (N); μₖ is dimensionless kinetic coefficient; N normal force (N); s is sliding distance (m); W_f is work on the sliding body (J) for constant opposing friction on a stationary surface.

Explanation and example →

Inclines, angle of friction and angle of repose
\[ \tan\theta_r=\mu_s,\qquad a=g(\sin\theta-\mu_k\cos\theta)\quad\text{(sliding downhill)} \]
Symbols and assumptions: θᵣ is repose angle; θ actual incline angle; μₛ and μₖ static/kinetic coefficients; a is downhill acceleration (m/s²); g gravity. The acceleration formula assumes downhill sliding, no other forces and maintained contact.

Explanation and example →

Pulling at an angle and maximum available friction
\[ N=mg-P\sin\theta,\qquad P\cos\theta=\mu_s(mg-P\sin\theta) \]
Symbols and assumptions: P is pull magnitude (N), θ angle above horizontal; m mass (kg), g gravity (m/s²); N normal force (N); μₛ static coefficient. Equations describe impending horizontal slip with N≥0 and no vertical acceleration.

Explanation and example →

Friction in connected bodies and turning
\[ a_{\max}=\mu_sg\quad\text{(upper block)},\qquad v_{\max}=\sqrt{\mu_srg}\quad\text{(level turn)} \]
Symbols and assumptions: a_max is maximum no-slip acceleration (m/s²) for a horizontally carried block with N=mg; v_max maximum turn speed (m/s); r radius (m); μₛ static coefficient; g gravity. The road relation assumes level motion and no other lateral force.

Work, Energy & Power

Explanation and example →

Work by constant and variable forces
\[ W=\vec F\cdot\vec s=Fs\cos\theta,\qquad W=\int_{x_i}^{x_f}F_x\,dx \]
Symbols and assumptions: W is work (J); F is constant force magnitude (N); s displacement magnitude (m); θ force–displacement angle; Fₓ is the signed force component along x; xᵢ/x_f are integration limits (m). The first formula requires constant force.

Explanation and example →

Kinetic energy and the work–energy theorem
\[ K=\tfrac12mv^2,\qquad W_{\rm net}=K_f-K_i \]
Symbols and assumptions: K is translational kinetic energy (J); m mass (kg); v speed (m/s); W_net is the sum of works by all forces (J); i/f mean initial/final. The particle form does not include a separate rotational energy.

Explanation and example →

Conservative forces and potential energy
\[ W_c=-\Delta U,\quad F_x=-\frac{dU}{dx},\quad U_g=mgh,\quad U_s=\tfrac12kx^2 \]
Symbols and assumptions: U is potential energy (J); W_c conservative work (J); Fₓ force (N); m mass (kg); g constant gravity; h height above a reference (m); k spring constant (N/m); x spring displacement (m). Spring law assumes Hookean behaviour.

Explanation and example →

Mechanical energy and dissipative work
\[ K_i+U_i+W_{nc}=K_f+U_f \]
Symbols and assumptions: K and U are kinetic and potential energies (J); W_nc is work of forces not represented by U (J); i/f are initial/final. Use a consistent reference for potential and include any rotational kinetic energy required by the model.

Explanation and example →

Power and efficiency
\[ P_{\rm av}=\frac{W}{\Delta t},\quad P=\vec F\cdot\vec v,\quad \eta=\frac{P_{\rm useful}}{P_{\rm input}} \]
Symbols and assumptions: P is power (W = J/s); W in the numerator is work (J), not the watt symbol; Δt time (s); F force (N); v velocity (m/s); η is dimensionless efficiency, often expressed as a percentage.

Explanation and example →

Elastic and inelastic collisions
\[ m_1u_1+m_2u_2=m_1v_1+m_2v_2,\quad e=\frac{v_2-v_1}{u_1-u_2},\quad V_{\rm stick}=\frac{m_1u_1+m_2u_2}{m_1+m_2} \]
Symbols and assumptions: m₁/m₂ are masses (kg); u₁/u₂ initial and v₁/v₂ final signed velocities (m/s); e is dimensionless restitution for a one-dimensional approach with u₁>u₂; V_stick is common velocity. Momentum requires negligible external impulse.

Explanation and example →

Vertical circles and minimum launch energy
\[ v_{\rm top,min}=\sqrt{gr},\qquad v_{\rm bottom,min}=\sqrt{5gr} \]
Symbols and assumptions: v is speed (m/s); g gravity (m/s²); r circle radius (m). These limits apply to a point mass on a taut light string making a complete vertical circle without dissipative losses; the bottom result assumes energy conservation.

Explanation and example →

One-dimensional elastic collision results
\[ v_1=\frac{(m_1-m_2)u_1+2m_2u_2}{m_1+m_2},\quad v_2=\frac{2m_1u_1+(m_2-m_1)u_2}{m_1+m_2} \]
Symbols and assumptions: m₁/m₂ masses (kg); u₁/u₂ initial and v₁/v₂ final signed velocities (m/s). Assumptions: one-dimensional perfectly elastic collision and negligible external impulse; denominator is positive total mass.

System of Particles & Rotational Motion

Explanation and example →

Centre of mass and its motion
\[ \vec R=\frac{\sum m_i\vec r_i}{M},\quad M=\sum m_i,\quad \vec F_{\rm ext}=M\vec A_{CM} \]
Symbols and assumptions: R is centre-of-mass position (m); mᵢ masses (kg); rᵢ positions (m); M total mass (kg); A_CM centre-of-mass acceleration (m/s²); F_ext total external force (N). The last equation assumes a fixed-mass system.

Explanation and example →

Angular variables and fixed-axis kinematics
\[ \omega=\omega_0+\alpha t,\quad \theta=\omega_0t+\tfrac12\alpha t^2,\quad v=r\omega,\quad a_t=r\alpha \]
Symbols and assumptions: ω₀/ω are initial/final angular velocities (rad/s); α constant angular acceleration (rad/s²); θ angular displacement (rad); t time (s); r perpendicular axis distance (m); v tangential speed (m/s); a_t tangential acceleration.

Explanation and example →

Torque, couples and equilibrium
\[ \vec\tau=\vec r\times\vec F,\quad |\tau|=Fd_\perp,\quad \sum\vec F=0,\quad \sum\vec\tau=0 \]
Symbols and assumptions: r is position from chosen origin to force application (m); F force (N); d_⊥ perpendicular distance to its line of action (m); τ torque (N m). Both balance equations are needed for static rigid-body equilibrium.

Explanation and example →

Moment of inertia and standard shapes
\[ I=\sum m_ir_i^2,\quad I_{\rm ring}=MR^2,\quad I_{\rm disc}=\tfrac12MR^2,\quad I_{\rm sphere}=\tfrac25MR^2,\quad I_{\rm rod,CM}=\tfrac1{12}M\ell^2 \]
Symbols and assumptions: I is moment of inertia (kg m²); mᵢ mass elements (kg); rᵢ perpendicular axis distances (m); M total mass; R radius; ℓ rod length. Ring/disc axes are central perpendicular axes; solid-sphere axis is a diameter; rod axis is perpendicular through its midpoint. All shapes are uniform.

Explanation and example →

Parallel-axis and perpendicular-axis theorems
\[ I=I_{CM}+Md^2,\qquad I_z=I_x+I_y\quad\text{(plane lamina in xy)} \]
Symbols and assumptions: I/I_CM are inertias (kg m²) about parallel axes; M mass (kg); d separation (m). For the second theorem, x and y lie in the lamina and z is perpendicular, all through one point.

Explanation and example →

Rotational dynamics, energy and power
\[ \tau_{\rm net}=I\alpha,\quad K_{\rm rot}=\tfrac12I\omega^2,\quad W=\int\tau\,d\theta,\quad P=\tau\omega \]
Symbols and assumptions: τ is axial torque (N m); I constant axis inertia (kg m²); α angular acceleration (rad/s²); ω angular velocity (rad/s); K_rot and W energy/work (J); θ angle (rad); P power (W). Fixed-axis rigid rotation is assumed.

Explanation and example →

Angular momentum and conservation
\[ \vec L=\vec r\times\vec p,\quad L=I\omega,\quad \tau_{\rm ext}=\frac{dL}{dt},\quad I_i\omega_i=I_f\omega_f\quad(\tau_{\rm ext}=0) \]
Symbols and assumptions: L is angular momentum (kg m²/s); r position from origin (m); p momentum (kg m/s); I inertia about the fixed axis; ω angular velocity. Scalar conservation applies along that axis with negligible external torque.

Explanation and example →

Rolling without slipping
\[ v_{CM}=\omega R,\quad K=\tfrac12Mv_{CM}^2+\tfrac12I_{CM}\omega^2,\quad a=\frac{g\sin\theta}{1+I_{CM}/(MR^2)} \]
Symbols and assumptions: M mass (kg); R rolling radius (m); v_CM centre speed (m/s); ω angular speed (rad/s); I_CM inertia about centre axis (kg m²); θ incline angle; a downhill acceleration. Assumes sufficient static friction, no slipping and no rolling resistance.

Gravitation

Explanation and example →

Universal gravitation and field superposition
\[ F=\frac{GMm}{r^2},\qquad g_r=\frac{GM}{r^2},\qquad \vec g_{\rm net}=\sum\vec g_i \]
Symbols and assumptions: G is gravitational constant (N m²/kg²); M source mass and m test mass (kg); r centre separation (m); F attractive force (N); g_r field magnitude (m/s²). Point masses or an external spherical field are assumed.

Explanation and example →

Gravity above and below the surface
\[ g_h=g_0\left(\frac{R}{R+h}\right)^2,\qquad g_d=g_0\left(1-\frac dR\right) \]
Symbols and assumptions: g₀ surface gravity (m/s²); R Earth radius (m); h height above surface (m); d depth below surface (m); g_h/g_d gravity magnitudes. The depth formula assumes a uniform, nonrotating spherical Earth; the height formula assumes spherical symmetry.

Explanation and example →

Gravitational potential and energy
\[ V=-\frac{GM}{r},\quad U=mV=-\frac{GMm}{r},\quad g_r=-\frac{dV}{dr}\ \text{(signed outward component)} \]
Symbols and assumptions: V is potential (J/kg); U potential energy (J); G gravitational constant; M source and m test masses (kg); r centre distance (m). Zero is at infinity; outside a spherical mass or for point masses. The signed field is inward.

Explanation and example →

Circular satellites and orbital energy
\[ v_o=\sqrt{\frac{GM}{r}},\quad T=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{r^3}{GM}},\quad E=-\frac{GMm}{2r},\quad K=\frac{GMm}{2r} \]
Symbols and assumptions: v_o orbital speed (m/s); T period (s); G constant; M central mass (kg); m satellite mass (kg); r circular centre-to-centre radius (m); E total and K kinetic energies (J). Ignore drag and the satellite’s effect on the source.

Explanation and example →

Escape speed and gravitational binding
\[ v_e=\sqrt{\frac{2GM}{r}}=\sqrt{2g_0R}\quad\text{(surface }r=R) \]
Symbols and assumptions: v_e escape speed (m/s); G constant; M source mass (kg); r initial centre distance (m); g₀ surface gravity; R source radius. Zero potential is at infinity; ignore drag and subsequent propulsion.

Explanation and example →

Kepler’s laws and geostationary satellites
\[ T^2=\frac{4\pi^2}{GM}a^3,\qquad \frac{dA}{dt}=\text{constant} \]
Symbols and assumptions: T is orbital period (s); a is ellipse semi-major axis (m), not acceleration; G constant; M dominant central mass (kg); A swept area (m²). The test-body approximation neglects orbiting mass compared with M.

Explanation and example →

Shells, solid spheres and rotation of Earth
\[ g_{\rm inside}=\frac{GMr}{R^3},\quad V_{\rm inside}=-\frac{GM}{2R}\left(3-\frac{r^2}{R^2}\right),\quad g_{\rm eq,app}=g_0-\Omega^2R \]
Symbols and assumptions: G gravitational constant; M sphere mass (kg); R radius and r interior centre distance (m), 0≤r≤R; g field magnitude (m/s²); V potential (J/kg), zero at infinity. Interior formulas require uniform density. Ω Earth angular speed (rad/s); g₀ gravitational surface value; equatorial apparent value uses a spherical rotating Earth.

Mechanical Properties of Solids

Stress
\[ \sigma = \frac{F}{A} \]
SI unit N m⁻² = pascal (Pa). Dimensions \([\mathrm{ML^{-1}T^{-2}}]\), the same as pressure.
Symbols and assumptions: σ = tensile or compressive stress (Pa); F = normal force (N); A = loaded cross-sectional area (m²).
Strain
\[ \text{strain} = \frac{\text{change in size}}{\text{original size}} \]
Pure number, dimensionless \([\mathrm{M^0L^0T^0}]\). Often quoted as a % (strain 0.002 = 0.2%).
Symbols and assumptions: Change in size and original size must use the same units. For a wire, longitudinal strain is ΔL/L, with L its original length and ΔL its extension.
Hooke's law
\[ \text{stress} \propto \text{strain} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \text{stress} = k \times \text{strain} \]
\(k\) is the modulus of elasticity. Its unit is Pa and its dimensions are \([\mathrm{ML^{-1}T^{-2}}]\) (strain has none). It is valid only up to the proportional limit.
Symbols and assumptions: k here means an elastic modulus (Pa), not a spring constant (N/m). Stress and strain must be the corresponding pair, such as longitudinal stress and longitudinal strain.
Young's modulus
\[ Y = \frac{\text{tensile (or compressive) stress}}{\text{longitudinal strain}} = \frac{F/A}{\Delta L/L} = \frac{F\,L}{A\,\Delta L} = \frac{MgL}{\pi r^{2}\,\Delta L} \]
Unit N m⁻² (Pa); dimensions \([\mathrm{ML^{-1}T^{-2}}]\). Rearranged: \(\Delta L = \dfrac{FL}{AY} = \dfrac{4FL}{\pi d^{2} Y}\).
Symbols and assumptions: Y = Young’s modulus (Pa); F = axial force (N); L = original length (m); ΔL = extension (m); A = cross-sectional area (m²); r = wire radius (m); d = diameter = 2r; M = hanging mass (kg); g = gravitational acceleration (m/s²). The Mg form assumes a static hanging load and negligible wire weight.
Uniform wire under its own weight
\[\Delta L=\int_0^L\frac{Mgx}{LAY}\,dx=\frac{MgL}{2AY}=\frac{\rho gL^2}{2Y}\]
With a point mass m at the bottom, add its constant-tension contribution: \(\Delta L=(m+M/2)gL/(AY)\).
Symbols and assumptions: M = wire mass (kg); m = additional end mass (kg); ρ = wire density (kg/m³); L = original length (m); x = distance above the free lower end (m); A = wire area (m²); Y = Young’s modulus (Pa); g = gravitational acceleration (m/s²); ΔL = extension (m).
Shear modulus
\[ G = \frac{\text{shearing stress}}{\text{shearing strain}} = \frac{F/A}{\Delta x/L} = \frac{F\,L}{A\,\Delta x} \approx \frac{F}{A\,\theta} \]
\(A\) is the area of the face the force acts along; \(L\) is the distance between the fixed and moving faces; \(\theta \approx \Delta x/L\) in radian. Unit Pa; dimensions \([\mathrm{ML^{-1}T^{-2}}]\). For most solids \(G \approx Y/3\).
Symbols and assumptions: G = shear modulus (Pa); F = tangential force (N); Δx = sideways displacement (m); A = loaded face area (m²); L = separation of the faces (m); θ = small shear angle (radians); Y = Young’s modulus (Pa).
Bulk modulus
\[ B = -\frac{p}{\Delta V/V} = -\frac{V\,\Delta p}{\Delta V} \]
\(p\) = hydraulic stress (extra pressure). The minus sign makes \(B\) positive, since \(\Delta V < 0\) when pressure rises. Unit Pa; dimensions \([\mathrm{ML^{-1}T^{-2}}]\).
Symbols and assumptions: B = bulk modulus (Pa); p or Δp = added pressure (Pa); V = initial volume (m³); ΔV = signed volume change (m³), negative on compression. The finite-change expression assumes small compression and approximately constant B.
Compressibility
\[ k = \frac{1}{B} = -\frac{1}{\Delta p}\,\frac{\Delta V}{V} \]
Unit Pa⁻¹ (m² N⁻¹); dimensions \([\mathrm{M^{-1}LT^{2}}]\). Gases ≫ liquids ≫ solids.
Symbols and assumptions: k = compressibility (Pa⁻¹), distinct from a spring constant; B = bulk modulus (Pa); Δp = pressure increase (Pa); V = initial volume (m³); ΔV = signed volume change (m³).
Sphere under pressure
\[ \frac{\Delta V}{V} = 3\,\frac{\Delta r}{r} \;\Rightarrow\; \frac{\Delta r}{r} = -\frac{p}{3B} \]
Because \(V \propto r^{3}\). Likewise, for a cube \(\Delta V/V = 3\,\Delta a/a\).
Symbols and assumptions: r = original sphere radius (m); Δr = signed radius change (m), negative on compression; p = added pressure (Pa); B = bulk modulus (Pa); V and ΔV = initial volume and its signed change. For a cube, a is its original side and Δa its change. The positive fractional decrease is p/(3B).
Poisson's ratio
\[ \sigma = -\frac{\text{lateral strain}}{\text{longitudinal strain}} = -\frac{\Delta d/d}{\Delta L/L} \]
Dimensionless, no unit. The minus sign makes \(\sigma\) positive when stretching causes lateral contraction. Here σ denotes Poisson's ratio (often written ν), not stress. For stable isotropic linear elasticity, theory allows \(-1 < \sigma < 0.5\); many familiar materials have about 0 to 0.5. Steel ≈ 0.28–0.30, rubber ≈ 0.49, cork ≈ 0.
Symbols and assumptions: σ (or ν) = Poisson’s ratio; d = original diameter; Δd = signed diameter change; L = original length; ΔL = signed length change. The strains use matching length units. A contracting diameter has Δd < 0 while a stretched length has ΔL > 0.
Volume change of a stretched wire
\[ \frac{\Delta V}{V} = (1-2\sigma)\,\frac{\Delta L}{L} \]
To first order in small strains, from \(V = \tfrac{\pi}{4}d^{2}L\): \(\Delta V/V = \Delta L/L + 2\,\Delta d/d\). Volume is unchanged only if \(\sigma = 0.5\) (incompressible).
Symbols and assumptions: V = original volume; ΔV = signed volume change; L = original length; ΔL = extension; σ = Poisson’s ratio. Both sides are dimensionless. This is the first-order small-strain result.
Energy stored
\[ U = \tfrac12 F\,\Delta l = \tfrac12\,\frac{YA}{L}\,(\Delta l)^{2} \]
Same as \(\tfrac12 kx^{2}\) with \(k = YA/L\).
Symbols and assumptions: U = stored elastic energy (J); F = final axial force (N); Δl = extension (m); Y = Young’s modulus (Pa); A = cross-sectional area (m²); L = original length (m); k = wire stiffness (N/m); x = extension in the spring notation. Loading is quasistatic and linear elastic.
Energy per unit volume
\[ u = \tfrac12\,\text{stress}\times\text{strain} = \tfrac12 Y(\text{strain})^{2} = \frac{(\text{stress})^{2}}{2Y} \]
Divide \(U\) by the volume \(AL\). Unit J m⁻³, dimensions \([\mathrm{ML^{-1}T^{-2}}]\), the same as stress.
Symbols and assumptions: u = elastic energy density (J/m³); U = total stored energy (J); Y = Young’s modulus (Pa); AL = initial wire volume (m³). Use dimensionless strain, not its percentage value.
Thermal stress in a clamped rod
\[ \text{strain} = \frac{L\alpha\Delta T}{L} = \alpha\Delta T,\qquad \text{stress} = Y\alpha\Delta T,\qquad F = YA\alpha\Delta T \]
\(\alpha\) = coefficient of linear expansion (K⁻¹ or °C⁻¹). The length cancels: the stress and the force do not depend on \(L\).
Symbols and assumptions: L = original length (m); α = linear expansion coefficient (K⁻¹); ΔT = temperature increase (K or °C difference); Y = Young’s modulus (Pa); A = cross-sectional area (m²); F = force magnitude (N). Heating produces compression; the displayed stress is its magnitude. Supports are rigid and expansion is fully prevented.
Minimum rope radius
\[ \frac{Mg}{\pi r^{2}} \le \sigma_y \;\Rightarrow\; r \ge \sqrt{\frac{Mg}{\pi\sigma_y}} = \sqrt{\frac{10^{4}\times9.8}{3.14\times3\times10^{8}}} \approx 1\ \text{cm} \]
Engineers add a safety factor of about 10 (design for 10⁵ kg), which gives \(r \approx 3\) cm. A single 3 cm rod would be stiff like a bar, so ropes are made by braiding many thin wires. This gives the same area but is flexible and strong.
Symbols and assumptions: M = supported mass (kg); g = gravitational acceleration (m/s²); r = rope radius (m); σ_y = material yield stress (Pa). The numerical example uses M = 10⁴ kg and σ_y = 3 × 10⁸ Pa, before adding a safety factor.
Sag (bending) of a centrally loaded beam
\[ \delta = \frac{W\,l^{3}}{4\,b\,d^{3}\,Y} \]
To reduce bending: use a material with large \(Y\), keep the span short, and above all increase the depth \(d\). \(\delta \propto 1/d^{3}\), so doubling the depth cuts the sag to 1/8, while doubling the breadth only halves it.
Symbols and assumptions: δ = downward displacement at the middle (m); W = load force (N), not mass; l = distance between supports (m); b = breadth (m); d = vertical depth (m); Y = Young’s modulus (Pa). This is the small-deflection result for a rectangular beam supported at both ends and loaded centrally.
Maximum height of a mountain
\[ h\rho g \le \sigma_\text{limit} \;\Rightarrow\; h_\text{max} = \frac{30\times10^{7}}{3\times10^{3}\times10} = 10^{4}\ \text{m} = 10\ \text{km} \]
Mount Everest is about 8.8 km, just under this limit. (NCERT notes that the stress at the base is really shear, and the estimate is rough.)
Symbols and assumptions: h = height (m); ρ = rock density (kg/m³); g = gravitational acceleration (m/s²); σ_limit = limiting stress (Pa). The model treats the mountain as a uniform column; it is an order-of-magnitude estimate.

Fluids I · Pressure & Flow

Density and relative density
\[ \rho = \frac{m}{V}, \qquad \text{RD} = \frac{\rho}{1000\ \text{kg m}^{-3}} \]
1 g cm⁻³ = 1000 kg m⁻³. Water: 1000; seawater: 1030; ice: about 917 (problems often use 900); mercury: 13600; air: about 1.2–1.3 kg m⁻³.
Symbols and assumptions: ρ = density (kg/m³); m = mass (kg); V = volume (m³); RD = relative density compared with water of density 1000 kg/m³, dimensionless.
Mixing two liquids
\[ \rho_{\text{equal }V} = \frac{\rho_1 + \rho_2}{2}, \qquad \rho_{\text{equal }m} = \frac{2\rho_1\rho_2}{\rho_1 + \rho_2} \]
Always use total mass ÷ total volume. The equal-mass result (harmonic mean) is always the smaller one.
Symbols and assumptions: ρ₁, ρ₂ = original liquid densities (kg/m³); ρ_equal V = mixture density when equal volumes are mixed; ρ_equal m = mixture density when equal masses are mixed. Both formulas assume volumes add without contraction or expansion.
Pascal's law at one level
\[P_1=P_2\quad\text{at equal height in a connected fluid at rest}\]
P₁ and P₂ are pressures at two points, in Pa. For a small fluid wedge at one point, balancing forces and the projected face areas similarly gives equal pressure in every orientation. Pressure is not the same at every depth: deeper points generally have greater pressure.
Symbols and assumptions: P₁, P₂ = equilibrium pressures (Pa) at two points at the same height in a connected fluid. Different depths can have different pressures.
Pressure at depth h
\[ P = P_0 + \rho g h \]
\(P_0\) = pressure at the free surface (usually atmospheric, 1.013 × 10⁵ Pa), ρ = liquid density, h = vertical depth below the surface.
Symbols and assumptions: P = absolute pressure at the point (Pa); P₀ = pressure at the free surface (Pa); ρ = liquid density (kg/m³); g = gravitational acceleration (m/s²); h = vertical depth below the surface (m). The liquid is at rest and its density is uniform.
Between any two points
\[ P_2 - P_1 = \rho g (h_2 - h_1) \]
Only the vertical separation matters. Points at the same level in the same connected liquid at rest have the same pressure.
Symbols and assumptions: P₁, P₂ = pressures (Pa); h₁, h₂ = depths (m) measured downward from the same reference level; ρ = uniform density (kg/m³); g = gravitational acceleration (m/s²). A deeper point has greater h and greater P.
Force on a vertical wall of width w, liquid depth H
\[ F = \underbrace{\frac{\rho g H}{2}}_{\text{average pressure}} \times (H w) = \frac{\rho g H^2 w}{2} \]
Gauge force (the atmosphere usually acts on both sides and cancels). The resultant acts at H/3 above the base. On the base itself, F = ρgH × (base area).
Symbols and assumptions: F = net force from the water (N); ρ = liquid density (kg/m³); g = gravitational acceleration (m/s²); H = vertical wetted depth (m); w = wall width (m). Atmospheric pressure acts on both sides and cancels; water is on only one side.
Final height: conserve volume
\[A_1h_1+A_2h_2=(A_1+A_2)h_f,\qquad h_f=\frac{A_1h_1+A_2h_2}{A_1+A_2}\]
A₁, A₂ = vessel areas (m²); h₁, h₂ = initial depths (m); h_f = final common depth (m). For equal areas, h_f = (h₁ + h₂)/2. A common surface pressure and the same liquid are essential assumptions.
Symbols and assumptions: A₁, A₂ = constant vessel areas (m²); h₁, h₂ = initial depths above the common bottom (m); h_f = settled common depth (m). Both surfaces have the same imposed pressure; the pipe volume is neglected.
Work by gravity during equalisation
\[W_g=\frac{\rho g}{2}\left[A_1h_1^2+A_2h_2^2-(A_1+A_2)h_f^2\right]=\frac{\rho gA_1A_2}{2(A_1+A_2)}(h_1-h_2)^2\]
W_g = work done by gravity (J); ρ = liquid density (kg/m³); g = gravitational acceleration (m/s²). A₁, A₂, h₁, h₂ and h_f have the meanings above. This is the decrease in gravitational potential energy between the initial and settled final states, not useful work automatically delivered by a machine.
Symbols and assumptions: W_g = work by gravity (J); ρ = common liquid density (kg/m³); g = gravitational acceleration (m/s²); A₁, A₂ = vessel areas (m²); h₁, h₂ = initial depths (m); h_f = final depth (m).
Shortcut for equal-area cylinders
\[W_g=\frac{\rho gA}{4}(h_1-h_2)^2\]
A = area of each cylinder, not their combined area. This special case follows by putting A₁ = A₂ = A in the general formula. The height difference is squared; exchanging the two initial heights leaves the result unchanged.
Symbols and assumptions: W_g = work by gravity (J); ρ = liquid density (kg/m³); g = gravitational acceleration (m/s²); A = area of each cylinder (m²); h₁, h₂ = initial depths (m). Only use this form when the vessel areas are equal.
Barometer
\[ P_0 = \rho_{Hg}\, g\, h \]
At sea level h = 0.76 m: \(13600 \times 9.8 \times 0.76 = 1.013\times10^5\) Pa. With water the column would be 10.3 m tall. The height is the vertical height. It does not depend on the tube's width or tilt; tilting only makes the column longer along the tube.
Symbols and assumptions: P₀ = atmospheric pressure (Pa); ρ_Hg = mercury density (kg/m³); g = gravitational acceleration (m/s²); h = vertical height difference between mercury surfaces (m). Pressure above the closed-end column is treated as negligible.
Open-tube manometer
\[ P_{\text{gas}} = P_0 + \rho g h \qquad (h = \text{difference in liquid levels}) \]
If the open side stands higher, the gas pressure is above atmospheric (h positive). If the open side is lower, the gas pressure is below atmospheric (subtract).
Symbols and assumptions: P_gas = gas absolute pressure (Pa); P₀ = atmospheric pressure (Pa); ρ = manometer-liquid density (kg/m³); g = gravitational acceleration (m/s²); h = vertical level difference (m). The displayed plus sign applies when the open-arm surface is higher; use a minus sign when it is lower.
Force multiplication
\[ \frac{F_1}{A_1} = \frac{F_2}{A_2} \;\Rightarrow\; F_2 = F_1 \frac{A_2}{A_1} = F_1\left(\frac{d_2}{d_1}\right)^2 \]
Mechanical advantage = A₂/A₁. For round pistons, area ∝ (diameter)², so square the diameter ratio.
Symbols and assumptions: F₁ = input force (N); F₂ = balanced load force (N); A₁, A₂ = piston areas (m²); d₁, d₂ = piston diameters in matching units. These are force magnitudes due to the same added pressure; piston weight, friction and height corrections are neglected.
No free energy
\[ A_1 x_1 = A_2 x_2, \qquad F_1 x_1 = F_2 x_2 \]
The liquid volume pushed out of the small cylinder equals the volume entering the big one. The big piston rises only x₂ = x₁A₁/A₂, so work in = work out (ideal machine).
Symbols and assumptions: A₁, A₂ = piston areas (m²); x₁, x₂ = piston displacement magnitudes (m); F₁, F₂ = corresponding forces (N). A₁x₁ = A₂x₂ conserves volume, and F₁x₁ = F₂x₂ conserves work in the ideal machine.
Buoyant force
\[ F_B = \rho_l\, V_{sub}\, g \]
ρ_l = density of the liquid, V_sub = submerged volume. F_B vs V_sub is a straight line through the origin with slope ρ_l g, and it becomes flat once the body is fully under (Test-05 Q36).
Symbols and assumptions: F_B = upward buoyant force (N); ρ_l = liquid density (kg/m³); V_sub = displaced/submerged volume (m³); g = gravitational acceleration (m/s²). The submerged volume need not be the whole body volume.
Floating (equilibrium)
\[ \rho_b V g = \rho_l V_{sub} g \;\Rightarrow\; \frac{V_{sub}}{V} = \frac{\rho_b}{\rho_l} \]
Fraction submerged = density ratio. Ice (900) in water: 90% under. Wood (600) in water: 60% under.
Symbols and assumptions: ρ_b = mean body density (kg/m³); ρ_l = liquid density (kg/m³); V = total body volume (m³); V_sub = submerged volume (m³); g = gravitational acceleration (m/s²). The body floats freely with no other vertical support or force.
Apparent weight (fully immersed)
\[ W_{app} = W - F_B = W\left(1 - \frac{\rho_l}{\rho_b}\right) \]
Loss of weight = F_B. Relative density of a solid = W_air ÷ (W_air − W_water).
Symbols and assumptions: W_app = support-force magnitude/readout (N); W = true weight (N); F_B = buoyancy (N); ρ_l = liquid density; ρ_b = mean body density, both in kg/m³. A negative W − F_B means a downward restraint is needed, not a negative reading on an ordinary hanging spring balance.
Body at the interface of two liquids
\[ \rho_b V = \rho_1 V_1 + \rho_2 V_2 \]
V₁ + V₂ = V. The body's density is the volume-weighted average of the two liquids.
Symbols and assumptions: ρ_b = mean body density; ρ₁, ρ₂ = upper/lower liquid densities, all in kg/m³; V = total body volume; V₁, V₂ = volumes in the two liquids, all in m³. The displayed form assumes V₁ + V₂ = V; no part is in air.
Equation of continuity (conservation of mass)
\[ A_1 v_1 = A_2 v_2 = Q \]
Q = volume flow rate (m³ s⁻¹), constant along the pipe. Mass flow rate = ρQ (kg s⁻¹).
Symbols and assumptions: A₁, A₂ = cross-sectional areas (m²); v₁, v₂ = section-average flow speeds (m/s); Q = volume flow rate (m³/s). This form requires steady incompressible flow with no leak between the sections.
Round pipes
\[ v \propto \frac{1}{A} \propto \frac{1}{d^2}, \qquad \frac{v_1}{v_2} = \left(\frac{d_2}{d_1}\right)^2 \]
Halve the diameter → 4× the speed. Kinetic energy per volume ½ρv² ∝ 1/d⁴.
Symbols and assumptions: v = section-average speed (m/s); A = area (m²); d = diameter (m); indices 1 and 2 identify two sections. Ratios assume the same conserved volume flow rate; diameter ratios must be squared.
Branching and many holes
\[ A v = \sum_i A_i v_i, \qquad A_1 v_1 = n\,a\,v_2 \]
Flow in = total flow out. Spray pumps and shower heads: n holes, each of area a.
Symbols and assumptions: A, v = inlet area (m²) and mean speed (m/s); A_i, v_i = area and outward mean speed of outlet i; n = number of identical holes; a = area of each hole (m²); A₁, v₁ = inlet values; v₂ = speed at each identical outlet (m/s).
Bernoulli's equation (along a streamline)
\[ P + \tfrac{1}{2}\rho v^2 + \rho g h = \text{constant} \]
Each term is energy per unit volume (J m⁻³ = Pa): P = pressure energy, ½ρv² = kinetic energy, ρgh = potential energy, all per unit volume. Valid along a streamline for steady, incompressible flow with negligible viscosity and no added work or energy loss. Irrotational flow permits comparison across streamlines.
Symbols and assumptions: P = local pressure (Pa); ρ = density (kg/m³); v = speed (m/s); g = gravitational acceleration (m/s²); h = height (m) measured upward from a chosen datum. Unlike the earlier depth h, this h is an upward elevation.
Per unit mass
\[ \frac{P}{\rho} + \frac{v^2}{2} + g h = \text{const} \]
Units J kg⁻¹.
Symbols and assumptions: P = pressure (Pa); ρ = density (kg/m³); v = speed (m/s); g = gravitational acceleration (m/s²); h = upward elevation (m). Each term has units J/kg; the ideal Bernoulli assumptions still apply.
Per unit weight ("heads")
\[ \frac{P}{\rho g} + \frac{v^2}{2g} + h = \text{const} \]
Pressure head + velocity head + elevation head, each a length (m).
Symbols and assumptions: P = pressure (Pa); ρ = density (kg/m³); g = gravitational acceleration (m/s²); v = speed (m/s); h = upward elevation (m). Every term is a head in metres; a head is energy per unit weight.
Speed of efflux
\[ v = \sqrt{2 g h} \]
h = depth of the hole below the free surface. Independent of the liquid's density and the hole's size.
Symbols and assumptions: v = exit speed (m/s); g = gravitational acceleration (m/s²); h = vertical depth of the hole below the free surface (m). Both surface and exit are at the same pressure; the tank area is much larger than the hole area and losses are neglected.
Extra pressure on the surface (closed tank, piston, load)
\[ v = \sqrt{2gh + \frac{2(P - P_0)}{\rho}} \]
An extra pressure ΔP on the surface adds ΔP/ρg to the effective head (Test-05 Q35).
Symbols and assumptions: v = exit speed (m/s); h = hole depth below the free surface (m); P = pressure on the liquid surface (Pa); P₀ = pressure outside the hole (Pa); ρ = liquid density (kg/m³); g = gravitational acceleration (m/s²). A piston load M adds Mg/A to surface pressure, with M its mass and A the piston area.
Horizontal range on the ground
\[ R = v\sqrt{\frac{2y}{g}} = 2\sqrt{h\,y} = 2\sqrt{h(H-h)} \]
h = depth below surface, y = height of hole above the ground, H = h + y (liquid surface above the ground). Maximum R = H when h = y = H/2. Holes at depth h and at depth H − h give the same range.
Symbols and assumptions: R = horizontal range (m); v = horizontal exit speed (m/s); y = hole height above the landing level (m); h = hole depth below the liquid surface (m); H = surface height above the landing level (m), so y = H − h; g = gravitational acceleration (m/s²). Air resistance and changes in surface height during flight are neglected.
Time to empty (hole at the bottom)
\[ t = \frac{A}{a}\sqrt{\frac{2H}{g}} \;\propto\; \sqrt{H} \]
A = tank area, a = hole area. From H₁ to H₂: \( t = \frac{A}{a}\sqrt{\frac{2}{g}}\,(\sqrt{H_1} - \sqrt{H_2}) \). The first half of the height drains in only 29% of the total time.
Symbols and assumptions: t = emptying time (s); A = constant tank cross-sectional area (m²); a = hole area (m²); H = initial water depth (m); g = gravitational acceleration (m/s²). The hole is small compared with the tank and viscous/contraction losses are neglected.
Thrust (reaction) of the jet
\[ F = \rho a v^2 = 2\rho a g h \]
Momentum per second carried out by the jet. This is twice the hydrostatic force ρgh·a on a plugged hole. Two holes on opposite walls at depths h₁, h₂: net force 2ρag(h₂ − h₁) (Q40).
Symbols and assumptions: F = horizontal reaction-force magnitude (N); ρ = liquid density (kg/m³); a = hole area (m²); v = exit speed (m/s); g = gravitational acceleration (m/s²); h = hole depth below the surface (m). The second form uses v² = 2gh.
Two equations, two unknowns
\[ A_1v_1 = A_2v_2, \qquad P_1 - P_2 = \tfrac12\rho\,(v_2^2 - v_1^2) \]
Substitute v₂ = v₁A₁/A₂ into Bernoulli and solve for v₁.
Symbols and assumptions: A₁, A₂ = pipe areas (m²); v₁, v₂ = flow speeds (m/s); P₁, P₂ = pressures (Pa); ρ = liquid density (kg/m³). The sections are horizontal and the flow is ideal, steady and incompressible.
Venturimeter result
\[ v_1 = \sqrt{\frac{2\,\Delta P}{\rho\left[(A_1/A_2)^2 - 1\right]}}, \qquad Q = A_1 v_1 = A_1A_2\sqrt{\frac{2\,\Delta P}{\rho\,(A_1^2 - A_2^2)}} \]
ΔP = P₁ − P₂. With vertical tubes of the same liquid, ΔP = ρgΔh. With a U-tube manometer holding a heavier liquid ρ_m (e.g. mercury under water), ΔP = (ρ_m − ρ)gh.
Symbols and assumptions: v₁ = speed in the wider section (m/s); A₁ = wider area and A₂ = throat area (m²), with A₁ > A₂; ΔP = P₁ − P₂ (Pa), positive; ρ = liquid density (kg/m³); Q = volume flow rate (m³/s). The pressure difference is measured at equal elevation.
Uniform-pressure approximation for a wing
\[P_\text{bottom}-P_\text{top}=\frac12\rho(v_\text{top}^{2}-v_\text{bottom}^{2}),\qquad F_\text{lift}=\Delta P\,A\]
ρ is the air density; A is the effective wing area. Positive F means upward lift. A real wing has a varying pressure distribution, so its total force is found by adding pressure forces over the surface.
Symbols and assumptions: P_bottom, P_top = air pressures below/above the wing (Pa); v_top, v_bottom = air speeds relative to the wing (m/s); ρ = air density (kg/m³); ΔP = P_bottom − P_top; A = effective wing area (m²); F_lift = signed upward lift (N).
Horizontal acceleration
\[\tan\theta=\frac{|a|}{g},\qquad |\Delta h|=\frac{|a|\ell}{g}\]
θ is the tilt from horizontal; ℓ is the horizontal tank length. The rear side is higher, assuming enough liquid and no spilling.
Symbols and assumptions: a = signed horizontal tank acceleration (m/s²); g = gravitational acceleration (m/s²); θ = free-surface tilt from horizontal; ℓ = horizontal tank length (m); Δh = surface-height difference across that length (m). Absolute values give magnitudes.
Vertical acceleration
\[P-P_0=\rho(g+a)h,\qquad F_B=\rho_\ell V_\text{sub}(g+a)\]
Here a is signed positive upward. For downward acceleration of magnitude a, replace g+a with g−a.
Symbols and assumptions: P = absolute pressure at depth h (Pa); P₀ = surface pressure (Pa); ρ or ρ_l = liquid density (kg/m³); g = gravitational acceleration (m/s²); a = signed lift acceleration, positive upward (m/s²); h = vertical depth (m); F_B = buoyancy (N); V_sub = submerged volume (m³).

Fluids II · Viscosity & Surface Tension

Newton's law of viscosity
\[ F = -\,\eta A \frac{dv}{dx} \]
\(\eta\) (eta) = coefficient of viscosity. The minus sign says the force opposes the relative motion. For a liquid film of thickness \(d\) under a plate moving at \(v\): \(F = \eta A v/d\).
Symbols and assumptions: F = signed viscous force (N), opposing relative layer motion; η = dynamic viscosity (Pa·s); A = sheared area (m²); v = flow velocity (m/s); x = distance across layers (m); dv/dx = velocity gradient (s⁻¹). Use |F| = ηA|dv/dx| for the magnitude.
Velocity gradient
\[ \frac{dv}{dx} = \frac{v}{d} \;\;(\text{uniform film}) \]
Change in speed per unit distance across the layers. Unit s⁻¹. It is also the rate of shear strain.
Symbols and assumptions: v = moving-plate speed relative to the fixed plate (m/s); d = film thickness (m); x = perpendicular distance through the film (m); dv/dx = velocity gradient (s⁻¹). The v/d form assumes a linear velocity profile and no slip.
Units & dimensions of η
\[ \eta = \frac{F/A}{v/d} \;\Rightarrow\; [\mathrm{M\,L^{-1}\,T^{-1}}] \]
SI: N s m⁻² = Pa·s (also called poiseuille, PI). CGS: poise. 1 poise = 0.1 Pa·s (so 1 Pa·s = 10 poise).
Symbols and assumptions: η = viscosity (Pa·s); F = viscous-force magnitude (N); A = plate area (m²); v = relative plate speed (m/s); d = film thickness (m). M, L and T in the dimensional expression mean mass, length and time.
Volume flow rate
\[Q=\frac{\pi r^4\Delta P}{8\eta\ell}\]
Q in m³/s, r and ℓ in m, ΔP in Pa and η in Pa·s. Flow resistance is \(\Delta P/Q=8\eta\ell/(\pi r^4)\).
Symbols and assumptions: Q = volume flow rate (m³/s); r = inner pipe radius (m); ΔP = pressure drop (Pa); η = viscosity (Pa·s); ℓ = pipe length (m). The pipe is horizontal, circular, straight and has fully developed laminar flow with no slip.
Velocity profile
\[v(s)=\frac{\Delta P}{4\eta\ell}(r^2-s^2),\quad v_\text{max}=2\bar v\]
The area-averaged speed is \(\bar v=Q/(\pi r^2)\). Zero at the wall; maximum at s = 0.
Symbols and assumptions: v(s) = axial speed (m/s) at radial distance s from the axis (m); r = pipe radius (m); ΔP = pressure drop (Pa); η = viscosity (Pa·s); ℓ = pipe length (m); v_max = axis speed; v̄ = section-average speed. At s = r, v = 0.
Stokes' law
\[ F_v = 6\pi\eta r v \]
r = radius of sphere, v = its speed relative to the fluid. Drag ∝ η, ∝ r, ∝ v. Valid for small spheres at low speed (laminar flow).
Symbols and assumptions: F_v = drag magnitude (N); η = liquid viscosity (Pa·s); r = sphere radius (m); v = sphere speed relative to the liquid (m/s). Drag opposes that relative motion. The sphere is rigid and sufficiently far from walls; the Reynolds number must be very small.
Terminal velocity
\[ v_t = \frac{2 r^2 (\rho - \sigma) g}{9\eta} \]
ρ = density of the sphere, σ = density of the fluid. If σ > ρ, \(v_t\) is negative: the body rises (air bubble).
Symbols and assumptions: v_t = terminal settling speed (m/s); r = sphere radius (m); ρ = sphere density; σ = liquid density, both in kg/m³; g = gravitational acceleration (m/s²); η = viscosity (Pa·s). Here σ is a density, not stress or Poisson’s ratio. A negative result indicates upward motion; Stokes-flow assumptions must hold.
At terminal velocity (Q45)
\[ F_v = W - B = \tfrac43\pi r^3(\rho-\sigma)g = Mg\left(1-\tfrac{\sigma}{\rho}\right) \]
If σ = ρ/2, the viscous force is Mg/2.
Symbols and assumptions: F_v = upward viscous drag (N); W = weight (N); B = buoyant force (N), not bulk modulus; M = sphere mass (kg); r = sphere radius (m); ρ = sphere density; σ = liquid density (kg/m³); g = gravitational acceleration (m/s²). This form describes a sphere settling downward.
n drops coalesce (Q32)
\[ R = n^{1/3}r,\quad v' = n^{2/3}\,v \]
Volume adds, so \(R^3 = nr^3\). Breaking one sphere into n equal drops: each has \(v/n^{2/3}\) (Q40: 27 pieces → speed ÷ 9).
Symbols and assumptions: n = number of identical initial drops; r = each small radius; R = merged radius, in matching units; v = initial terminal speed; v′ = merged terminal speed (m/s). Densities and viscosity stay unchanged; both speeds must be in the Stokes regime.
Rising air bubble (Q41)
\[ v = \frac{2r^2\sigma g}{9\eta} \;\Rightarrow\; \eta = \frac{2r^2\sigma g}{9v} \]
Air density ≈ 0 compared with the liquid density σ, so \(\rho - \sigma \approx -\sigma\): the bubble moves up.
Symbols and assumptions: v = steady upward speed (m/s); r = bubble radius (m); σ = solution density (kg/m³); g = gravitational acceleration (m/s²); η = solution viscosity (Pa·s). Air weight is neglected and the school model assumes Stokes-type drag; a clean mobile bubble interface can behave differently.
Reynolds number
\[ R_e = \frac{\rho v d}{\eta} = \text{inertia/viscosity scale} \]
ρ = fluid density, v = flow speed, d = pipe diameter, η = viscosity. Dimensionless (no unit). Approximate ranges used in your coaching test: \(R_e \lt 1000\) laminar · 1000–2000 unsteady · \(R_e \gt 2000\) turbulent.
Symbols and assumptions: R_e = Reynolds number, dimensionless; ρ = fluid density (kg/m³); v = mean pipe speed (m/s); d = pipe diameter (m); η = viscosity (Pa·s). The force ratio represents a characteristic scaling, not a universal equality for every geometry.
Surface tension
\[ T = \frac{F}{L} \]
Unit N m⁻¹ (= J m⁻²). Dimensions [M T⁻²]. Water at 20 °C: ≈ 0.072 N/m. Soap solution: ≈ 0.03 N/m. Mercury: ≈ 0.47 N/m.
Symbols and assumptions: T = surface tension (N/m); F = tangential force (N) across a line of length L (m) on one liquid surface. The line lies in the surface; the force is perpendicular to that line. T here is not temperature.
Film on a frame (two surfaces)
\[ F = 2TL \]
A soap film has a front and a back surface, so a slider of length L is pulled by both: \(T L + T L\).
Symbols and assumptions: F = force pulling the movable wire (N); T = surface tension (N/m); L = wire length in contact with one face of the film (m). Both faces contribute, giving the factor 2.
Film (two surfaces)
\[ W = T \cdot 2\,\Delta A \]
ΔA = change in area of the film as you see it (one face).
Symbols and assumptions: W = work to increase surface area (J); T = constant surface tension (N/m); ΔA = increase in area of one face (m²). The total new area is 2ΔA.
Soap bubble, r₁ → r₂
\[ W = 8\pi T\,(r_2^2 - r_1^2) \]
Two surfaces, each \(4\pi r^2\). Blowing from zero: \(8\pi r^2T\).
Symbols and assumptions: W = increase in surface energy (J); T = surface tension (N/m); r₁, r₂ = initial/final bubble radii (m). This counts two surfaces of a thin soap film and assumes T stays constant.
Liquid drop (one surface)
\[ E = 4\pi r^2 T \]
A drop has only its outer surface.
Symbols and assumptions: E = surface energy (J); r = drop radius (m); T = surface tension (N/m). The drop has area 4πr² and one liquid–air interface; creating extra area increases E.
n small drops → one big drop
\[ \Delta E = 4\pi r^2 T\, n\,(1 - n^{-1/3}) = 4\pi R^2 T\,(n^{1/3}-1) \]
Volume conserved, \(R = n^{1/3} r\). Energy is released (as heat). Splitting R into n drops needs this much work.
Symbols and assumptions: ΔE = positive energy released (J); n = number of equal small drops; r = each small radius (m); R = merged radius (m); T = surface tension (N/m). Volume conservation gives R³ = nr³; surface area decreases.
Balance of surface tensions at the contact line (NCERT)
\[ S_{la}\cos\theta + S_{sl} = S_{sa} \;\Rightarrow\; \cos\theta = \frac{S_{sa}-S_{sl}}{S_{la}} \]
l = liquid, s = solid, a = air. If \(S_{sl} \gt S_{sa}\), cos θ < 0, so θ is obtuse (does not wet, e.g. water–leaf). If \(S_{sl} \lt S_{sa}\), θ is acute (wets, e.g. water–glass).
Symbols and assumptions: S_la = liquid–air surface tension; S_sl = solid–liquid interfacial energy per area; S_sa = solid–air interfacial energy per area, all in N/m = J/m². θ = equilibrium contact angle measured through the liquid. This balance assumes a smooth equilibrium contact line.
Liquid drop
\[ P_{in}-P_{out} = \frac{2T}{r} \]
One surface (outer).
Symbols and assumptions: P_in, P_out = pressures inside/outside the drop (Pa); T = surface tension (N/m); r = spherical drop radius (m). Their difference is the excess pressure, not the full inside pressure.
Soap bubble in air
\[ P_{in}-P_{out} = \frac{4T}{r} \]
Two surfaces (outer and inner).
Symbols and assumptions: P_in, P_out = inside/outside pressures (Pa); T = surface tension (N/m); r = bubble radius (m). A thin film has two surfaces, giving twice the drop’s excess pressure.
Air bubble at depth h in a liquid
\[ P_{in} = P_0 + \rho g h + \frac{2T}{r} \]
One surface. P₀ = atmospheric pressure on the liquid.
Symbols and assumptions: P_in = bubble inside pressure (Pa); P₀ = pressure on the liquid free surface (Pa); ρ = surrounding liquid density (kg/m³); g = gravitational acceleration (m/s²); h = bubble depth (m); T = liquid–air surface tension (N/m); r = bubble radius (m).
Common interface of two stuck bubbles
\[ r = \frac{r_1 r_2}{r_2 - r_1} \]
From \(\frac{4T}{r_1} - \frac{4T}{r_2} = \frac{4T}{r}\) (r₁ < r₂). The interface bulges into the bigger bubble.
Symbols and assumptions: r = radius of curvature of the common film (m); r₁, r₂ = radii of the two bubbles (m), with r₁ < r₂; T = soap-film surface tension (N/m). For equal bubble radii, the separating film is flat, corresponding to infinite r.
Moles of air in a bubble
\[ n = \frac{PV}{RT_K} = \frac{\left(P_0 + \frac{4T}{r}\right)\frac43\pi r^3}{RT_K} \]
Use the inside pressure (Q18). \(T_K\) = absolute temperature. If two bubbles merge isothermally in vacuum (P₀ = 0): \(R^2 = r_1^2 + r_2^2\).
Symbols and assumptions: n = amount of gas (mol); P = absolute inside pressure (Pa); V = gas volume (m³); R in PV/(RT_K) = universal gas constant 8.314 J/(mol·K); T_K = gas temperature (K); T = surface tension (N/m); r = bubble radius (m); P₀ = outside pressure (Pa). In the merger result, R instead denotes the final bubble radius: read each R in its local context.
Capillary rise (Jurin's law)
\[ h = \frac{2T\cos\theta}{r\rho g} \]
r = inner radius. θ > 90° gives h < 0 (fall, e.g. mercury). \(h \propto 1/r\), so \(hr\) = constant for a given liquid.
Symbols and assumptions: h = signed vertical rise above the outside liquid level (m); T = surface tension (N/m); θ = contact angle through the liquid; r = tube inner radius (m); ρ = liquid density (kg/m³); g = gravitational acceleration (m/s²). Negative h means capillary depression.
Force method: weight held up
\[ \pi r^2 h\rho g = 2\pi r T\cos\theta \]
Surface tension pulls up all round the rim. The weight of the column is \(2\pi rT\cos\theta\): independent of h, and ∝ r. So the mass raised ∝ r.
Symbols and assumptions: r = inner tube radius (m); h = vertical rise (m); ρ = liquid density (kg/m³); g = gravitational acceleration (m/s²); T = surface tension (N/m); θ = contact angle through the liquid. The left side is the raised column’s weight; the right is the upward component of force around its rim.
Measuring T by capillary rise
\[ T = \frac{r h \rho g}{2\cos\theta} \;\xrightarrow{\theta\,\approx\,0}\; \frac{rh\rho g}{2} \]
Measure h with a travelling microscope and the bore radius r. A finer version adds the meniscus volume: \(T = r(h + r/3)\rho g/2\).
Symbols and assumptions: T = inferred surface tension (N/m); r = measured inner radius (m); h = vertical rise to the meniscus bottom (m); ρ = liquid density (kg/m³); g = gravitational acceleration (m/s²); θ = contact angle. The h + r/3 correction assumes a nearly hemispherical meniscus at θ ≈ 0.
School-level capillary-rise measurement
\[T=\frac{\rho grh}{2\cos\theta}\approx\frac{\rho grh}{2}\quad(\theta\approx0)\]
Measure h vertically from the outside free surface to the bottom of the concave meniscus. Use the bore radius, not the wall thickness or outer radius. This approximation neglects the small meniscus-volume correction.
Symbols and assumptions: T = inferred surface tension (N/m); ρ = liquid density (kg/m³); g = gravitational acceleration (m/s²); r = inner tube radius (m); h = vertical rise above the outside liquid surface (m); θ = contact angle measured through the liquid.

Symbols and assumptions: p pressure (Pa), V volume (m³), T temperature (K), γ=C_P/C_V dimensionless; W work by gas (J), U gas internal energies (J); i/f initial/final. First three require a reversible adiabatic ideal gas with constant γ. The last requires a rigid insulated combined system without other energy exchanges.

Cycles, heat engines and efficiency
\[ \Delta U_{\rm cycle}=0,\quad W=Q_h-Q_c,\quad \eta=\frac{W}{Q_h}=1-\frac{Q_c}{Q_h} \]
Symbols and assumptions: W net cycle work (J); Q_h positive heat absorbed from hot source (J); Q_c positive magnitude rejected to cold sink (J); η dimensionless efficiency. The engine returns to its starting state after each cycle.

Second law, Carnot limits and refrigerators
\[ \eta_C=1-\frac{T_c}{T_h},\qquad COP=\frac{Q_c}{W},\qquad COP_C=\frac{T_c}{T_h-T_c} \]
Symbols and assumptions: T_h/T_c hot/cold reservoir temperatures (K), T_h>T_c>0; η_C maximum reversible engine efficiency; Q_c heat removed from cold reservoir (J); W input work (J); COP coefficient of performance; COP_C reversible refrigerator limit. Kelvin is essential.

1 Basics

Models, laws and sensible approximations

Physics describes observations with quantities that can be measured. A law summarises a reproducible relationship; a model keeps the features needed for a particular question. A particle model ignores the size of an object when its dimensions do not affect the motion being studied.

The same ball can be a particle in projectile motion and an extended body when its spin matters. State the system, reference frame and assumptions before choosing an equation. After calculating, check units, limiting cases and whether the magnitude is physically sensible.

Models, laws and sensible approximations
\[ \text{prediction}=\text{model}(\text{measured inputs}) \]
Symbols and assumptions: Prediction means the observable being calculated; model means a stated physical relationship; measured inputs carry units and uncertainty. This is a workflow, not an algebraic force law.
Watch the assumption

A more detailed model is useful only when the neglected effect matters to the question.

Worked example

A ball travels 30 m while its diameter is 0.06 m. Is a particle model reasonable for its trajectory?

Show solution

The diameter is 0.002 times the travel distance. If rotation and air drag are neglected, tracking the centre is reasonable. For rolling down a slope, rotational energy must also be included.

Worked example · Concept

An Atwood machine with masses \(m_1\gt m_2\) has acceleration \(a=\dfrac{(m_1-m_2)g}{m_1+m_2}\). Check this result using two limiting cases.

Show solution
  1. Let \(m_2=0\). Then \(a=m_1g/m_1=g\). With nothing on the other side, \(m_1\) falls freely. This matches.
  2. Let \(m_1=m_2\). Then \(a=0\). Equal masses balance. This matches.
  3. Let \(m_1\gg m_2\). Then \(a\to g\), never more than \(g\). A pulley system cannot make a mass fall faster than free fall. This matches.

Answer: The formula passes every limiting check, so it is likely correct.

More solved problem patterns
Worked example · Concept

A rope pulls a box across the floor. Name the fundamental force behind (a) the tension in the rope, (b) the friction on the box and (c) the weight of the box.

Show solution
  1. Tension is the pull between neighbouring atoms of the stretched rope. Atomic bonds are electric, so tension is electromagnetic.
  2. Friction comes from the interaction of atoms at the two surfaces. It is also electromagnetic.
  3. Weight is the pull of the Earth on the mass of the box. It is gravitational.

Answer: (a) electromagnetic (b) electromagnetic (c) gravitational

Worked example · Assertion–Reason

Assertion: The Earth can be treated as a particle when finding its orbit around the Sun. Reason: The Earth's radius is very small compared with its distance from the Sun.

Show solution
  1. Earth's radius is about \(6.4\times10^{6}\) m. The Earth–Sun distance is about \(1.5\times10^{11}\) m.
  2. The ratio is about \(4\times10^{-5}\), so the size has almost no effect on the orbit. The assertion is true.
  3. The reason is true and is exactly why the particle model works.

Answer: Both A and R are true, and R correctly explains A.

The four fundamental forces

Every force you meet in mechanics comes from one of four fundamental interactions. NCERT compares their strengths relative to the strong force.

ForceRelative strengthRangeActs between
Strong nuclear1Short, about \(10^{-15}\) m (nuclear size)Nucleons and heavier elementary particles
Electromagnetic\(10^{-2}\)InfiniteCharged particles
Weak nuclear\(10^{-13}\)Very short, about \(10^{-16}\) mSome elementary particles, for example electron and neutrino (beta decay)
Gravitational\(10^{-39}\)InfiniteAll objects with mass

Contact forces such as friction, normal reaction, tension and the spring force are all electromagnetic. They come from the electric forces between the atoms of the surfaces in contact. Gravity is the weakest force, yet it rules the motion of planets because large bodies are electrically neutral overall and gravity is always attractive.

Conservation laws and symmetry

Physics rests on a few conservation laws: energy, linear momentum, angular momentum and electric charge. Each one links to a symmetry of nature.

  • Laws are the same at every place (homogeneity of space) → linear momentum is conserved.
  • Laws are the same in every direction (isotropy of space) → angular momentum is conserved.
  • Laws are the same at every time (homogeneity of time) → energy is conserved.

A conservation law tells you what stays fixed. It does not tell you how fast a process happens.

Checking a model before trusting it

After any calculation, run three quick checks.

  1. Units: both sides must have the same dimensions.
  2. Limiting cases: set one input to zero or to a very large value. The result must match a case you already know.
  3. Size: the magnitude must be sensible. A walking speed of 300 m/s signals an error.

A model is good when the effects it ignores are small compared with the effect being asked about.

Question patterns
  • "Arrange the fundamental forces in increasing order of strength." Answer: gravitational < weak < electromagnetic < strong.
  • "Which force is responsible for friction / tension / normal reaction?" Answer: electromagnetic.
  • "Which force has the shortest range?" Answer: weak nuclear.
  • Statement-type questions linking conservation laws with symmetries.
  • "Can this body be treated as a particle?" Compare its size with the distance or scale of the motion.
Another common mistake

Friction, tension and normal reaction are electromagnetic in origin, not "mechanical" or gravitational. Gravity only supplies the weight.

Another common mistake

Weak nuclear force is far stronger than gravity (\(10^{-13}\) against \(10^{-39}\)). Its range is tiny, so it does not act over everyday distances.

Check the idea

2 Core

Proportionality, powers and scaling

A proportionality states how one quantity changes when the others are fixed. If a quantity depends on a square, doubling the input multiplies the output by four. Inverse dependence means the output decreases when that input increases.

Write the complete dependence before taking a ratio: constants cancel only if they really remain unchanged. This is the basis of many NEET comparison questions in gravitation, elasticity, heat and oscillations.

Proportionality, powers and scaling
\[ Q=Cx^a y^b,\qquad \frac{Q_2}{Q_1}=\left(\frac{x_2}{x_1}\right)^a\left(\frac{y_2}{y_1}\right)^b \]
Symbols and assumptions: Q is the dependent quantity; C is a constant with the required units; x and y are inputs; a and b are dimensionless exponents. Subscripts 1 and 2 denote two cases with the same C.
Other useful formulas
Small percentage changes
\[ \frac{\Delta Q}{Q}\times100\approx a\left(\frac{\Delta x}{x}\times100\right)+b\left(\frac{\Delta y}{y}\times100\right) \]
Symbols and assumptions: Q = Cxᵃyᵇ is the dependent quantity; Δx/x and Δy/y are small fractional changes (below about 5%); a and b are the powers with their signs. For large changes use the exact factor (1 + Δx/x)ᵃ.
Kinetic energy and momentum
\[ K=\frac{p^2}{2m},\qquad p=\sqrt{2mK} \]
Symbols and assumptions: K is kinetic energy (J), p is momentum (kg m/s), m is mass (kg). Valid for any non-relativistic moving body.
Watch the assumption

An increase of 100% means doubling; it does not mean multiplication by 100.

Worked example

For a fixed spring constant, a spring period varies as the square root of mass. What happens if mass becomes nine times larger?

Show solution

Using \(T\propto\sqrt m\), the period ratio is \(\sqrt9=3\). The mass increases ninefold but the period only triples.

Worked example · Ratio

Two bodies of masses in the ratio 1 : 4 have equal kinetic energies. Find the ratio of their momenta.

Show solution
  1. At fixed \(K\), \(p=\sqrt{2mK}\propto\sqrt m\).
  2. \(\dfrac{p_1}{p_2}=\sqrt{\dfrac{m_1}{m_2}}=\sqrt{\dfrac14}=\dfrac12\).
  3. The heavier body has more momentum even though both have the same energy.

Answer: 1 : 2

More solved problem patterns
Worked example · Numerical

The kinetic energy of a body increases by 44%. By what percentage does its momentum increase?

Show solution
  1. Mass is unchanged, so \(p\propto\sqrt K\).
  2. \(K_2=1.44K_1\Rightarrow p_2=\sqrt{1.44}\,p_1=1.2\,p_1\).
  3. The momentum rises by 20%. The small-change rule (half of 44% = 22%) is not accurate for such a large change.

Answer: 20%

Worked example · Graph

For planets, \(T^2\propto r^3\). A student plots \(\log T\) against \(\log r\). What are the shape and slope of the graph?

Show solution
  1. Write \(T=Cr^{3/2}\).
  2. Take logs: \(\log T=\tfrac32\log r+\log C\).
  3. This is \(y=mx+c\) with \(y=\log T\) and \(x=\log r\).

Answer: A straight line of slope 3/2 with intercept \(\log C\).

Small changes: the percentage rule

If \(Q\propto x^a y^b\) and each input changes by a small percentage, then

\[\%\ \text{change in } Q \approx a\,(\%\ \text{change in } x) + b\,(\%\ \text{change in } y).\]

This comes from the binomial approximation \((1+\varepsilon)^a\approx1+a\varepsilon\). It works well below about 5%. For large changes, use the exact factor. For example, a 10% rise in radius raises area by exactly \(1.1^2-1=21\%\), close to the approximate 20%. A 50% rise gives exactly 125%, far from the approximate 100%.

Read what is held constant

The same two quantities can scale differently depending on what is fixed. Kinetic energy and momentum are linked by \(K=\dfrac{p^2}{2m}\).

  • Same momentum: \(K\propto \dfrac1m\). The lighter body has more kinetic energy.
  • Same kinetic energy: \(p=\sqrt{2mK}\propto\sqrt m\). The heavier body has more momentum.
  • Same mass: \(K\propto p^2\). If \(p\) doubles, \(K\) becomes four times.

Before taking a ratio, write the formula in terms of the quantities that change, with the fixed ones gathered into the constant.

Graph shapes reveal the power

  • \(y\propto x\): straight line through the origin.
  • \(y\propto x^2\): parabola. Plot \(y\) against \(x^2\) to get a straight line.
  • \(y\propto 1/x\): rectangular hyperbola. Plot \(y\) against \(1/x\) to get a straight line.
  • \(y=Cx^n\): plot \(\log y\) against \(\log x\). The line has slope \(n\) and intercept \(\log C\).
Question patterns
  • "If the momentum increases by 50%, the kinetic energy increases by …" (exact factor needed).
  • "Two bodies of masses in the ratio 1 : 4 have equal kinetic energies. Ratio of their momenta?"
  • "A quantity varies as \(x^3/y^2\). Errors / changes of 1% and 2% are made …" (small-change rule).
  • Graph-match questions: which plot gives a straight line for a given law.
Another common mistake

Percentage changes multiply, they do not add. If \(x\) rises 20% and \(y\) rises 20% in \(Q=xy\), the exact change is \(1.2\times1.2=1.44\), a 44% rise, not 40%.

Another common mistake

When comparing kinetic energy and momentum of two bodies, check whether the question fixes \(p\), \(K\) or \(m\). The answer reverses between "same momentum" and "same energy".

Check the idea

3 Basics

Trigonometry and small angles

Sine, cosine and tangent connect a direction with perpendicular components. Draw the right triangle and identify the side opposite the chosen angle. Changing which axis the angle is measured from exchanges sine and cosine.

Angles in calculus and small-angle approximations must be in radians. The approximation sin θ ≈ θ comes from the near-straight behaviour of the sine curve at zero; it is useful for pendulums but not for large swings.

Trigonometry and small angles
\[ \sin\theta=\frac{\text{opposite}}{\text{hypotenuse}},\quad \cos\theta=\frac{\text{adjacent}}{\text{hypotenuse}},\quad \theta_{\rm rad}=\theta_{\rm deg}\frac{\pi}{180} \]
Symbols and assumptions: θ is the angle; triangle lengths must use the same unit; trigonometric ratios are dimensionless. For |θ| ≪ 1 rad, sin θ ≈ tan θ ≈ θ and cos θ ≈ 1−θ²/2.
Other useful formulas
Double angle and compound angle
\[ \sin2\theta=2\sin\theta\cos\theta,\quad \sin(A\pm B)=\sin A\cos B\pm\cos A\sin B \]
Symbols and assumptions: θ, A and B are angles in degrees or radians; the identities hold for all angles.
Arc length and small angles
\[ s=r\theta,\qquad \sin\theta\approx\tan\theta\approx\theta,\quad \cos\theta\approx1-\frac{\theta^2}{2} \]
Symbols and assumptions: s is arc length and r is radius in the same unit (m); θ is in radians. The approximations need |θ| ≪ 1 rad (below about 0.2 rad for 1% accuracy).
Watch the assumption

Do not substitute a degree value directly for θ in sin θ ≈ θ.

Worked example

An incline rises 3 m for every 4 m horizontally. Find sin θ and cos θ.

Show solution

The sloping length is 5 m by Pythagoras. Thus sin θ = 3/5 and cos θ = 4/5. The slope angle is measured from the horizontal.

Worked example · Numerical

An incline has \(\tan\theta=3/4\). Find \(\sin2\theta\) and \(\cos2\theta\).

Show solution
  1. From the 3–4–5 triangle, \(\sin\theta=3/5\) and \(\cos\theta=4/5\).
  2. \(\sin2\theta=2\times\tfrac35\times\tfrac45=\tfrac{24}{25}\).
  3. \(\cos2\theta=\cos^2\theta-\sin^2\theta=\tfrac{16}{25}-\tfrac{9}{25}=\tfrac{7}{25}\).

Answer: \(\sin2\theta=24/25\), \(\cos2\theta=7/25\)

More solved problem patterns
Worked example · Numerical

A 10 N force acts at 120° to the positive x-axis. Find its components.

Show solution
  1. \(F_x=10\cos120^\circ=10\times(-\tfrac12)=-5\) N. The cosine is negative in the second quadrant.
  2. \(F_y=10\sin120^\circ=10\times\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\approx8.66\) N.
  3. Check: \(\sqrt{5^2+8.66^2}=10\) N.

Answer: \(F_x=-5\) N, \(F_y\approx8.66\) N

Worked example · Numerical

A 1.0 m pendulum swings to 0.1 rad from the vertical. By how much does the bob rise?

Show solution
  1. The rise is \(h=\ell(1-\cos\theta)\).
  2. With \(\cos\theta\approx1-\theta^2/2\), \(h\approx\ell\theta^2/2=1.0\times0.01/2=0.005\) m.
  3. The exact value is 0.004996 m, so the approximation is excellent.

Answer: About 5 mm

The 37° and 53° values come from the 3–4–5 triangle and are approximations that coaching tests use freely. Key identities:

  • \(\sin^2\theta+\cos^2\theta=1\)
  • \(\sin2\theta=2\sin\theta\cos\theta\) (used in projectile range)
  • \(\cos2\theta=1-2\sin^2\theta=2\cos^2\theta-1\)
  • \(\sin(90^\circ-\theta)=\cos\theta\), \(\sin(180^\circ-\theta)=\sin\theta\), \(\cos(180^\circ-\theta)=-\cos\theta\)

Radians and small angles

An angle in radians is arc length divided by radius: \(\theta=s/r\). A full turn is \(2\pi\) rad, so \(1\ \text{rad}\approx57.3^\circ\).

For a small angle the arc and the chord are nearly equal, so \(\sin\theta\approx\tan\theta\approx\theta\). The cosine is flat at zero, so \(\cos\theta\approx1-\theta^2/2\). At 10° (0.1745 rad), \(\sin\theta=0.1736\): the error is only 0.5%. These approximations drive the simple pendulum, parallax and the angular size of distant objects.

Question patterns
  • Direct value questions: \(\sin37^\circ\), \(\cos120^\circ\), \(\sin2\theta\) given \(\tan\theta\).
  • Components of a force or velocity at an angle above 90°.
  • Small-angle estimates: rise of a pendulum bob, angular size, \(\sin\theta\approx\theta\).
  • Match-the-column on standard values.
Another common mistake

\(\tan37^\circ=0.754\), not exactly 3/4. Use 3/5 and 4/5 only when the question says so or the options are clearly rounded.

Another common mistake

\(\cos(180^\circ-\theta)=-\cos\theta\). A force at 120° to the x-axis has a negative x-component.

4 Exam

Binomial approximation and small changes

When a number is close to 1, its powers are easy to estimate. For a small dimensionless \(x\), \((1+x)^n\approx1+nx\). This works for any power \(n\): positive, negative or fractional.

Rewrite the expression as a power of one plus a small fraction. Keep the first-order change only when the fraction is much smaller than one, and check whether the question needs a more accurate result.

Binomial approximation and small changes
\[ (1+x)^n\approx1+nx\qquad(|x|\ll1) \]
Symbols and assumptions: x is a small dimensionless number (often h/R, Δℓ/ℓ or v/c); n is any real exponent. The first neglected term is n(n − 1)x²/2, so the error is tiny when |x| is below about 0.05.
Other useful formulas
Two small factors together
\[ (1+x)^m(1+y)^n\approx1+mx+ny \]
Symbols and assumptions: x and y are small dimensionless numbers; m and n are real powers. The neglected terms are of order x², y² and xy.
g at small height
\[ g_h=\frac{g}{(1+h/R)^2}\approx g\left(1-\frac{2h}{R}\right) \]
Symbols and assumptions: g is surface value (m/s²), h is height above the surface (m), R is Earth’s radius (m). Valid for h ≪ R.
Watch the assumption

You cannot apply the rule to \((R+h)^{-2}\) directly. Factor out \(R\) first: \((R+h)^{-2}=R^{-2}(1+h/R)^{-2}\).

Worked example

Find \(g\) at a height of 32 km above Earth. Take Earth's radius as 6400 km.

Show solution

\(g_h=g(1+h/R)^{-2}\approx g(1-2h/R)\). Here \(h/R=32/6400=0.005\), so \(g_h\approx g(1-0.01)=0.99g\). The exact value is \(0.9901g\).

Worked example · Numerical

The length of a simple pendulum increases by 2%. By what percentage does its period change?

Show solution
  1. \(T\propto\ell^{1/2}\), so \(T_2/T_1=(1.02)^{1/2}\).
  2. \((1+0.02)^{1/2}\approx1+\tfrac12(0.02)=1.01\).
  3. The period increases by about 1%.

Answer: About 1% increase

More solved problem patterns
Worked example · Ratio

The decrease in \(g\) at a small height \(h\) equals the decrease at depth \(d\). Find \(d\) in terms of \(h\). (At depth \(d\), \(g_d=g(1-d/R)\) exactly.)

Show solution
  1. At height: \(g_h\approx g(1-2h/R)\), so the decrease is \(2gh/R\).
  2. At depth: the decrease is \(gd/R\).
  3. Equate: \(gd/R=2gh/R\Rightarrow d=2h\).

Answer: \(d=2h\)

Worked example · Numerical

Estimate \(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{0.98}}\).

Show solution
  1. Write it as \((1-0.02)^{-1/2}\).
  2. \(\approx1+\left(-\tfrac12\right)(-0.02)=1+0.01=1.01\).
  3. The exact value is 1.0102.

Answer: About 1.01

Why (1 + x)ⁿ ≈ 1 + nx works

The binomial series is

\[(1+x)^n=1+nx+\frac{n(n-1)}{2!}x^2+\frac{n(n-1)(n-2)}{3!}x^3+\cdots\]

When \(|x|\ll1\), each term is far smaller than the one before. Keeping only the first two gives \(1+nx\). For \(n=\tfrac12\) and \(x=0.02\), the dropped \(x^2\) term is \(-\tfrac18(0.02)^2=-0.00005\), negligible next to 0.01.

Calculus gives the same result. Near \(x=0\), any smooth \(f(x)\approx f(0)+f'(0)\,x\). With \(f=(1+x)^n\), \(f(0)=1\) and \(f'(0)=n\).

Forms used in physics

  • \(\dfrac{1}{1+x}\approx1-x\), \(\dfrac{1}{1-x}\approx1+x\)
  • \(\sqrt{1+x}\approx1+\dfrac x2\), \(\dfrac1{\sqrt{1-x}}\approx1+\dfrac x2\)
  • \((1+x)^m(1+y)^n\approx1+mx+ny\) when both are small
  • \(e^x\approx1+x\), \(\ln(1+x)\approx x\)

Uses: \(g\) at small height \(g(1-2h/R)\); a 2% longer pendulum has a period about 1% longer; percentage errors in \(x^ay^b\); small changes in volume with temperature.

When the rule fails

The rule is a linear fit near \(x=0\). As \(x\) grows, the curve and the line separate. For \(x=0.2\), \((1.2)^2=1.44\) while \(1+2(0.2)=1.40\). For \(x=0.5\), \((1.5)^{-2}=0.444\) while \(1-2(0.5)=0\), which is useless. Use the exact form when \(x\) is not small.

Question patterns
  • "Find \(g\) at a height \(h\ll R\)" or "At what height does \(g\) fall by 1%?"
  • "Decrease in \(g\) at height \(h\) equals the decrease at depth \(d\). Relation between \(d\) and \(h\)?"
  • "Length of a pendulum increases by 2%. Percentage change in period?"
  • Pure estimates: \(\sqrt{1.02}\), \((0.998)^{-3}\), \(1/\sqrt{0.98}\).
Another common mistake

The rule needs \(|x|\ll1\). Using it for \(h=R/2\) gives \(g(1-1)=0\), which is absurd. The true value is \(4g/9\).

Another common mistake

Keep the sign of \(n\). For \((1+x)^{-1/2}\), the answer is \(1-x/2\), not \(1+x/2\).

5 Core

Graphs: slope and intercept

A graph is an equation made visible. Its horizontal and vertical axes must have quantities and units. A straight line has a constant rate of change; its intercept tells you the value of the vertical quantity when the horizontal quantity is zero.

An upward line means a positive slope, not necessarily a positive value of the plotted quantity. Always read what is on each axis: the slope of displacement versus time is velocity, while the slope of velocity versus time is acceleration.

Graphs: slope and intercept
\[ y=mx+b,\qquad m=\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} \]
Symbols and assumptions: x and y are the plotted quantities; m is slope with units [y]/[x]; b is the y-intercept with the same units as y; Δ denotes a difference. Use distinct points on a straight line.
Other useful formulas
Line through two points and intercept form
\[ m=\frac{y_2-y_1}{x_2-x_1},\qquad \frac{x}{a}+\frac{y}{b}=1 \]
Symbols and assumptions: (x₁, y₁) and (x₂, y₂) are two points on the line; m is the slope (units of y per unit of x); a and b are the x- and y-intercepts in the units of x and y.
Linearised pendulum law
\[ T^2=\frac{4\pi^2}{g}\,\ell\quad\Rightarrow\quad g=\frac{4\pi^2}{\text{slope of }T^2\text{ vs }\ell} \]
Symbols and assumptions: T is period (s), ℓ is length (m), g is gravitational acceleration (m/s²). Holds for small swings of a simple pendulum.
Watch the assumption

The drawn steepness depends on axis scales; the numerical slope must be calculated from labelled values.

Worked example

A velocity graph passes through (2 s, 5 m/s) and (6 s, 13 m/s). Find acceleration.

Show solution

The velocity change is 8 m/s in 4 s, so acceleration is 2 m/s². Extrapolating gives an initial velocity of 1 m/s.

Worked example · Graph

In a pendulum experiment, the line of \(T^2\) against \(\ell\) passes through (0.5 m, 2.0 s²) and (1.0 m, 4.0 s²). Find \(g\).

Show solution
  1. Slope = \(\dfrac{4.0-2.0}{1.0-0.5}=4.0\ \text{s}^2/\text{m}\).
  2. Slope \(=4\pi^2/g\), so \(g=4\pi^2/4.0=\pi^2\).
  3. \(\pi^2\approx9.87\ \text{m/s}^2\).

Answer: \(g\approx9.87\ \text{m/s}^2\)

More solved problem patterns
Worked example · Graph

Two straight x–t lines on the same axes make angles of 30° and 60° with the time axis. Find the ratio of the velocities.

Show solution
  1. On one graph with fixed scales, velocity = slope = \(\tan\theta\).
  2. \(v_1:v_2=\tan30^\circ:\tan60^\circ=\tfrac1{\sqrt3}:\sqrt3\).
  3. Multiply both by \(\sqrt3\): the ratio is 1 : 3.

Answer: 1 : 3

Worked example · Numerical

A line has slope −2 and y-intercept 4. Where does it cut the x-axis?

Show solution
  1. Equation: \(y=-2x+4\).
  2. At the x-axis, \(y=0\): \(0=-2x+4\Rightarrow x=2\).

Answer: At \(x=2\)

Standard shapes and the laws behind them

  • Straight line \(y=mx+c\): uniform motion (x–t), \(v=u+at\) (v–t).
  • Parabola \(y=kx^2\): \(s=\tfrac12at^2\) from rest, kinetic energy against speed.
  • Rectangular hyperbola \(xy=k\): Boyle's law (P–V at fixed T), \(\lambda\) against \(f\) at fixed wave speed.
  • Exponential decay \(y=y_0e^{-kx}\): radioactive decay, motion with drag proportional to speed.

Recognise the shape first, then read slope, intercept or area.

Slope, intercept and area

  1. Slope = \(\Delta y/\Delta x\) with units of \(y\) per unit of \(x\). On x–t it is velocity; on v–t it is acceleration; on F–x (spring) it is the spring constant.
  2. Intercept is the value of \(y\) when \(x=0\). On v–t it is the initial velocity.
  3. Area has units of \(y\times x\). Area under v–t is displacement; under F–x it is work.

Slope equals \(\tan\theta\) of the drawn angle only when both axes use the same scale. If two lines on the same graph make angles 30° and 60° with the x-axis, their slopes are in the ratio \(\tan30^\circ:\tan60^\circ=1:3\).

Linearising a law to find a constant

Experiments are analysed with straight lines. For the pendulum \(T=2\pi\sqrt{\ell/g}\), squaring gives \(T^2=\dfrac{4\pi^2}{g}\ell\). A plot of \(T^2\) against \(\ell\) is a straight line through the origin with slope \(4\pi^2/g\). Then \(g=4\pi^2/\text{slope}\).

Question patterns
  • "Which graph represents Boyle's law / uniform acceleration from rest?" (shape recognition).
  • "Two lines make angles 30° and 60° with the time axis. Ratio of velocities?"
  • "A plot of \(T^2\) against \(\ell\) has slope … Find \(g\)."
  • "log y against log x is a straight line of slope 2 …" (find the law).
Another common mistake

A straight line that does not pass through the origin is not a direct proportion. \(y=mx+c\) with \(c\ne0\) is linear, but doubling \(x\) does not double \(y\).

Check the idea

6 Core

Rates: differentiation

An average rate compares a finite change with the interval over which it occurs. An instantaneous rate is the limit over a very short interval. On a curve, it is the slope of the tangent at that point.

Differentiating position gives velocity and differentiating velocity gives acceleration. Constants vanish because they do not change. For a power of time, the exponent becomes a multiplier and the power decreases by one.

Rates: differentiation
\[ \frac{d}{dt}(Ct^n)=nCt^{n-1},\qquad v=\frac{dx}{dt},\qquad a=\frac{dv}{dt} \]
Symbols and assumptions: x is position (m), t is time (s), v is velocity (m/s), a is acceleration (m/s²); C has units making Ctⁿ a position or the stated quantity; n is a dimensionless power.
Other useful formulas
Chain rule and product rule
\[ \frac{d}{dt}\sin(\omega t)=\omega\cos(\omega t),\quad \frac{d}{dx}(uv)=u\frac{dv}{dx}+v\frac{du}{dx} \]
Symbols and assumptions: ω is a constant (rad/s), t is time (s), u and v are differentiable functions of x.
Force from potential energy
\[ F=-\frac{dU}{dx} \]
Symbols and assumptions: U is potential energy (J) as a function of position x (m); F is the conservative force (N) along x. The minus sign makes F point toward decreasing U.
Watch the assumption

Instantaneous speed is |v|; differentiating position gives signed velocity, not always a positive speed.

Worked example

A position is x = 2t³ − 3t + 5 in SI units. Find velocity at t = 2 s.

Show solution

Differentiating gives v = 6t² − 3. At 2 s this is 24 − 3 = 21 m/s. The position offset 5 m contributes no velocity.

Worked example · Numerical

A particle moves as \(x=A\sin(\omega t)\). Find its velocity and acceleration, and show \(a=-\omega^2x\).

Show solution
  1. Chain rule: \(v=\dfrac{dx}{dt}=A\omega\cos(\omega t)\).
  2. Again: \(a=\dfrac{dv}{dt}=-A\omega^2\sin(\omega t)\).
  3. Since \(x=A\sin(\omega t)\), this is \(a=-\omega^2x\), the defining property of SHM.

Answer: \(v=A\omega\cos\omega t\), \(a=-\omega^2x\)

Worked example · Numerical

The potential energy of a particle is \(U=3x^2-2x\) J, with \(x\) in metres. Find the force at \(x=1\) m.

Show solution
  1. \(\dfrac{dU}{dx}=6x-2\).
  2. \(F=-\dfrac{dU}{dx}=-(6x-2)\).
  3. At \(x=1\): \(F=-(6-2)=-4\) N, directed toward \(-x\).

Answer: \(-4\) N

Worked example · Graph

On an x–t curve, the tangent at point P is horizontal and the curve is shaped like a cap (bending down). Describe the motion at P.

Show solution
  1. A horizontal tangent means \(dx/dt=0\): the body is momentarily at rest.
  2. Cap shape means \(d^2x/dt^2\lt0\): acceleration is negative.
  3. The body was moving forward, stops at P, and starts moving backward. P is the farthest forward point.

Answer: Momentarily at rest with negative acceleration, turning back.

  • Chain rule: \(\dfrac{d}{dt}f(g(t))=f'(g)\,\dfrac{dg}{dt}\). So \(\dfrac{d}{dt}\sin(\omega t)=\omega\cos(\omega t)\).

Every rate in physics is a derivative

  • \(v=dx/dt\), \(a=dv/dt=d^2x/dt^2\)
  • Force \(F=dp/dt\); power \(P=dW/dt\); current \(I=dq/dt\)
  • Conservative force \(F=-dU/dx\): force points toward lower potential energy.

On a graph, the derivative is the slope of the tangent. A positive slope means the quantity is increasing; zero slope means a turning point; negative slope means decreasing.

Second derivative and curvature

On an x–t curve, the second derivative is the acceleration. A curve bending upward (concave up, like a cup) has \(a\gt0\). A curve bending downward (like a cap) has \(a\lt0\). A straight x–t line has \(a=0\).

Question patterns
  • "Position is \(x=at^3+bt^2+c\). Find velocity / acceleration at time t."
  • "When does the particle come to rest?" (set \(dx/dt=0\)).
  • "\(U=\dots\). Find the force at x = …" (\(F=-dU/dx\)).
  • "Charge \(q=\dots\). Find current at t = …".
Another common mistake

The derivative of \(\sin(2x)\) is \(2\cos(2x)\), not \(\cos(2x)\). Forgetting the chain-rule factor is the most common calculus slip.

Another common mistake

Velocity is zero where the x–t tangent is flat. That does not make the acceleration zero there.

7 Exam

Maxima and minima with derivatives

At the top of a hill or the bottom of a valley on a smooth graph, the tangent is flat. So a maximum or minimum of \(y(x)\) occurs where \(dy/dx=0\).

A maximum or minimum is a candidate where the rate of change is zero. Check whether it is actually a peak or a trough, and also compare the allowed endpoints. A stationary point outside the physical range is not an answer.

Maxima and minima with derivatives
\[ \frac{dy}{dx}=0;\qquad \frac{d^2y}{dx^2}\lt0\Rightarrow\text{maximum},\quad \frac{d^2y}{dx^2}\gt0\Rightarrow\text{minimum} \]
Symbols and assumptions: y(x) is a smooth function of x; the derivatives are evaluated at the point where dy/dx = 0. On a restricted range, also check the end points.
Other useful formulas
Maximum of a sin θ + b cos θ
\[ \left(a\sin\theta+b\cos\theta\right)_{\max}=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}\quad\text{at }\tan\theta=\frac ab \]
Symbols and assumptions: a and b are constants with the same units; θ is an angle. The minimum value is −√(a² + b²).
Least pulling force on a rough floor
\[ F(\theta)=\frac{\mu mg}{\cos\theta+\mu\sin\theta},\qquad F_{\min}=\frac{\mu mg}{\sqrt{1+\mu^2}}\ \text{at}\ \tan\theta=\mu \]
Symbols and assumptions: μ is the coefficient of friction, m is mass (kg), g is 10 m/s², θ is the angle of the pull above the horizontal. Assumes the block is just about to slide (limiting friction).
Watch the assumption

\(dy/dx=0\) alone does not prove a maximum or minimum. For \(y=x^3\) the slope is zero at \(x=0\), but it is neither: the curve just flattens and keeps rising.

Worked example

A ball's height is \(y=20t-5t^2\) m. Find the maximum height.

Show solution

\(dy/dt=20-10t=0\) gives \(t=2\) s. \(d^2y/dt^2=-10\lt0\), so it is a maximum. \(y_{\max}=40-20=20\) m.

Worked example · Numerical

Show that the range \(R=\dfrac{u^2\sin2\theta}{g}\) is maximum at 45°.

Show solution
  1. \(\dfrac{dR}{d\theta}=\dfrac{2u^2\cos2\theta}{g}=0\Rightarrow\cos2\theta=0\Rightarrow\theta=45^\circ\).
  2. \(\dfrac{d^2R}{d\theta^2}=-\dfrac{4u^2\sin2\theta}{g}\), which is negative at 45°.
  3. So 45° gives a maximum, \(R_{\max}=u^2/g\).

Answer: \(\theta=45^\circ\), \(R_{\max}=u^2/g\)

More solved problem patterns
Worked example · Numerical

A 4 kg block rests on a floor with \(\mu=0.75\). A rope pulls it at angle \(\theta\) above the horizontal. Find the least force that just moves it, and the angle. Use \(g=10\ \text{m/s}^2\).

Show solution
  1. Normal force: \(N=mg-F\sin\theta\). Just sliding: \(F\cos\theta=\mu N\).
  2. So \(F=\dfrac{\mu mg}{\cos\theta+\mu\sin\theta}\). F is least when the denominator is largest.
  3. Largest value of \(\cos\theta+\mu\sin\theta\) is \(\sqrt{1+\mu^2}=\sqrt{1.5625}=1.25\), at \(\tan\theta=0.75\), so \(\theta\approx37^\circ\).
  4. \(F_{\min}=\dfrac{0.75\times4\times10}{1.25}=24\) N. A horizontal pull would need 30 N.

Answer: 24 N at about 37° above the horizontal

Worked example · Numerical

Car A is 100 m east of a crossing and drives west toward it at 8 m/s. At the same moment car B leaves the crossing heading north at 6 m/s. Find the minimum distance between them.

Show solution
  1. At time \(t\): A is \(100-8t\) m east, B is \(6t\) m north.
  2. \(s^2=(100-8t)^2+(6t)^2\).
  3. \(\dfrac{d(s^2)}{dt}=-16(100-8t)+72t=0\Rightarrow200t=1600\Rightarrow t=8\) s.
  4. Then A is 36 m east and B is 48 m north: \(s=\sqrt{36^2+48^2}=60\) m.

Answer: 60 m, at t = 8 s

The recipe and why it works

  1. Write the quantity to be optimised as a function of one variable.
  2. Differentiate and set the derivative to zero. Solve for the variable.
  3. Check the second derivative, or check the sign of the first derivative on each side.
  4. Substitute back to get the maximum or minimum value.

Just before a maximum the function rises (\(dy/dx\gt0\)); just after, it falls (\(dy/dx\lt0\)). So the slope passes through zero while decreasing. That is what \(d^2y/dx^2\lt0\) says.

Standard results worth remembering

  • Range \(R=\dfrac{u^2\sin2\theta}{g}\) is maximum at \(\theta=45^\circ\).
  • \(a\sin\theta+b\cos\theta\) has maximum value \(\sqrt{a^2+b^2}\).
  • If \(x+y\) is fixed, the product \(xy\) is largest when \(x=y\).
  • Least force to drag a block on a rough floor: \(F_{\min}=\dfrac{\mu mg}{\sqrt{1+\mu^2}}\), applied at \(\tan\theta=\mu\) above the horizontal.

Closest approach of two moving bodies

Write the separation squared, \(s^2(t)\), as a function of time. Minimising \(s^2\) is easier than minimising \(s\) and gives the same time. Set \(d(s^2)/dt=0\).

Question patterns
  • "Find the maximum height" or "the time when velocity is maximum" (set \(a=0\)).
  • "At what angle is the range maximum?"
  • "Minimum force needed to move a block on a rough floor" and its angle.
  • "Minimum distance between two particles moving on perpendicular roads".
  • Pure maths: maximum of \(3\sin\theta+4\cos\theta\).
Another common mistake

A maximum on a restricted range may sit at an end point, where \(dy/dx\ne0\). Check the end points too.

Another common mistake

Find the value of the variable, then substitute back. Many students stop at "\(x=2\)" when the question asks for the minimum value of \(y\).

Accumulation: integration and area

Integration reverses differentiation and also measures accumulated change. If a rate varies, split the time interval into tiny pieces and add rate times duration. On a graph this becomes the signed area under the curve.

To obtain position from velocity, add the initial position after finding the displacement. Areas below the time axis are negative for displacement; take their magnitudes when calculating total distance.

Accumulation: integration and area
\[ \int_{t_1}^{t_2}Ct^n\,dt=\frac{C}{n+1}(t_2^{n+1}-t_1^{n+1}),\qquad \Delta x=\int v\,dt \]
Symbols and assumptions: C and n describe the rate; n ≠ −1 for the displayed power rule; t₁ and t₂ are limits (s); v is velocity (m/s); Δx is displacement (m). A velocity–time area therefore has units m.
Other useful formulas
Common integrals
\[ \int\frac{dx}{x}=\ln x+C,\quad \int\sin x\,dx=-\cos x+C,\quad \int\cos x\,dx=\sin x+C \]
Symbols and assumptions: x is the variable of integration (x > 0 for ln x); C is a constant fixed by an initial condition.
Average value
\[ \langle f\rangle=\frac{1}{t_2-t_1}\int_{t_1}^{t_2}f(t)\,dt \]
Symbols and assumptions: f(t) is any quantity varying with time; t₁ and t₂ are the limits (s). Average velocity is the case f = v.
Watch the assumption

An integral gives a change between limits; it does not supply an unknown initial value.

Worked example

Velocity grows uniformly from 2 to 8 m/s over 3 s. Find displacement.

Show solution

The area is a trapezium: average velocity (2+8)/2 = 5 m/s times 3 s gives 15 m. Using just the final velocity would give an incorrect 24 m.

Worked example · Numerical

A particle has \(a=6t\) m/s². At \(t=0\), \(v=2\) m/s and \(x=0\). Find \(v\) and \(x\) at \(t=2\) s.

Show solution
  1. \(v=\int6t\,dt=3t^2+C_1\). At \(t=0\), \(v=2\), so \(C_1=2\): \(v=3t^2+2\).
  2. \(x=\int(3t^2+2)\,dt=t^3+2t+C_2\). At \(t=0\), \(x=0\), so \(C_2=0\).
  3. At \(t=2\): \(v=14\) m/s and \(x=8+4=12\) m.

Answer: \(v=14\) m/s, \(x=12\) m

A force on a 0.5 kg ball rises linearly from 0 to 10 N and falls back to 0 over 0.2 s (a triangle on the F–t graph). The ball starts at rest. Find its final speed.

Show solution

  2. Impulse = change in momentum: \(0.5\,v-0=1.0\).

Show solution

Standard integrals and the constant

  • \(\int x^n\,dx=\dfrac{x^{n+1}}{n+1}+C\) for \(n\ne-1\)
  • \(\int\dfrac{dx}{x}=\ln x+C\)
  • \(\int\sin x\,dx=-\cos x+C\), \(\int\cos x\,dx=\sin x+C\), \(\int e^x\,dx=e^x+C\)

The constant \(C\) is fixed by an initial condition. If \(a=6t\), then \(v=3t^2+C\). If \(v=2\) m/s at \(t=0\), then \(C=2\). A definite integral between limits does not need \(C\), because it cancels.

Average value of a varying quantity

The time-average of a quantity is its integral divided by the interval: \(\langle f\rangle=\dfrac1T\int_0^T f\,dt\). Average velocity is total displacement over time for this reason. Over a full cycle, \(\langle\sin\rangle=0\) and \(\langle\sin^2\rangle=\tfrac12\).

Question patterns
  • "Acceleration \(a=kt\). Starting from rest, find velocity / position at time t."
  • "Area under the F–t graph gives …" (impulse).
  • "From the v–t graph find displacement and distance in the first 4 s."
  • Direct evaluation: \(\int_1^2 3x^2dx\), \(\int_0^\pi\sin x\,dx\).
Another common mistake

Integration gives a change, not a value. To get position, add the initial position to the displacement found from the integral.

Another common mistake

For the area under an x–t graph there is no standard meaning. Only areas under rate graphs (v–t, a–t, F–t, F–x) are useful.

Exponentials and logarithms

An exponential describes a fixed fractional change per equal interval, such as cooling toward room temperature. A logarithm answers the inverse question: what exponent gives a particular ratio? Its argument must be dimensionless.

Natural logarithms use base e and appear in integration of 1/x and in reversible isothermal gas work. Logarithms turn products into sums; logarithms of a ratio turn into differences. These operations apply only to positive real arguments in the elementary formulas here.

Exponentials and logarithms
\[ \ln(ab)=\ln a+\ln b,\quad \ln(a/b)=\ln a-\ln b,\quad \ln(e^x)=x \]
Symbols and assumptions: a and b are positive dimensionless numbers; x is a dimensionless exponent; ln is the natural logarithm. The argument of an exponential must also be dimensionless, for example a cooling constant times time.
Other useful formulas
Half-life and time constant
\[ y=y_0e^{-\lambda t},\quad t_{1/2}=\frac{\ln2}{\lambda}\approx\frac{0.693}{\lambda},\quad \frac{y}{y_0}=\left(\frac12\right)^{t/t_{1/2}} \]
Symbols and assumptions: y₀ is the starting value; λ is the decay constant (s⁻¹); t is time (s); t½ is the half-life (s). Assumes a constant λ.
Natural and common logs
\[ \ln x=2.303\log_{10}x \]
Symbols and assumptions: x is a positive dimensionless number; ln is base e, log₁₀ is base 10.
Watch the assumption

ln(V_f/V_i) is valid; ln of a dimensional volume alone is not a complete physical expression.

Worked example

For a temperature excess θ=θ₀e^(−λt), when does the excess halve?

Show solution

½=e^(−λt), so taking ln gives −ln2=−λt and t=ln2/λ. λ must have inverse-time units.

Worked example · Numerical

A quantity decays exponentially with a half-life of 5 s. What fraction remains after 15 s, and when does it fall to 1/16?

Show solution
  1. 15 s is 3 half-lives, so the fraction is \((1/2)^3=1/8\).
  2. \(1/16=(1/2)^4\), which needs 4 half-lives.
  3. Time = \(4\times5=20\) s.

Answer: 1/8 after 15 s; 1/16 after 20 s

Worked example · Graph

A plot of \(\ln y\) against \(t\) is a straight line with slope \(-0.2\ \text{s}^{-1}\). Find the half-life.

Show solution
  1. \(\ln y=\ln y_0-\lambda t\), so the slope is \(-\lambda\) and \(\lambda=0.2\ \text{s}^{-1}\).
  2. \(t_{1/2}=0.693/0.2\approx3.47\) s.

Find \(\log_{10}2000\) and \(\ln1000\).

Show solution

Log rules and base conversion

  • \(\log(ab)=\log a+\log b\), \(\log(a/b)=\log a-\log b\), \(\log a^n=n\log a\)
  • \(\log1=0\) in every base; \(\ln e=1\); \(\log_{10}10=1\)
  • \(\ln x=2.303\log_{10}x\)
  • Useful values: \(\log_{10}2=0.3010\), \(\log_{10}3=0.4771\), \(\ln2=0.693\)

Write a number in scientific notation before taking its log: \(\log_{10}(2\times10^{3})=0.301+3=3.301\).

Exponential decay and growth

In \(y=y_0e^{-\lambda t}\) the fractional drop in each equal interval is the same. Two time scales matter:

  • Time constant \(\tau=1/\lambda\): \(y\) falls to \(1/e\approx0.37\) of its start.
  • Half-life \(t_{1/2}=\ln2/\lambda\approx0.693/\lambda\): \(y\) halves.

After \(n\) half-lives the fraction left is \((1/2)^n\). Taking logs gives \(\ln y=\ln y_0-\lambda t\), so a graph of \(\ln y\) against \(t\) is a straight line with slope \(-\lambda\).

Why the exponent must be dimensionless

\(e^{x}=1+x+x^2/2+\cdots\). Adding 1 to \(x\) and \(x^2\) makes sense only if \(x\) has no dimensions. So in \(e^{-\lambda t}\), \([\lambda]=T^{-1}\). The same rule fixes the dimensions of constants inside \(\sin\), \(\cos\) and \(\log\).

Question patterns
  • "What fraction remains after 3 half-lives?" or "time to fall to 1/16".
  • "The graph of ln y against t is a straight line of slope … Find the half-life."
  • "Dimensions of λ in \(e^{-\lambda t}\)" (dimensions chapter link).
  • Direct evaluation of logs using \(\log2=0.301\).
Another common mistake

After two half-lives, one quarter remains, not zero. "Two halves make a whole" does not apply to decay.

Another common mistake

\(\ln\) and \(\log_{10}\) differ by the factor 2.303. Check which one the formula uses before substituting.

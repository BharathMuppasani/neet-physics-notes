1 Core

Inertia and Newton’s three laws

In an inertial frame, an object maintains its velocity unless a net external force acts. Mass measures resistance to acceleration. Newton’s second law relates net force to momentum change; the familiar ma form assumes constant mass.

Action and reaction are equal, opposite forces on different bodies. They cannot cancel on one body’s free-body diagram. They do cancel as internal forces when both bodies belong to the chosen system.

Inertia and Newton’s three laws
\[ \vec F_{\rm net}=\frac{d\vec p}{dt}=m\vec a\quad(\text{constant }m),\qquad \vec p=m\vec v \]
Symbols and assumptions: F_net is sum of external forces (N); p is momentum (kg m/s); m is mass (kg); a acceleration (m/s²); v velocity (m/s). These equations are used in an inertial reference frame.
Watch the assumption

Motion does not require a continuing net force; acceleration does.

Worked example

A 2 kg block experiences 10 N right and 4 N left. Find acceleration.

Show solution

Net force is 6 N right, so a=6/2=3 m/s² right. Use the net force, not the larger individual force.

Check the idea

2 Core

Free-body diagrams and contact forces

Isolate one body and draw only the forces acting on it. Weight acts downward, tension along a taut string, and normal force perpendicular to a contact surface. Label who exerts every force to avoid inventing extra forces.

The normal force is determined by acceleration and other forces perpendicular to the surface. It equals mg only in special circumstances. Resolve weight parallel and perpendicular to an incline to simplify the equations.

Free-body diagrams and contact forces
\[ W=mg,\qquad W_{\parallel}=mg\sin\theta,\qquad W_{\perp}=mg\cos\theta \]
Symbols and assumptions: W is gravitational force (N); m mass (kg); g gravity magnitude (m/s²); θ incline angle from horizontal. Parallel weight points downhill; perpendicular weight points into the plane. These are components of one force.
Watch the assumption

Do not draw mg together with both of its components as three separate forces.

Worked example

A 5 kg block rests on a smooth 30° incline. Use g=10 m/s². Find normal force and downhill acceleration.

Show solution

N=50 cos30°=25√3 N. Downhill net force is 50 sin30°=25 N, giving acceleration 5 m/s².

Check the idea

3 Core

Connected masses, pulleys and string constraints

A light inextensible string over a smooth massless pulley has the same tension throughout. Its length constraint relates the motions of the connected masses. For a simple fixed pulley, their acceleration magnitudes match.

Write a separate force equation for each body, then add to eliminate internal tension. A movable pulley can have two string segments supporting one load, so its displacement relation differs; derive the constraint from total string length.

Connected masses, pulleys and string constraints
\[ a=\frac{(m_2-m_1)g}{m_1+m_2},\qquad T=\frac{2m_1m_2g}{m_1+m_2} \]
Symbols and assumptions: m₁ and m₂ are masses (kg) with m₂>m₁; a is simple Atwood acceleration magnitude (m/s²); T is string tension (N); g gravity (m/s²). Assumptions: light taut string, fixed smooth massless pulley and negligible drag.
Watch the assumption

Tension is not simply the weight of either accelerating mass.

Worked example

An ideal Atwood machine has masses 2 and 3 kg. Use g=10 m/s². Find acceleration and tension.

Show solution

a=(3−2)10/5=2 m/s². For the rising mass, T−20=2×2, so T=24 N. The other equation gives 30−24=3×2.

Check the idea

4 Core

Apparent weight and accelerated frames

A weighing scale measures its contact normal force, not the gravitational force directly. In an upward-accelerating lift, the scale must provide extra upward force. In a downward-accelerating lift, it provides less.

In a frame accelerating with the lift, one may add a pseudo force opposite the frame acceleration to use equilibrium-style equations. This is a frame accounting device, not an interaction from another body.

Apparent weight and accelerated frames
\[ N=m(g+a)\ \text{(upward acceleration)},\quad N=m(g-a)\ \text{(downward acceleration)},\quad \vec F_{\rm pseudo}=-m\vec a_{\rm frame} \]
Symbols and assumptions: N is scale force (N); m mass (kg); a is the positive acceleration magnitude (m/s²); g gravity magnitude. Downward contact formula assumes a≤g and maintained contact. Pseudo force belongs only to the specified non-inertial frame.
Watch the assumption

A lift moving downward can give a larger apparent weight if it is slowing, because its acceleration is upward.

Worked example

A 50 kg person is in a lift descending but slowing at 2 m/s². With g=10, what does the scale read?

Show solution

Acceleration is upward. Thus N=50(10+2)=600 N, equivalent to 60 kg on a scale calibrated with g=10.

Check the idea

5 Core

Impulse and momentum conservation

Impulse is accumulated force over a time interval and equals momentum change. A short impact can have a large force even when its impulse is modest. Using average force connects an impulse to the collision duration.

A system conserves total momentum when its net external impulse is negligible. Internal collision forces cancel pairwise. Kinetic energy need not be conserved, so check momentum and energy separately.

Impulse and momentum conservation
\[ \vec J=\int\vec F\,dt=\Delta\vec p,\qquad \vec F_{\rm av}=\frac{\Delta\vec p}{\Delta t},\qquad \vec P_f=\vec P_i\quad(J_{\rm ext}=0) \]
Symbols and assumptions: J is impulse (N s); F force (N); p momentum (kg m/s); Δt interaction duration (s); P is total system momentum. Conservation applies to the chosen system when external impulse is zero or negligible.
Watch the assumption

Reversal requires subtracting signed velocities; the momentum change can be twice the initial magnitude.

Worked example

A 0.2 kg ball reverses from +10 to −10 m/s in 0.02 s. Find average force.

Show solution

Δp=0.2(−10−10)=−4 kg m/s. Average force is −4/0.02=−200 N, opposite the initial direction.

Check the idea

6 Core

Forces in circular motion and banking

Circular motion requires an inward net force. Draw the actual forces and equate their radial component sum to mv²/r. On a level road, friction can provide this inward force; on an ideal banked road, a normal-force component can provide it.

For a frictionless banked turn, the normal force’s vertical component balances weight and its horizontal component supplies centripetal force. Dividing these equations gives the design speed. There is no extra centripetal force to draw.

Forces in circular motion and banking
\[ \sum F_r=\frac{mv^2}{r},\qquad \tan\theta=\frac{v^2}{rg}\quad\text{(frictionless bank)} \]
Symbols and assumptions: m is mass (kg); v speed (m/s); r turn radius (m); F_r actual force components directed inward (N); θ road-bank angle; g gravity (m/s²). The banking relation assumes no vertical acceleration and no friction.
Watch the assumption

Weight and normal force are not an action–reaction pair; both act on the vehicle.

Worked example

A frictionless banked turn has r=40 m and tanθ=0.25. With g=10, find design speed.

Show solution

v=√(rg tanθ)=√(40×10×0.25)=10 m/s. Mass cancels from both radial and vertical balance.

Check the idea

1 Basics

Static friction is adjustable

Static friction prevents relative slipping at the contact. It adjusts to the amount needed for equilibrium, up to a maximum value. The coefficient sets the limit; it does not force friction to equal that limit in every stationary situation.

To solve, first assume no slip, find the friction required from the equations, and check whether it is within μₛN. If the requirement exceeds the limit, slipping begins and the static assumption fails.

Static friction is adjustable
\[ 0\leq |f_s|\leq\mu_sN,\qquad f_{s,\max}=\mu_sN \]
Symbols and assumptions: fₛ is signed static friction along the contact (N); μₛ is dimensionless static coefficient; N is normal force (N). Friction opposes the relative sliding tendency, not necessarily the centre-of-mass velocity.
Other useful formulas
Range of the total contact force (level surface, horizontal push)
\[ mg\;\le\;\sqrt{N^2+f^2}\;\le\;mg\sqrt{1+\mu_s^2} \]
Symbols and assumptions: m mass (kg); g = 10 m/s²; N = mg normal force (N); f friction (N), from 0 up to μₛmg; μₛ static coefficient (no unit). Holds while the block is at rest and the push is horizontal.
Angle of friction
\[ \tan\lambda=\frac{f_{s,\max}}{N}=\mu_s \]
Symbols and assumptions: λ angle between the resultant contact force and the normal at the point of slipping (degrees or rad); μₛ static coefficient (no unit). λ is a property of the pair of surfaces.
Watch the assumption

At rest with no horizontal force and no slip tendency, friction can be zero.

Worked example

A block has normal force 40 N and μₛ=0.5. A 12 N horizontal force is applied. Find static friction.

Show solution

The maximum available static friction is 20 N. Since only 12 N is required to balance the push, friction is 12 N opposite it and the block remains at rest.

Worked example · Numerical

A 5 kg block rests on a floor with μₛ = 0.4 and μₖ = 0.3. A horizontal force of (a) 10 N, (b) 20 N, (c) 25 N acts on it. Find the friction and the acceleration in each case.

Show solution
  1. Normal force N = mg = 50 N. Limiting static friction = 0.4 × 50 = 20 N. Kinetic friction = 0.3 × 50 = 15 N.
  2. (a) 10 N < 20 N, so the block stays at rest. Static friction adjusts to 10 N and a = 0.
  3. (b) 20 N equals the limit. The block is on the verge of moving. Friction = 20 N and a = 0.
  4. (c) 25 N > 20 N, so it slides. Friction is now kinetic, 15 N. a = (25 − 15)/5 = 2 m/s².

Answer: (a) 10 N, 0 (b) 20 N, 0 (c) 15 N, 2 m/s²

Worked example · Graph

For a 6 kg block, the friction–applied-force graph rises along a 45° line to a peak of 30 N, then drops and stays at 24 N. Find μₛ and μₖ.

Show solution
  1. N = mg = 60 N on a level floor with a horizontal push.
  2. The peak is limiting static friction: μₛ = 30/60 = 0.5.
  3. The flat level is kinetic friction: μₖ = 24/60 = 0.4.
  4. Check: μₖ < μₛ, as expected for the drop after the peak.

Answer: μₛ = 0.5, μₖ = 0.4

Worked example · Conceptual

A 2 kg book is held against a vertical wall by a horizontal push F. μₛ = 0.5. (a) Find the least F that holds it. (b) If F = 100 N, what is the friction on the book?

Show solution
  1. The push F is perpendicular to the wall, so the normal force is N = F. Friction acts vertically and must balance the weight, 20 N.
  2. (a) Holding needs mg ≤ μₛF, so F ≥ 20/0.5 = 40 N.
  3. (b) With F = 100 N, the friction available is up to 50 N. But only 20 N is needed for equilibrium, so friction = 20 N upward.
  4. Pressing harder raises the limit, not the actual friction.

Answer: (a) 40 N (b) 20 N upward

Why friction exists and the laws of limiting friction

Even polished surfaces are rough on a microscopic scale. They touch only at a few high points, and the real contact area is a tiny fraction of the apparent area. At these points the atoms are so close that they bond (a kind of "cold welding"). Friction is the force needed to shear these bonds and to plough the high points through each other.

The laws of limiting friction follow from this picture:

  1. Limiting friction acts along the surface, opposite to the direction in which the body is about to slip.
  2. It is proportional to the normal force: \(f_{s,\max}=\mu_sN\). A larger N squeezes more high points into contact.
  3. It does not depend on the apparent area of contact. Spreading the same N over a larger area lowers the pressure, so each high point flattens less. The real contact area stays about the same.
  4. It depends on the nature and condition of the two surfaces, which is what \(\mu_s\) describes.

Kinetic friction obeys the same laws with \(\mu_k\). Usually \(\mu_k<\mu_s\), because sliding surfaces do not get time to form as many bonds. This is why it is harder to start a heavy box moving than to keep it moving.

Reading the friction–applied-force graph

Push a block with a horizontal force F that grows slowly from zero (see the figure).

  • Static region: the block stays at rest, so friction exactly balances the push, f = F. The graph is a straight line through the origin with slope 1 (45° when both axes use the same scale).
  • Limiting point: at F = μₛN the block is just about to move. This is the peak of the graph.
  • Kinetic region: once the block slides, friction drops to μₖN and stays almost constant, whatever F is. The extra force F − μₖN now produces acceleration.

If a question gives the graph and the mass, read μₛ from the peak and μₖ from the flat level, each divided by N = mg.

Total contact force and the angle of friction

The surface exerts one contact force on the block. Its two components are N (perpendicular) and f (along the surface). Its magnitude is \(\sqrt{N^2+f^2}\).

On a horizontal surface with only a horizontal push, N = mg while f can be anything from 0 to μₛmg. So the contact force lies between mg (no friction needed) and mg√(1+μₛ²) (limiting friction). It is never less than mg.

At the limit, the contact force tilts away from the normal by an angle λ with \(\tan\lambda=f_{\max}/N=\mu_s\). This λ is the angle of friction. It returns in the incline section as the angle of repose.

Question patterns
  • "A block of mass m is at rest on a rough floor and a force F (less than μₛmg) acts on it. Friction is …" Answer: F, not μₛmg.
  • Graph of friction against applied force: identify the static line, the peak (limiting friction) and the kinetic level.
  • "The contact force exerted by the floor on the block lies between …" Answer: mg and mg√(1+μ²).
  • Block pressed against a vertical wall: find the minimum horizontal force to hold it, then the friction when the force is larger.
  • Assertion–reason on why friction is independent of the area of contact.
Another common mistake

Friction does not depend on the area of contact. A brick has the same limiting friction lying flat or standing on its end, because N and the surfaces are unchanged.

Another common mistake

μ is not limited to values below 1. Rubber on dry concrete and very clean metals can have μ greater than 1. Only "μ ≥ 0" is guaranteed.

2 Core

Kinetic friction and work

Once surfaces slide relative to each other, kinetic friction opposes that relative motion. In the elementary model its magnitude is approximately μₖN. It generally converts organised mechanical energy into internal energy.

Find the normal force first, including any vertical push or pull. Then use the sliding-force equation along the motion. The kinetic coefficient is often smaller than the static coefficient, though material and speed dependence are more complicated in reality.

Kinetic friction and work
\[ f_k=\mu_kN,\qquad W_f=-f_ks\quad\text{(fixed surface, opposite sliding)} \]
Symbols and assumptions: fₖ is kinetic friction magnitude (N); μₖ is dimensionless kinetic coefficient; N normal force (N); s is sliding distance (m); W_f is work on the sliding body (J) for constant opposing friction on a stationary surface.
Other useful formulas
Stopping distance and time (level floor)
\[ s=\frac{u^2}{2\mu_kg},\qquad t=\frac{u}{\mu_kg} \]
Symbols and assumptions: u initial speed (m/s); μₖ kinetic coefficient (no unit); g = 10 m/s²; s stopping distance (m); t stopping time (s). Only friction acts horizontally and the floor is level.
Heat produced by a sliding contact
\[ Q=\mu_kN\,s_{\rm rel} \]
Symbols and assumptions: Q heat (J); μₖ kinetic coefficient; N normal force (N); s_rel distance one surface slides relative to the other (m). Valid for constant kinetic friction.
Watch the assumption

Friction can do positive work on a body carried by a moving belt; use the actual force and ground-frame displacement.

Worked example

A 2 kg block slides on a level surface with μₖ=0.2. Use g=10. Find deceleration and work over 3 m.

Show solution

N=20 N and fₖ=4 N. Acceleration opposes motion with magnitude 4/2=2 m/s². Work is −4×3=−12 J.

Worked example · Ratio

Blocks A (m) and B (2m) slide on the same rough floor. Find the ratio of their stopping distances s_A : s_B when they start (a) with the same speed, (b) with the same kinetic energy.

Show solution
  1. Deceleration is μₖg for both, independent of mass.
  2. (a) s = u²/(2μₖg) depends only on u. Same u gives s_A : s_B = 1 : 1.
  3. (b) Work–energy: μₖmg·s = K, so s = K/(μₖmg) ∝ 1/m.
  4. With equal K, s_A : s_B = 2m : m = 2 : 1.

Answer: (a) 1 : 1 (b) 2 : 1

More solved problem patterns
Worked example · Numerical

A 4 kg block starts from rest and is pulled 5 m across a floor by a horizontal 30 N force. μₖ = 0.25. Find its speed using the work–energy theorem.

Show solution
  1. Friction = 0.25 × 40 = 10 N.
  2. Work by the pull = 30 × 5 = 150 J. Work by friction = −10 × 5 = −50 J.
  3. Net work = 100 J = ½ × 4 × v².
  4. v² = 50, so v ≈ 7.07 m/s. (Check: a = 20/4 = 5 m/s² and v² = 2 × 5 × 5 = 50.)

Answer: √50 ≈ 7.07 m/s

Worked example · Conceptual

A 5 kg box is gently placed on a belt moving steadily at 2 m/s. μₖ = 0.2. Find (a) the time before the box moves with the belt, (b) the work done by friction on the box, (c) the heat produced.

Show solution
  1. The box slides backward relative to the belt, so friction on it is forward: f = 0.2 × 50 = 10 N, a = 2 m/s².
  2. (a) It reaches 2 m/s after t = 2/2 = 1 s. In that time the box moves ½ × 2 × 1² = 1 m and the belt moves 2 m.
  3. (b) Work on the box = +10 × 1 = 10 J, which equals its kinetic energy ½ × 5 × 2².
  4. (c) Relative sliding = 2 − 1 = 1 m, so heat = 10 × 1 = 10 J. The motor supplies 10 × 2 = 20 J in total.

Answer: (a) 1 s (b) +10 J (c) 10 J

Stopping distance and stopping time on a rough floor

A body sliding on a level floor with no other horizontal force slows down only because of kinetic friction.

  1. Friction = μₖmg, so the deceleration is a = μₖg. The mass cancels.
  2. From v² = u² − 2as with v = 0: stopping distance s = u²/(2μₖg).
  3. From v = u − at: stopping time t = u/(μₖg).
  4. The same result comes from the work–energy theorem: friction work −μₖmg·s removes the whole kinetic energy ½mu².

Proportional reasoning: s ∝ u² and t ∝ u. Doubling the speed makes the stopping distance four times larger but the time only twice as long. For the same kinetic energy K, s = K/(μₖmg), so the lighter body slides farther.

Work done by kinetic friction: which body and which frame

Kinetic friction opposes the relative sliding of the two surfaces. On a fixed floor that is the same as opposing the body's motion, so its work is negative. If the other surface moves, look at each body in the ground frame:

  • A box dropped on a moving conveyor belt slides backward relative to the belt. Friction on the box points forward and does positive work on the box, speeding it up.
  • Friction on the belt points backward and does negative work on the belt. The motor must supply that energy.
  • The two works add to −f × (relative sliding distance). This net negative amount is the heat produced at the contact.
Question patterns
  • "A car moving at u is braked with locked wheels. Find the stopping distance." Then: "If the speed is doubled …" (×4).
  • Ratio questions: two bodies with the same speed (same s) or the same kinetic energy (s ∝ 1/m) on the same surface.
  • Work done by friction on a box placed on a moving belt, and the heat produced.
  • Statement-type: "Kinetic friction always does negative work" (false).
Another common mistake

Kinetic friction opposes the relative velocity of the surfaces, not the applied force. A box on an accelerating conveyor receives friction in its direction of motion.

Another common mistake

Once sliding begins, use μₖ even if the question also gives μₛ. Using μₛ for a moving body is a common error in numerical answers.

3 Exam

Inclines, angle of friction and angle of repose

On an incline, gravity has a downhill component and normal force is reduced to mg cosθ if no other perpendicular force acts. Static friction must supply mg sinθ to hold the block. Their ratio determines the limiting angle.

The angle of repose is the largest slope angle allowing rest in this model. The angle of friction describes the tilt of the combined normal-and-limiting-friction reaction away from the normal. Both have tangent μₛ at the slipping threshold.

Inclines, angle of friction and angle of repose
\[ \tan\theta_r=\mu_s,\qquad a=g(\sin\theta-\mu_k\cos\theta)\quad\text{(sliding downhill)} \]
Symbols and assumptions: θᵣ is repose angle; θ actual incline angle; μₛ and μₖ static/kinetic coefficients; a is downhill acceleration (m/s²); g gravity. The acceleration formula assumes downhill sliding, no other forces and maintained contact.
Other useful formulas
Holding a block on an incline with a force along it
\[ mg(\sin\theta-\mu_s\cos\theta)\;\le\;F\;\le\;mg(\sin\theta+\mu_s\cos\theta) \]
Symbols and assumptions: F force parallel to the incline, pointing up the slope (N); m mass (kg); g = 10 m/s²; θ incline angle; μₛ static coefficient. The lower limit applies when tanθ > μₛ; otherwise it is zero.
Rough slide takes n times as long as smooth slide
\[ \mu_k=\tan\theta\left(1-\frac{1}{n^2}\right) \]
Symbols and assumptions: μₖ kinetic coefficient; θ incline angle; n = t_rough / t_smooth for the same length, both starting from rest.
Block projected up a rough incline
\[ a_{\rm up}=g(\sin\theta+\mu_k\cos\theta),\qquad a_{\rm down}=g(\sin\theta-\mu_k\cos\theta) \]
Symbols and assumptions: a magnitudes of acceleration (m/s²) while moving up and while sliding back down; θ incline angle; μₖ kinetic coefficient. The block slides back only if tanθ > μₛ.
Watch the assumption

Friction points uphill for downhill sliding, but can point downhill if an external pull tends to move the block uphill.

Worked example

A block has μₛ=1/√3 on an incline. Find its angle of repose.

Show solution

The limiting condition mg sinθ=μₛmg cosθ gives tanθ=1/√3, so θᵣ=30°.

Worked example · Numerical

A 10 kg block lies on a 37° incline (sin 37° = 0.6, cos 37° = 0.8). μₛ = 0.5. Find the force along the incline needed (a) to just start it moving up, (b) to just stop it sliding down.

Show solution
  1. mg = 100 N. Down-slope pull mg sinθ = 60 N. Normal force mg cosθ = 80 N, so limiting friction = 0.5 × 80 = 40 N.
  2. Check: tan 37° = 0.75 > 0.5, so without help the block would slide down.
  3. (a) Moving up, friction acts down the slope: F = 60 + 40 = 100 N.
  4. (b) About to slide down, friction acts up the slope: F = 60 − 40 = 20 N.

Answer: (a) 100 N (b) 20 N. Any force from 20 N to 100 N keeps it at rest.

More solved problem patterns
Worked example · Ratio

A block takes twice as long to slide down a rough 45° incline as down the same incline when it is smooth. Find μₖ.

Show solution
  1. Same length L from rest: L = ½at², so a ∝ 1/t². With t_rough = 2t_smooth, a_rough = a_smooth/4.
  2. g(sin45° − μcos45°) = ¼ g sin45°.
  3. Divide by g cos45°: 1 − μ = ¼, so μ = 0.75.
  4. Formula check: μ = tan45°(1 − 1/2²) = 0.75.

Answer: μₖ = 0.75

Worked example · Conceptual

A 2 kg block rests on a 30° incline with μₛ = 0.8. A student says friction = μₛmg cos30° ≈ 13.9 N. Is the student right?

Show solution
  1. First check whether the block can stay at rest: tan30° ≈ 0.577 < 0.8, so it does.
  2. At rest, friction balances the down-slope pull: f = mg sin30° = 20 × 0.5 = 10 N.
  3. 13.9 N is the maximum friction available, not the friction acting. The student has used the limit when the block is not at the limit.

Answer: No. Friction is 10 N up the slope.

Worked example · Numerical

A block is projected up a 30° incline at 10 m/s. μₖ = 1/(2√3). How far up does it go, and how do the times up and down compare?

Show solution
  1. μₖcos30° = (1/(2√3)) × (√3/2) = 0.25.
  2. Going up: a = 10(0.5 + 0.25) = 7.5 m/s². Distance = 10²/(2 × 7.5) ≈ 6.67 m.
  3. tan30° ≈ 0.577 > μ, so it slides back. Coming down: a = 10(0.5 − 0.25) = 2.5 m/s².
  4. Same distance: t ∝ 1/√a, so t_down/t_up = √(7.5/2.5) = √3.

Answer: About 6.67 m. The return trip takes √3 times as long.

Derivation: angle of repose equals angle of friction

  1. Tilt a plane slowly until the block is just about to slide. Call this angle θᵣ, the angle of repose.
  2. Along the plane: friction balances the downhill pull, f = mg sinθᵣ. Perpendicular: N = mg cosθᵣ.
  3. At the limit f = μₛN, so mg sinθᵣ = μₛ mg cosθᵣ, giving tanθᵣ = μₛ.
  4. Only two forces now act: the weight and the total contact force. So the contact force must be vertical. It makes angle θᵣ with the normal to the plane.
  5. But the angle between the limiting contact force and the normal is, by definition, the angle of friction λ. Hence θᵣ = λ, and tanθᵣ = tanλ = μₛ.

Below θᵣ the block stays at rest and friction is only mg sinθ. Above θᵣ it slides with a = g(sinθ − μₖcosθ).

Forces needed along the incline

A force F pushes the block along the incline (parallel to the surface). Friction always points opposite to the way the block would slip.

  • To start moving it up (or move it up at constant speed with μₖ): friction acts down, so F = mg(sinθ + μcosθ).
  • To just stop it sliding down (only needed when tanθ > μ): friction acts up, so F = mg(sinθ − μcosθ).
  • For any F between these two values the block stays at rest, and static friction takes whatever value and direction is needed.

Projected up a rough incline, and the "n times longer" result

Going up: gravity and friction both act down the slope, so the deceleration is g(sinθ + μₖcosθ). Coming down (if tanθ > μₛ): a = g(sinθ − μₖcosθ). The trip down is slower, so it takes longer than the trip up over the same distance: t_down/t_up = √(a_up/a_down).

Rough vs smooth: if sliding down a rough incline takes n times as long as down the same smooth incline (same length L from rest), then L = ½ a t² gives a_smooth/a_rough = n². So sinθ = n²(sinθ − μcosθ), which rearranges to μ = tanθ(1 − 1/n²). At 45° this is simply μ = 1 − 1/n².

Question patterns
  • "Find the angle of repose if μ = …", or "a block just begins to slide at 30°; find μ".
  • Minimum and maximum force along the incline to keep a block at rest.
  • "Time to slide down a rough incline is n times that on a smooth one. Find μ." (Answer μ = tanθ(1 − 1/n²).)
  • Block projected up a rough incline: distance travelled, and the ratio of times for the upward and downward trips.
  • Graph of friction against incline angle as θ increases from 0 to 90°.
Another common mistake

A block at rest on an incline below the angle of repose has friction mg sinθ, not μₛmg cosθ. Use μN only at the limit or while sliding.

Another common mistake

The incline angle is θ, but the normal force is mg cosθ only when no other force has a component perpendicular to the incline. A horizontal push changes N.

4 Exam

Pulling at an angle and maximum available friction

An upward-angled pull partially supports the block and reduces the normal force. A downward-angled push increases it. Because the friction limit depends on normal force, changing the force direction also changes the threshold for sliding.

At impending motion on a level floor, horizontal pull balances limiting friction while vertical forces balance. Solve both together rather than using N=mg. If the normal force reaches zero, the assumed contact model no longer applies.

Pulling at an angle and maximum available friction
\[ N=mg-P\sin\theta,\qquad P\cos\theta=\mu_s(mg-P\sin\theta) \]
Symbols and assumptions: P is pull magnitude (N), θ angle above horizontal; m mass (kg), g gravity (m/s²); N normal force (N); μₛ static coefficient. Equations describe impending horizontal slip with N≥0 and no vertical acceleration.
Other useful formulas
Pull at angle θ above horizontal (point of sliding)
\[ P=\frac{\mu mg}{\cos\theta+\mu\sin\theta},\qquad P_{\min}=\frac{\mu mg}{\sqrt{1+\mu^2}}\ \text{at}\ \tan\theta=\mu \]
Symbols and assumptions: P pull (N); m mass (kg); g = 10 m/s²; μ static coefficient for starting (kinetic for constant velocity); θ angle above horizontal. Assumes the block stays on the floor (N ≥ 0).
Push at angle θ below horizontal
\[ P=\frac{\mu mg}{\cos\theta-\mu\sin\theta}\qquad(\text{no motion possible if }\tan\theta\ge 1/\mu) \]
Symbols and assumptions: P push (N) directed downward at θ below the horizontal; μ friction coefficient; m mass (kg); g = 10 m/s².
Watch the assumption

Using μmg without checking vertical force balance misses the main effect of an angled pull.

Worked example

A 10 kg block is pulled with a 20 N upward vertical component while g=10. If μₛ=0.5, find the horizontal friction limit.

Show solution

The normal force is 100−20=80 N. Maximum static friction is 0.5×80=40 N.

Worked example · Numerical

A 10 kg block lies on a floor with μₛ = 0.75. Find the smallest force that can start it moving, and its direction. Compare with a horizontal pull.

Show solution
  1. Best angle: tanθ = μ = 0.75, so θ = 37°.
  2. P_min = μmg/√(1 + μ²) = 0.75 × 100/1.25 = 60 N.
  3. Check: N = 100 − 60 × 0.6 = 64 N; μN = 48 N; P cosθ = 60 × 0.8 = 48 N. Balanced at the limit.
  4. A horizontal pull needs μmg = 75 N, which is 25% more.

Answer: 60 N at 37° above the horizontal (horizontal pull would need 75 N)

More solved problem patterns
Worked example · Ratio

A 10 kg block (μₛ = 0.5) is to be started either by a pull at 37° above the horizontal or a push at 37° below the horizontal. Find both forces.

Show solution
  1. μmg = 50 N, cos37° = 0.8, sin37° = 0.6.
  2. Pull: P = 50/(0.8 + 0.5 × 0.6) = 50/1.1 ≈ 45.5 N.
  3. Push: P = 50/(0.8 − 0.5 × 0.6) = 50/0.5 = 100 N.
  4. The push presses the block down, raising N to 100 + 60 = 160 N and the friction limit to 80 N.

Answer: Pull ≈ 45.5 N; push = 100 N. Pushing needs about 2.2 times as much.

Worked example · Statement I / II

Statement I: A person pushing a lawn roller at an angle below the horizontal needs less force than one pulling it at the same angle above the horizontal.
Statement II: Pulling at an angle above the horizontal reduces the normal force.

Show solution
  1. A downward push adds P sinθ to the normal force, increasing friction. An upward pull subtracts it.
  2. So pulling needs less force. Statement I is false.
  3. Statement II is true: N = mg − P sinθ for an upward pull.

Answer: Statement I false, Statement II true.

Go deeper · extra explanation and derivations

Derivation: the best angle to pull a block

  1. Pull with P at angle θ above the horizontal (figure). Vertical balance: N = mg − P sinθ.
  2. At the point of sliding: P cosθ = μN = μ(mg − P sinθ).
  3. So \(P=\dfrac{\mu mg}{\cos\theta+\mu\sin\theta}\).
  4. P is least when the denominator is greatest. Write cosθ + μ sinθ = √(1 + μ²) cos(θ − λ), where tanλ = μ. Its maximum, √(1 + μ²), occurs at θ = λ.
  5. So the best angle is the angle of friction, tanθ = μ, and \(P_{\min}=\dfrac{\mu mg}{\sqrt{1+\mu^2}}=mg\sin\lambda\).

Physical picture: tilting the pull upward does two things. Its horizontal part shrinks (bad), but it lifts some weight off the floor, cutting friction (good). The best balance is at θ = λ.

Pulling versus pushing

A push directed downward at θ below the horizontal presses the block into the floor: N = mg + P sinθ. At the point of sliding P cosθ = μ(mg + P sinθ), so

\[P_{\rm push}=\frac{\mu mg}{\cos\theta-\mu\sin\theta}.\]

The denominator is smaller than for a pull, so pushing always needs more force. If cosθ ≤ μ sinθ (that is, tanθ ≥ 1/μ), no push however large can start the block: every extra newton of push adds at least as much friction as driving force. This is why a lawn roller is pulled rather than pushed.

Question patterns
  • "Find the minimum force needed to move a block on a rough floor, and the angle at which it acts." Answer: μmg/√(1 + μ²) at tan⁻¹μ.
  • Pulling vs pushing at the same angle: which needs more force, or find both.
  • Acceleration of a block pulled at an angle (normal force is not mg).
  • Statement questions on why a lawn roller is easier to pull than push.
Another common mistake

"Pulling at an angle always helps" is false. Near 90° almost all the pull is vertical. With μ < 1, a vertical pull needs P = mg to make N zero, far more than the horizontal pull μmg.

Another common mistake

If the block is not sliding, friction equals P cosθ, not μ(mg − P sinθ). Check the limit before using μN.

5 Exam

Friction in connected bodies and turning

When stacked blocks move together, static friction can accelerate the upper block. Test whether the required ma is below μₛN. A level-road turn uses the same idea: static friction accelerates the vehicle sideways without sideways sliding.

Choose the system to eliminate internal friction when finding common acceleration, then isolate the body that needs friction to determine the no-slip limit. Do not assume that friction on two touching bodies has the same direction; their friction forces are opposite.

Friction in connected bodies and turning
\[ a_{\max}=\mu_sg\quad\text{(upper block)},\qquad v_{\max}=\sqrt{\mu_srg}\quad\text{(level turn)} \]
Symbols and assumptions: a_max is maximum no-slip acceleration (m/s²) for a horizontally carried block with N=mg; v_max maximum turn speed (m/s); r radius (m); μₛ static coefficient; g gravity. The road relation assumes level motion and no other lateral force.
Other useful formulas
Rough table with hanging mass
\[ a=\frac{(m_2-\mu_km_1)g}{m_1+m_2},\qquad T=m_2(g-a),\qquad \text{moves only if } m_2>\mu_sm_1 \]
Symbols and assumptions: m₁ block on the table (kg); m₂ hanging block (kg); μₛ, μₖ table coefficients; g = 10 m/s²; a acceleration (m/s²); T tension (N). Light string, ideal pulley.
Banked road with friction
\[ v_{\max}=\sqrt{rg\,\frac{\tan\theta+\mu_s}{1-\mu_s\tan\theta}},\qquad v_{\min}=\sqrt{rg\,\frac{\tan\theta-\mu_s}{1+\mu_s\tan\theta}} \]
Symbols and assumptions: r radius of the turn (m); g = 10 m/s²; θ bank angle; μₛ static coefficient between tyres and road; v in m/s. If μₛ ≥ tanθ, v_min = 0.
Watch the assumption

Static friction can point in the direction of a body’s acceleration and do positive work on it.

Worked example

A top block is carried by a lower block accelerating at 3 m/s². With μₛ=0.4 and g=10, will it slip?

Show solution

The no-slip limit is μₛg=4 m/s². Required acceleration is 3, so static friction can carry it without slipping.

Worked example · Numerical

A 4 kg block on a rough table (μₛ = 0.3, μₖ = 0.25) is tied over a smooth pulley to a hanging 2 kg block. Find the acceleration and the tension.

Show solution
  1. Check motion: m₂g = 20 N; μₛm₁g = 0.3 × 40 = 12 N. 20 > 12, so the system moves.
  2. Kinetic friction = 0.25 × 40 = 10 N. a = (20 − 10)/(4 + 2) = 10/6 ≈ 1.67 m/s².
  3. Tension: T = m₂(g − a) = 2(10 − 1.67) ≈ 16.7 N.
  4. Check with the table block: T − 10 = 4 × 1.67 ≈ 6.67 N. Correct.

Answer: a ≈ 1.67 m/s², T ≈ 16.7 N

Worked example · Numerical

A curve of radius 30 m is banked at θ with tanθ = 0.5. μₛ = 0.5. Find the maximum and minimum safe speeds.

Show solution
  1. v_max² = rg(tanθ + μ)/(1 − μtanθ) = 300 × 1/(1 − 0.25) = 400, so v_max = 20 m/s.
  2. v_min²: numerator tanθ − μ = 0, so v_min = 0.
  3. Meaning: friction alone can hold a parked car on this bank, because μₛ = tanθ.

Answer: v_max = 20 m/s, v_min = 0

Worked example · Conceptual

A truck carries a crate on its flat floor. The truck speeds up from rest. Which force accelerates the crate, and in which direction does it act?

Show solution
  1. The crate tends to stay behind as the floor moves forward, so relative to the floor it tends to slip backward.
  2. Static friction opposes that tendency, so it acts forward on the crate.
  3. That forward friction is the only horizontal force on the crate, so it causes the crate’s acceleration. It does positive work on the crate.

Answer: Static friction from the floor, acting forward (in the direction of the truck’s acceleration)

Block on a rough table pulled by a hanging mass

A block m₁ lies on a rough table and is tied over a light pulley to a hanging block m₂.

  1. Will it move? The pull available is m₂g. The most static friction can supply is μₛm₁g. Motion starts only if m₂g > μₛm₁g, i.e. m₂ > μₛm₁.
  2. If it moves: treat the two blocks as one system along the string. Driving force m₂g, opposing force μₖm₁g, total mass m₁ + m₂: \[a=\frac{(m_2-\mu_km_1)g}{m_1+m_2}\]
  3. Tension: from the hanging block, T = m₂(g − a).
  4. If it does not move: a = 0, T = m₂g, and friction = m₂g (not μₛm₁g).

Banked road with friction

On a road banked at θ, friction can act down the slope (car tending to skid outward at high speed) or up the slope (car tending to slide inward at low speed). Resolving forces gives the safe range of speeds:

\[v_{\max}=\sqrt{rg\,\frac{\tan\theta+\mu_s}{1-\mu_s\tan\theta}},\qquad v_{\min}=\sqrt{rg\,\frac{\tan\theta-\mu_s}{1+\mu_s\tan\theta}}\]

Special cases: θ = 0 gives v_max = √(μₛrg), the level-road result. μₛ = 0 gives v = √(rg tanθ), the design speed of a frictionless bank. If μₛ ≥ tanθ, v_min = 0 and a car can even stand still on the bank.

Friction limits a vehicle’s acceleration

Driving and braking forces on a car come from static friction between tyres and road. If all wheels are driven or braked, the largest acceleration or deceleration is μₛg. A box on a truck floor can only be accelerated by friction too, so the truck must not accelerate or brake harder than μₛg (box–floor) or the box slides.

Question patterns
  • Block on a rough table with a hanging mass: find a and T, or the minimum μ to keep it at rest.
  • Two blocks in contact pushed along a rough floor: find the contact force.
  • Maximum speed on a banked road with friction (often with μ = tanθ or simple angles).
  • "A box is on the floor of a truck … find the maximum deceleration / minimum stopping distance so the box does not slide."
Another common mistake

For a block on a rough table with a hanging mass, do not write a = (m₂ − μm₁)g/(m₁ + m₂) before checking that m₂ > μₛm₁. Otherwise you get a negative or false acceleration for a system that is at rest.

Another common mistake

On a banked road the friction direction depends on speed. Below the design speed √(rg tanθ), friction acts up the slope.

6 Exam

Block on block: moving together or slipping

A small block m sits on a larger block M. A horizontal force acts on one of them. The only horizontal force between them is friction, so friction decides whether they move together or the top block slips.

First assume both blocks move together. Find their common acceleration, then check whether the friction needed is within its static limit. If not, solve each block separately with kinetic friction.

Block on block: moving together or slipping
\[ F_{\max}=\mu_s(m+M)g\ \ (\text{force on lower block}),\qquad F_{\max}=\mu_smg\,\frac{m+M}{M}\ \ (\text{force on upper block}) \]
Symbols and assumptions: m upper block (kg); M lower block (kg); μₛ static coefficient between the blocks; g = 10 m/s²; F_max largest force (N) for which they move together. Smooth floor in both results.
Other useful formulas
After slipping (force on lower block, smooth floor)
\[ a_m=\mu_kg,\qquad a_M=\frac{F-\mu_kmg}{M},\qquad t_{\rm off}=\sqrt{\frac{2L}{a_M-a_m}} \]
Symbols and assumptions: a_m, a_M ground-frame accelerations of the upper and lower blocks (m/s²); μₖ kinetic coefficient between blocks; F applied force (N); L distance the top block must slide relative to the lower one (m); t_off time (s).
Lower block on a rough floor, force on lower block
\[ F_{\max}=(\mu_1+\mu_2)(m+M)g \]
Symbols and assumptions: μ₁ static coefficient between the blocks; μ₂ coefficient between lower block and floor; m, M masses (kg); g = 10 m/s². Largest force for which both move together.
Watch the assumption

When the force acts on the upper block, the limit is not μₛ(m + M)g. Friction now has to drag the lower block, so the limit is μₛmg(m + M)/M.

Worked example

A 2 kg block sits on a 4 kg block on a smooth floor. μₛ between them is 0.3. What is the largest horizontal force on the upper block for which they move together?

Show solution

The lower block is driven only by friction, at most 0.3 × 20 = 6 N, so its largest acceleration is 6/4 = 1.5 m/s². Both move with 1.5 m/s², so F = 6 × 1.5 = 9 N. The formula gives μₛmg(m + M)/M = 6 × 6/4 = 9 N.

Worked example · Numerical

A 2 kg block sits at the front end of a 4 kg plank 1.5 m long on a smooth floor. μₛ = μₖ = 0.3 between them. A 30 N force pulls the plank forward. Find the two accelerations and the time for the block to slide off the back.

Show solution
  1. Limit for moving together: μ(m + M)g = 0.3 × 6 × 10 = 18 N. 30 N > 18 N, so they slip.
  2. Block: a_m = μg = 3 m/s² (forward).
  3. Plank: a_M = (30 − 0.3 × 20)/4 = 24/4 = 6 m/s².
  4. Relative acceleration 3 m/s². t = √(2 × 1.5/3) = 1 s.

Answer: 3 m/s² and 6 m/s²; the block falls off after 1 s

Worked example · Graph

A force F on the lower block grows from zero. Describe the graph of friction on the upper block against F (m on M, smooth floor).

Show solution
  1. Moving together: f = m × F/(m + M). This is a straight line through the origin with slope m/(m + M), less than 1.
  2. The line ends at F = μₛ(m + M)g, where f = μₛmg.
  3. Beyond that the blocks slip, and f = μₖmg, a constant horizontal line (a small drop if μₖ < μₛ).

Answer: A rising line of slope m/(m + M), then a flat line at μₖmg

Worked example · Numerical

A 1 kg block sits on a 2 kg block. μ₁ = 0.4 between them and μ₂ = 0.1 between the lower block and the floor. Find the largest horizontal force on the lower block for which they move together.

Show solution
  1. The upper block can accelerate at most at μ₁g = 4 m/s².
  2. Floor friction on the lower block = μ₂(1 + 2)g = 3 N (both blocks press on the floor).
  3. Whole system: F − 3 = 3 × 4, so F = 15 N.
  4. Formula check: (μ₁ + μ₂)(m + M)g = 0.5 × 30 = 15 N.

Answer: 15 N

Go deeper · extra explanation and derivations

Method: assume together, then check

  1. Assume no slip. Common acceleration a = F/(m + M) (smooth floor).
  2. Isolate the block without F. Friction is the only horizontal force on it, so the friction needed is its mass times a.
  3. Compare with the limit μₛmg.
    • If needed ≤ limit: they move together, and friction equals the needed value (not μₛmg).
    • If needed > limit: they slip. Use kinetic friction μₖmg on each block, in opposite directions.

Derivation of the two limits

Force F on the lower block. The upper block is carried by friction: m a ≤ μₛmg, so a ≤ μₛg. The pair then needs F = (m + M)a, giving F_max = μₛ(m + M)g.

Force F on the upper block. Now the lower block is carried by friction: M a ≤ μₛmg, so a ≤ μₛmg/M. Hence F_max = (m + M)μₛmg/M.

Below either limit, the friction on the carried block is a fraction of F: f = mF/(m + M) in the first case and f = MF/(m + M) in the second.

After slipping: separate accelerations and time to fall off

Take F on the lower block, smooth floor, F above the limit.

  • Upper block: only kinetic friction acts, forward: a_m = μₖg (independent of F).
  • Lower block: a_M = (F − μₖmg)/M.
  • Relative acceleration a_rel = a_M − a_m. The upper block slides backward relative to the lower one (but still moves forward relative to the ground).
  • If it must slide a length L to fall off, starting from rest relative to M: t = √(2L/a_rel).

With a rough floor (coefficient μ₂), add the floor friction μ₂(m + M)g on the lower block. Its normal force from the floor is the weight of both blocks.

Question patterns
  • "Find the maximum force on the lower (or upper) block so that the blocks move together."
  • "F = … N acts on the lower block. Find the friction between the blocks." (Check slip first.)
  • Find both accelerations after slipping, and the time for the top block to fall off a plank of length L.
  • Graph of friction on the upper block against F: a rising line, then a flat kinetic level.
Another common mistake

While the blocks move together, the friction between them is not μₛmg. It is only what the carried block needs, m × a.

Another common mistake

After slipping, the upper block still moves forward in the ground frame. It only slides backward relative to the lower block.

7 Basics

Rolling friction, lubrication and why we need friction

A wheel rolling on a road meets far less resistance than a box sliding on it. Rolling friction comes from the small dent the wheel and road make at the contact. The material in front is squashed and does not spring back fully, so a little energy is lost each turn.

Wheels and bearings reduce resistance by replacing sliding with rolling. Do not confuse rolling resistance with the static friction that can make a wheel start, stop, or turn without slipping.

Rolling friction, lubrication and why we need friction
\[ f_{\rm roll}=\mu_rN,\qquad \mu_r\ll\mu_k<\mu_s \]
Symbols and assumptions: f_roll rolling resistance (N); μᵣ coefficient of rolling friction (no unit, typically 0.001 to 0.01); N normal force (N); μₖ, μₛ sliding coefficients. Rolling friction also falls as the wheel radius increases.
Watch the assumption

Rolling friction is not the static friction at the contact of an ideal rolling wheel. In ideal pure rolling on rigid surfaces, static friction does no work. Rolling resistance appears only because real surfaces deform.

Worked example

A 50 kg trolley has μᵣ = 0.02. The same load dragged on its base would have μₖ = 0.4. Compare the forces needed to keep it moving at constant speed.

Show solution

Rolling: 0.02 × 500 = 10 N. Sliding: 0.4 × 500 = 200 N. Wheels reduce the force 20 times.

Worked example · Conceptual

A person walks forward on a level road. What is the direction of the friction force on the person’s foot, and what does it do?

Show solution
  1. To walk, the foot pushes the ground backward.
  2. Without friction the foot would slip backward, so static friction on the foot acts forward.
  3. This forward friction is the external force that accelerates the person. On ice, friction is small and the foot slips.

Answer: Forward; it is the external force that moves the person ahead

  2. Dragging needs μₖN with μₖ around 0.3 to 0.5; rolling needs μᵣN with μᵣ around 0.01.

Answer: Both true; R correctly explains A

Ways to reduce friction

  • Lubrication: oil or grease puts a thin fluid layer between the surfaces. Solid–solid friction is replaced by the much smaller fluid (viscous) friction.
  • Ball and roller bearings: replace sliding by rolling.
  • Air cushion: a hovercraft or air-track glider floats on a thin layer of air.
  • Polishing: reduces the high points, but only up to a point. Very highly polished flat surfaces can have more friction, because more atoms come close enough to bond (adhesion).
  • Streamlining: reduces fluid friction (drag) on vehicles and aircraft.

Friction as a helper: walking, cycles and cars

  • Walking: your foot pushes backward on the ground. The ground’s static friction on the foot points forward and drives you ahead.
  • Bicycle being pedalled: the rear wheel is driven, its bottom tends to slip backward, so friction on the rear wheel acts forward. The front wheel is pushed along, so friction on it acts backward and simply makes it turn.
  • Bicycle freewheeling (no pedalling): friction on both wheels acts backward.
  • Car tyres have treads so water is pushed out, keeping contact and friction on wet roads.
  • Friction is also why nails and screws hold, why brakes work, and why belts drive pulleys.
Question patterns
  • "Direction of friction on the front and rear wheels of a bicycle being pedalled" (rear forward, front backward).
  • "Friction is a necessary evil": statement or multiple-correct questions on its uses.
  • "Why are ball bearings used?" and "Why does lubrication reduce friction?"
  • Comparing rolling, kinetic and static coefficients: μᵣ < μₖ < μₛ.
Another common mistake

Friction does not always oppose motion of the body. When you walk, friction on your foot acts forward, in your direction of motion.

Another common mistake

Making surfaces extremely smooth does not always reduce friction. Very flat polished surfaces can stick together strongly.

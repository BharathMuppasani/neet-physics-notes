1 Basics

Periodic motion and the SHM condition

Oscillation is repeated motion about an equilibrium position. Periodic motion repeats after a fixed time, but not every periodic motion is simple harmonic. SHM requires acceleration proportional to displacement and directed toward equilibrium.

The minus sign in a=−ω²x states the restoring direction. A linear spring provides this force while it remains Hookean. A pendulum approximates it only for small angular displacement.

Periodic motion and the SHM condition
\[ a=-\omega^2x,\qquad F=-kx,\qquad \omega=\sqrt{k/m} \]
Symbols and assumptions: x is displacement from stable equilibrium (m); a acceleration (m/s²); ω angular frequency (rad/s); F restoring force (N); k positive stiffness (N/m); m mass (kg). The spring relation assumes a linear restoring force.
Other useful formulas
Effective spring constant from U(x)
\[ k=\left.\frac{d^2U}{dx^2}\right|_{x_0},\qquad \omega=\sqrt{\frac km} \]
Symbols and assumptions: k effective stiffness (N/m); U potential energy (J); x position (m); x₀ stable equilibrium where dU/dx = 0 and U is a minimum; ω angular frequency (rad/s); m mass (kg). Valid for small oscillations about x₀.
Period of sin² and cos² motion
\[ \sin^2\omega t=\tfrac12-\tfrac12\cos2\omega t\quad\Rightarrow\quad T=\frac{\pi}{\omega} \]
Symbols and assumptions: ω angular frequency of the original sine (rad/s); t time (s); T period of the squared function (s). The motion is SHM about the mean value ½, at angular frequency 2ω.
Watch the assumption

A force directed toward equilibrium is insufficient for SHM unless its magnitude is proportional to displacement.

Worked example

A 0.5 kg mass has restoring force F=−8x in SI units. Find angular frequency.

Show solution

k=8 N/m. Thus ω=√(8/0.5)=4 rad/s. The negative force coefficient indicates restoration.

Worked example · Conceptual

Classify each function as SHM, periodic but not SHM, or non-periodic: (a) \(\sin\omega t - \cos\omega t\) (b) \(\sin^2\omega t\) (c) \(\sin\omega t + \sin2\omega t\) (d) \(e^{-\omega t}\).

Show solution
  1. (a) \(\sin\omega t - \cos\omega t = \sqrt2\sin(\omega t - \pi/4)\): a single sinusoid, so SHM with amplitude \(\sqrt2\) and period \(2\pi/\omega\).
  2. (b) \(\sin^2\omega t = \tfrac12 - \tfrac12\cos2\omega t\): SHM about the mean \(\tfrac12\), amplitude \(\tfrac12\), period \(\pi/\omega\).
  3. (c) Two different frequencies cannot combine into one sinusoid. The sum repeats every \(2\pi/\omega\), so it is periodic but not SHM.
  4. (d) An exponential decay never repeats, so it is non-periodic.

Answer: (a) SHM, (b) SHM, (c) periodic but not SHM, (d) non-periodic.

More solved problem patterns
Worked example · Numerical

A 0.1 kg particle has potential energy \(U = 5x^2 - 20x + 30\) J (x in metres). Find its equilibrium position and the angular frequency of small oscillations.

Show solution
  1. Equilibrium: \(dU/dx = 10x - 20 = 0\), so \(x_0 = 2\) m.
  2. Stiffness: \(k = d^2U/dx^2 = 10\) N/m, which is positive, so the equilibrium is stable.
  3. \(\omega = \sqrt{k/m} = \sqrt{10/0.1} = 10\) rad/s.
  4. The linear term only shifts the mean position; the constant 30 J plays no role.

Answer: \(x_0 = 2\) m; \(\omega = 10\) rad/s.

Periodic, oscillatory and simple harmonic

These are three nested ideas.

  • Periodic motion repeats after a fixed time \(T\). The Earth going round the Sun is periodic.
  • Oscillatory motion is periodic motion back and forth about an equilibrium point. A swing is oscillatory; the Earth’s orbit is periodic but not oscillatory.
  • Simple harmonic motion is the special oscillation where the restoring force is proportional to displacement: \(F = -kx\). Its displacement is a single sine or cosine of time.

Every SHM is oscillatory, and every oscillation is periodic. The reverse is not true.

Testing a function for SHM

A function of time is SHM if it can be written as \(A\sin(\omega t+\phi)\) plus, at most, a constant (which only shifts the mean position).

  • \(\sin\omega t + \cos\omega t = \sqrt2\sin(\omega t + \pi/4)\): SHM with amplitude \(\sqrt2\).
  • \(\sin^2\omega t = \tfrac12 - \tfrac12\cos2\omega t\): SHM about the mean \(\tfrac12\), with angular frequency \(2\omega\) and period \(\pi/\omega\).
  • \(\sin\omega t + \sin2\omega t\): periodic (period \(2\pi/\omega\)) but not SHM, because two frequencies are mixed.
  • \(\sin^3\omega t\): periodic but not SHM (it contains \(\sin3\omega t\)).
  • \(e^{-\omega t}\) or \(\log\omega t\): not periodic at all.

For a force law, check that \(F\) is proportional to \(-x\). A force like \(F = -kx^3\) is restoring and gives periodic motion, but it is not SHM, and its period depends on the amplitude.

SHM from a potential energy curve

If a body has potential energy \(U(x)\), equilibrium is where \(dU/dx = 0\), and it is stable at a minimum. Near that minimum, \(U\) looks like a parabola, so the force is approximately \(F = -k(x-x_0)\) with \(k = d^2U/dx^2\) at \(x_0\). This is why almost any stable system makes SHM for small displacements. Then \(\omega = \sqrt{k/m}\).

Question patterns
  • “Which of the following functions represents SHM / periodic but not SHM / non-periodic motion?”
  • “The potential energy of a particle is U = ax² + bx + c. Find the angular frequency of small oscillations.”
  • “A particle moves with F = −kx³. Is the motion SHM?”
  • Graph: “Which force–displacement graph represents SHM?” (straight line through origin, negative slope)
  • Statement-type questions on periodic versus oscillatory motion.
Another common mistake

Every periodic motion is not oscillatory, and every oscillation is not SHM. Uniform circular motion is periodic but not to-and-fro.

Another common mistake

\(\sin^2\omega t\) has period \(\pi/\omega\), not \(2\pi/\omega\). Squaring doubles the frequency.

2 Core

Displacement, phase and initial conditions

A sine or cosine describes SHM. Amplitude is maximum displacement from equilibrium; phase states where the body is within its cycle. The phase constant encodes its initial position and direction of motion.

One full phase increase of 2π corresponds to one period. A cosine beginning at maximum positive displacement has zero initial velocity; starting at equilibrium generally requires another phase. Determine phase from both position and velocity, not position alone.

Displacement, phase and initial conditions
\[ x=A\cos(\omega t+\phi),\qquad T=\frac{2\pi}{\omega},\qquad f=\frac1T \]
Symbols and assumptions: x displacement and A amplitude (m); ω angular frequency (rad/s); t time (s); φ initial phase (rad); T period (s); f frequency (Hz). The equilibrium coordinate is x=0.
Other useful formulas
Amplitude and phase from initial conditions
\[ A=\sqrt{x_0^2+\frac{v_0^2}{\omega^2}},\qquad \tan\phi=-\frac{v_0}{\omega x_0} \]
Symbols and assumptions: A amplitude (m); x₀ initial displacement (m); v₀ initial velocity (m/s); ω angular frequency (rad/s); φ initial phase of x = A cos(ωt + φ) (rad). Choose the quadrant of φ from the signs of x₀ and v₀.
Time from the mean position
\[ t=\frac{T}{2\pi}\sin^{-1}\!\left(\frac xA\right) \]
Symbols and assumptions: t shortest time to go from x = 0 to x (s); T period (s); x displacement (m); A amplitude (m). Gives T/12 for A/2, T/8 for A/√2 and T/6 for √3A/2.
Watch the assumption

Frequency f and angular frequency ω differ by 2π.

Worked example

For x=0.04 cos(5πt) m, find amplitude, frequency and period.

Show solution

A=0.04 m and ω=5π rad/s. Hence f=ω/(2π)=2.5 Hz and T=0.4 s.

Worked example · Numerical

A body in SHM has a period of 2.4 s. Find the shortest time it takes to go from the mean position to half the amplitude, and from half the amplitude to the extreme.

Show solution
  1. From the mean: \(x = A\sin\theta\). At \(x = A/2\), \(\theta = \pi/6\).
  2. \(t_1 = (\pi/6)/(2\pi)\times T = T/12 = 0.2\) s.
  3. Mean to extreme takes \(T/4 = 0.6\) s, so \(A/2\) to \(A\) takes \(0.6 - 0.2 = 0.4\) s.
  4. Equal distances, unequal times: the body is slower near the extreme.

Answer: 0.2 s and 0.4 s

More solved problem patterns
Worked example · Numerical

At \(t = 0\), a particle in SHM with \(\omega = 2\) rad/s is at \(x = 3\) cm moving with velocity \(+8\) cm/s. Find the amplitude.

Show solution
  1. \(A = \sqrt{x_0^2 + (v_0/\omega)^2}\).
  2. \(v_0/\omega = 8/2 = 4\) cm.
  3. \(A = \sqrt{9+16} = 5\) cm.

Answer: 5 cm

Worked example · Graph

An x–t graph of SHM starts at \(x = -A\) at \(t = 0\), rises to \(+A\) at \(t = 0.5\) s and returns to \(-A\) at \(t = 1\) s. Write the equation.

Show solution
  1. One full cycle takes 1 s, so \(T = 1\) s and \(\omega = 2\pi\) rad/s.
  2. At \(t = 0\) the body is at the negative extreme, at rest.
  3. \(x = A\cos(\omega t + \phi)\) with \(\cos\phi = -1\) gives \(\phi = \pi\).
  4. So \(x = A\cos(2\pi t + \pi) = -A\cos2\pi t\).

Answer: \(x = -A\cos(2\pi t)\), i.e. phase constant π in the cosine form.

The reference circle

Imagine a point P moving round a circle of radius \(A\) at steady angular speed \(\omega\). Drop a perpendicular from P to a diameter. The foot of that perpendicular, N, moves back and forth along the diameter in SHM: \(x = A\cos(\omega t+\phi)\).

The angle \(\omega t+\phi\) is the phase. One full turn is \(2\pi\) and takes one period. The circle makes time questions easy: the time to go from one position to another is the angle turned by P divided by \(\omega\).

Finding amplitude and phase from the start

If at \(t = 0\) the body is at \(x_0\) with velocity \(v_0\), then for \(x = A\cos(\omega t+\phi)\):

  1. \(x_0 = A\cos\phi\) and \(v_0 = -A\omega\sin\phi\).
  2. Square and add: \(A = \sqrt{x_0^2 + (v_0/\omega)^2}\).
  3. Divide: \(\tan\phi = -v_0/(\omega x_0)\). Check the signs of \(x_0\) and \(v_0\) to pick the right quadrant.

The same position with opposite velocity gives a different phase. At \(x = A/2\) moving toward the mean, \(\phi = \pi/3\); moving away from the mean, \(\phi = -\pi/3\).

Standard time intervals

Measured from the mean position, the phase angle \(\theta\) satisfies \(x = A\sin\theta\). The time taken is \(t = \theta/\omega = \theta T/2\pi\).

  • Mean to \(A/2\): \(\theta = \pi/6\), \(t = T/12\).
  • Mean to \(A/\sqrt2\): \(\theta = \pi/4\), \(t = T/8\).
  • Mean to \(\sqrt3A/2\): \(\theta = \pi/3\), \(t = T/6\).
  • \(A/2\) to \(A\): \(T/4 - T/12 = T/6\).

The body takes less time to cover the half of the path near the mean, because it moves fastest there.

Question patterns
  • “Find the minimum time to go from the mean position to half the amplitude (or from A to A/2).”
  • “At t = 0, x = 3 cm and v = 8 cm/s with ω = 2 rad/s. Find the amplitude.”
  • “Write the equation of SHM from this x–t graph.” (find A, T and the starting phase)
  • “Find the phase difference between x₁ = A sin ωt and x₂ = A cos ωt.”
Another common mistake

Going from the mean to \(A/2\) takes \(T/12\), not \(T/8\). The second half of the path (\(A/2\) to \(A\)) takes twice as long, \(T/6\), because the body is slower there.

Another common mistake

The phase depends on both position and direction of motion. \(x = A/2\) moving inward and \(x = A/2\) moving outward are different phases.

Check the idea

3 Core

Velocity and acceleration through a cycle

In SHM, speed is greatest at equilibrium and zero at the turning points. Acceleration is zero at equilibrium and largest in magnitude at the extremes. Velocity and acceleration are not simultaneously maximal.

Differentiating displacement gives a quarter-cycle phase shift in velocity and a half-cycle reversal in acceleration. The displacement–speed relation determines speed but not the direction; use the part of the cycle to choose the sign.

Velocity and acceleration through a cycle
\[ v=-A\omega\sin(\omega t+\phi),\quad v^2=\omega^2(A^2-x^2),\quad a=-\omega^2x \]
Symbols and assumptions: v signed velocity (m/s); A amplitude and x displacement (m); ω angular frequency (rad/s); t time (s); φ phase (rad); a acceleration (m/s²). The relation gives |v| and allows two directions at an interior position.
Other useful formulas
Maximum speed and acceleration
\[ v_{\max}=A\omega,\quad a_{\max}=A\omega^2,\quad \frac{a_{\max}}{v_{\max}}=\omega \]
Symbols and assumptions: v_max speed at the mean position (m/s); a_max acceleration magnitude at the extremes (m/s²); A amplitude (m); ω angular frequency (rad/s).
Velocity–displacement ellipse
\[ \frac{x^2}{A^2}+\frac{v^2}{A^2\omega^2}=1 \]
Symbols and assumptions: x displacement (m); v velocity (m/s); A amplitude (m); ω angular frequency (rad/s). The v–x graph is an ellipse with semi-axes A and Aω.
ω from two positions
\[ \omega^2=\frac{v_1^2-v_2^2}{x_2^2-x_1^2} \]
Symbols and assumptions: v₁, v₂ speeds (m/s) at displacements x₁, x₂ (m) of the same SHM; ω angular frequency (rad/s).
Watch the assumption

At an extreme, zero velocity does not imply zero acceleration.

Worked example

For A=0.10 m and ω=20 rad/s, find speed at x=0.06 m.

Show solution

|v|=20√(0.10²−0.06²)=20×0.08=1.6 m/s. Direction depends on whether the particle is moving toward or away from the positive extreme.

Worked example · Numerical

A particle in SHM has speed 8 cm/s at \(x = 3\) cm and 6 cm/s at \(x = 4\) cm. Find the amplitude and the period.

Show solution
  1. \(\omega^2 = (v_1^2 - v_2^2)/(x_2^2 - x_1^2) = (64 - 36)/(16 - 9) = 4\), so \(\omega = 2\) rad/s.
  2. From \(v_1^2 = \omega^2(A^2 - x_1^2)\): \(64 = 4(A^2 - 9)\), so \(A^2 = 25\), \(A = 5\) cm.
  3. \(T = 2\pi/\omega = \pi\) s, about 3.14 s.

Answer: A = 5 cm, T = π s

More solved problem patterns
Worked example · Graph

The velocity–displacement graph of an SHM is an ellipse crossing the x-axis at ±0.2 m and the v-axis at ±2 m/s. Find \(\omega\) and the maximum acceleration.

Show solution
  1. The x-intercepts give the amplitude: \(A = 0.2\) m.
  2. The v-intercepts give the maximum speed: \(A\omega = 2\) m/s.
  3. \(\omega = 2/0.2 = 10\) rad/s.
  4. \(a_{\max} = A\omega^2 = 0.2\times100 = 20\) m/s².

Answer: ω = 10 rad/s; a_max = 20 m/s²

Worked example · Ratio

At what displacement is the speed of a particle in SHM half its maximum speed?

Show solution
  1. \(v = \omega\sqrt{A^2 - x^2}\) and \(v_{\max} = A\omega\).
  2. Set \(\omega\sqrt{A^2 - x^2} = A\omega/2\): \(A^2 - x^2 = A^2/4\).
  3. \(x^2 = 3A^2/4\), so \(x = \pm\sqrt3A/2\approx\pm0.87A\).
  4. Half the speed occurs much nearer the extreme than the middle.

Answer: \(x = \pm\dfrac{\sqrt3}{2}A\)

Derivation and the maximum values

Start from \(x = A\sin(\omega t+\phi)\).

  1. \(v = dx/dt = A\omega\cos(\omega t+\phi)\). Using \(\cos^2 = 1-\sin^2\), \(v = \pm\omega\sqrt{A^2-x^2}\).
  2. \(a = dv/dt = -A\omega^2\sin(\omega t+\phi) = -\omega^2x\).
  3. Maximum speed \(v_{\max} = A\omega\) at the mean. Maximum acceleration \(a_{\max} = A\omega^2\) at the extremes.
  4. Two handy combinations: \(a_{\max}/v_{\max} = \omega\) and \(v_{\max}^2/a_{\max} = A\).

Reading the graphs

  • Against time: \(x\), \(v\) and \(a\) are all sinusoids of the same period. \(v\) leads \(x\) by \(\pi/2\); \(a\) leads \(v\) by \(\pi/2\), so \(a\) is opposite in phase to \(x\).
  • \(v\) against \(x\): an ellipse, \(\dfrac{x^2}{A^2}+\dfrac{v^2}{A^2\omega^2} = 1\). Its horizontal semi-axis is \(A\) and its vertical semi-axis is \(A\omega\). It becomes a circle if the axes are scaled so that \(A\omega\) and \(A\) look equal, or if \(\omega = 1\).
  • \(a\) against \(x\): a straight line through the origin with slope \(-\omega^2\).
  • \(v^2\) against \(x^2\): a straight line with slope \(-\omega^2\) and intercept \(A^2\omega^2\).

Two positions, two speeds

If the speeds \(v_1\) and \(v_2\) at displacements \(x_1\) and \(x_2\) are known, write \(v_1^2 = \omega^2(A^2-x_1^2)\) and \(v_2^2 = \omega^2(A^2-x_2^2)\). Subtract to remove \(A\):

\(\omega^2 = \dfrac{v_1^2 - v_2^2}{x_2^2 - x_1^2}\), then \(A^2 = \dfrac{v_1^2x_2^2 - v_2^2x_1^2}{v_1^2 - v_2^2}\).

Question patterns
  • “Maximum velocity is 0.5 m/s and maximum acceleration is 2.5 m/s². Find the amplitude and period.”
  • “Speeds at x = 3 cm and 4 cm are 8 cm/s and 6 cm/s. Find A and T.”
  • Graph shape: “v against x is a(n) ___”, “a against x is a(n) ___”. (ellipse; straight line, negative slope)
  • Phase relations: “Phase difference between velocity and acceleration / displacement and acceleration.”
  • “At what displacement is the speed half its maximum value?” (√3A/2)
Another common mistake

Maximum speed and maximum acceleration never occur together. Speed peaks at the mean, where acceleration is zero; acceleration peaks at the extremes, where speed is zero.

Another common mistake

Velocity leads displacement by \(\pi/2\), not lags. Acceleration is in antiphase (\(\pi\)) with displacement, not in phase.

4 Core

Energy exchange in SHM

A linear oscillator stores potential energy as it moves away from equilibrium. On returning, that energy becomes kinetic. In ideal undamped SHM, the total remains constant and is proportional to amplitude squared.

Energy depends on x², so the same displacement magnitude has the same kinetic and potential energies on either side. Equal energies occur at |x|=A/√2, rather than halfway between equilibrium and an extreme.

Energy exchange in SHM
\[ E=\tfrac12kA^2,\quad U=\tfrac12kx^2,\quad K=\tfrac12k(A^2-x^2) \]
Symbols and assumptions: E total, U potential and K kinetic energies (J); k stiffness (N/m); A amplitude and x displacement (m). Set U=0 at equilibrium and assume no damping or driving.
Other useful formulas
Total energy in terms of frequency
\[ E=\tfrac12m\omega^2A^2=2\pi^2mf^2A^2 \]
Symbols and assumptions: E total mechanical energy (J); m mass (kg); ω angular frequency (rad/s); f frequency (Hz); A amplitude (m). Ideal undamped SHM.
Position where K = nU
\[ x=\frac{A}{\sqrt{n+1}} \]
Symbols and assumptions: x displacement magnitude (m); A amplitude (m); n ratio of kinetic to potential energy (pure number). n = 1 gives A/√2.
Frequency of energy oscillation
\[ f_{K}=f_{U}=2f \]
Symbols and assumptions: f_K, f_U frequencies at which kinetic and potential energy vary (Hz); f frequency of the SHM itself (Hz).
Watch the assumption

At x=A/2, potential energy is E/4, not E/2.

Worked example

A spring with k=100 N/m oscillates with A=0.20 m. Find total energy and energies at x=0.10 m.

Show solution

E=½×100×0.04=2 J. U=½×100×0.01=0.5 J and K=1.5 J.

Worked example · Numerical

A 0.5 kg body oscillates with amplitude 0.1 m and \(\omega = 10\) rad/s. Find its total energy, and its potential and kinetic energies at \(x = 0.05\) m.

Show solution
  1. \(E = \tfrac12m\omega^2A^2 = \tfrac12\times0.5\times100\times0.01 = 0.25\) J.
  2. \(U = \tfrac12m\omega^2x^2 = \tfrac12\times0.5\times100\times0.0025 = 0.0625\) J.
  3. \(K = E - U = 0.1875\) J.
  4. Check: at \(x = A/2\), \(U = E/4\) and \(K = 3E/4\).

Answer: E = 0.25 J; U = 0.0625 J; K = 0.1875 J

More solved problem patterns
Worked example · Graph

A body does SHM with period 2 s. Sketch in words the graph of its potential energy against time, and give the period of that graph.

Show solution
  1. \(U = \tfrac12kx^2 = E\sin^2\omega t\) if it starts at the mean.
  2. \(\sin^2\omega t\) is never negative. It rises from 0 to \(E\) and back to 0 twice per cycle of motion.
  3. So the graph is a row of identical humps between 0 and \(E\), never dipping below the time axis.
  4. Its period is \(T/2 = 1\) s.

Answer: Humps between 0 and E repeating every 1 s (half the SHM period).

Worked example · Ratio

The amplitude of an SHM is doubled and its frequency is halved, with the same mass. How does the total energy change?

Show solution
  1. \(E = 2\pi^2mf^2A^2\), so \(E\propto f^2A^2\).
  2. New factor: \((1/2)^2\times2^2 = 1\).
  3. The energy is unchanged.

Answer: No change

Go deeper · extra explanation and derivations

Derivation: the energy expressions

  1. Potential energy: work done against \(F = -kx\) to stretch from 0 to \(x\) is \(U = \int_0^x kx\,dx = \tfrac12kx^2\).
  2. Kinetic energy: \(K = \tfrac12mv^2 = \tfrac12m\omega^2(A^2 - x^2) = \tfrac12k(A^2 - x^2)\), using \(k = m\omega^2\).
  3. Total: \(E = K + U = \tfrac12kA^2 = \tfrac12m\omega^2A^2 = 2\pi^2mf^2A^2\). It does not depend on \(x\) or \(t\).

At fixed mass, \(E\propto A^2\) and \(E\propto f^2\). Doubling both amplitude and frequency multiplies the energy by 16.

How the energies change with time

With \(x = A\sin\omega t\): \(U = E\sin^2\omega t\) and \(K = E\cos^2\omega t\). Since \(\sin^2\omega t = \tfrac12(1-\cos2\omega t)\), each energy oscillates at angular frequency \(2\omega\), that is, at twice the frequency of the motion. Its period is \(T/2\).

Averaged over a cycle, \(\langle K\rangle = \langle U\rangle = E/2\). Energy never goes negative, so it peaks twice per cycle: \(U\) at both extremes, \(K\) at each pass through the mean.

Useful fractions

  • At \(x = A/2\): \(U = E/4\), \(K = 3E/4\).
  • \(K = U\) at \(x = A/\sqrt2\).
  • \(K = nU\) at \(x = A/\sqrt{n+1}\). For \(K = 3U\), \(x = A/2\); for \(K = 8U\), \(x = A/3\).
Question patterns
  • “At what displacement is KE equal to PE / three times PE?”
  • “What fraction of the total energy is kinetic at x = A/2?”
  • “If the frequency of SHM is f, the frequency of oscillation of its KE is ___.”
  • Graph: “Which graph shows PE (or KE) against displacement / time?”
  • “The amplitude is doubled and the frequency halved. How does the energy change?”
Another common mistake

Kinetic and potential energy vary with frequency \(2f\), not \(f\). If the body oscillates at 4 Hz, its kinetic energy peaks 8 times per second.

Another common mistake

At half the amplitude, potential energy is \(E/4\), not \(E/2\). Energies go as \(x^2\).

5 Exam

Spring systems and shifted equilibrium

A spring-mass oscillator’s period depends on mass and effective stiffness. Gravity shifts a vertical spring’s equilibrium extension but does not alter the period about that equilibrium in the ideal linear model.

Parallel springs undergo the same extension and their forces add. Series springs carry the same force and their extensions add. First find the effective spring constant, then measure the oscillation displacement from the new equilibrium.

Spring systems and shifted equilibrium
\[ T=2\pi\sqrt{m/k_{\rm eff}},\quad k_{\parallel}=k_1+k_2,\quad \frac1{k_{\rm series}}=\frac1{k_1}+\frac1{k_2},\quad x_0=\frac{mg}{k} \]
Symbols and assumptions: T period (s); m mass (kg); k/k_eff/k₁/k₂ stiffnesses (N/m); x₀ static vertical extension (m); g gravity. Springs are light, linear and arranged for the stated shared force or extension.
Other useful formulas
Pieces of a cut spring
\[ k_i=k\,\frac{L}{l_i} \]
Symbols and assumptions: k_i stiffness of a piece (N/m); k original stiffness (N/m); L original length (m); l_i length of that piece (m). Assumes uniform coiling.
Two bodies on a spring
\[ T=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{\mu}{k}},\qquad \mu=\frac{m_1m_2}{m_1+m_2} \]
Symbols and assumptions: T period (s); μ reduced mass (kg); m₁, m₂ the two masses (kg); k spring constant (N/m). Smooth floor, no external horizontal force.
Period from static extension
\[ T=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{x_0}{g}} \]
Symbols and assumptions: T period (s); x₀ extension at equilibrium when the mass hangs at rest (m); g gravity (m/s²). Light, ideal spring.
Watch the assumption

Use displacement from equilibrium in F=−kx, not the total vertical extension from the natural length.

Worked example

Two springs each of 80 N/m are connected in series to a 1 kg mass. Find period.

Show solution

k_eff=40 N/m. T=2π√(1/40)≈0.993 s. Connecting them in parallel would give k_eff=160 N/m and half this period.

Worked example · Numerical

A mass hung from a spring stretches it by 2.5 cm at equilibrium. Find the period of vertical oscillation. Take \(g = 10\) m/s².

Show solution

Show solution

  2. Series: \(1/k_s = 1/k_A + 1/k_B\), hence \(T_s^2 = T_A^2 + T_B^2\).

  4. In parallel instead: \(1/T_p^2 = 1/9 + 1/16 = 25/144\), \(T_p = 2.4\) s.

Go deeper · extra explanation and derivations

Derivation: series and parallel

Parallel. Both springs stretch by the same \(x\). Their forces add: \(F = k_1x + k_2x\), so \(k_p = k_1 + k_2\). Stiffer.

Series. The same force \(F\) passes through both springs. Their extensions add: \(x = F/k_1 + F/k_2\), so \(1/k_s = 1/k_1 + 1/k_2\), or \(k_s = k_1k_2/(k_1+k_2)\). Softer than either spring.

A block tied between two springs fixed to opposite walls is a parallel arrangement: displacing the block stretches one spring and compresses the other by the same \(x\), and both forces point back toward equilibrium.

Period rules: in series \(T_s^2 = T_1^2 + T_2^2\); in parallel \(1/T_p^2 = 1/T_1^2 + 1/T_2^2\), where \(T_1, T_2\) are the periods of the same mass on each spring alone.

Cutting a spring

For a given material and coil, \(k\propto1/L\): a shorter spring stretches less under the same force. So:

  • Cut into \(n\) equal parts, each piece has stiffness \(nk\).
  • Cut in the ratio \(l_1 : l_2\), the pieces have \(k_1 = k(l_1+l_2)/l_1\) and \(k_2 = k(l_1+l_2)/l_2\).

Joining the pieces back in series restores \(k\); joining them in parallel gives a much stiffer system.

Vertical springs, two bodies and spring mass

  • Vertical spring. At equilibrium \(kx_0 = mg\), so \(m/k = x_0/g\) and \(T = 2\pi\sqrt{x_0/g}\). The static extension alone gives the period. On a smooth incline, the period is the same \(2\pi\sqrt{m/k}\).
  • Two bodies joined by a spring on a smooth floor oscillate about their centre of mass with \(T = 2\pi\sqrt{\mu/k}\), where \(\mu = m_1m_2/(m_1+m_2)\) is the reduced mass.
  • Mass of the spring. A spring of mass \(m_s\) adds about \(m_s/3\) to the moving mass: \(T = 2\pi\sqrt{(m+m_s/3)/k}\).

Simple pendulum and effective gravity

A simple pendulum consists of a small bob on a light string. The tangential restoring force is −mg sinθ. For small θ in radians, sinθ≈θ makes the angular motion harmonic.

The period depends on length and gravity, not bob mass. Large amplitudes invalidate the small-angle period. In an accelerating support frame, replace gravity with the magnitude of effective gravity and measure displacement about the shifted equilibrium direction.

Simple pendulum and effective gravity
\[ T=2\pi\sqrt{\ell/g},\qquad g_{\rm eff}=g+a\quad\text{(lift accelerating upward)} \]
Symbols and assumptions: T period (s); ℓ pivot-to-bob length (m); g gravity magnitude (m/s²); a upward lift acceleration (m/s²); g_eff effective magnitude. Assume small swings, a light inextensible string, a point bob and negligible drag.
Other useful formulas
Pendulum in a horizontally accelerating vehicle
\[ T=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{\ell}{\sqrt{g^2+a^2}}} \]
Symbols and assumptions: T period (s); ℓ length (m); g gravity (m/s²); a horizontal acceleration of the vehicle (m/s²). Small oscillations about the tilted equilibrium.
Bob oscillating in a liquid
\[ T=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{\ell}{g(1-\sigma/\rho)}} \]
Symbols and assumptions: T period (s); ℓ length (m); g gravity (m/s²); σ liquid density and ρ bob density (kg/m³), with ρ > σ. Viscous drag neglected.
Pendulum of very large length
\[ T=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{1}{g\left(\frac1\ell+\frac1R\right)}}\ \xrightarrow{\ \ell\to\infty\ }\ 2\pi\sqrt{\frac Rg} \]
Symbols and assumptions: T period (s); ℓ length (m); R Earth radius (m); g surface gravity (m/s²). Accounts for the changing direction of g along the swing.
Watch the assumption

In ideal free fall the restoring effective gravity vanishes; the usual pendulum oscillation model breaks down.

Worked example

Show solution

T∝√ℓ, so the period doubles to 4 s. The bob’s mass is irrelevant in this approximation.

Show solution

  2. \(T\propto1/\sqrt{g_{\rm eff}}\), so \(T^{\prime}/T = \sqrt{10/12.5} = \sqrt{0.8}\approx0.894\).
  3. The period decreases by about 10.6%. Using \(g + a = 17.5\) would overstate the change.

Go deeper · extra explanation and derivations

Derivation: period of a simple pendulum

  1. Displace the bob by angle \(\theta\). Gravity \(mg\) splits into \(mg\cos\theta\) along the string (balanced with tension plus the centripetal need) and \(mg\sin\theta\) along the arc, toward the lowest point.
  2. Torque about the pivot: \(\tau = -mg\ell\sin\theta\). For small \(\theta\) in radians, \(\sin\theta\approx\theta\), so \(\tau\approx-mg\ell\theta\).
  3. With \(I = m\ell^2\): \(\alpha = \tau/I = -(g/\ell)\theta\). This is SHM with \(\omega^2 = g/\ell\).
  4. \(T = 2\pi\sqrt{\ell/g}\). The bob’s mass cancels. The approximation is good to about 0.5% for amplitudes up to about 15°.

A seconds pendulum has \(T = 2\) s (one second per swing). Its length is \(g/\pi^2\), about 0.99 m with \(g = 9.8\) m/s².

Effective gravity: the master rule

In a non-inertial frame or with buoyancy, replace \(g\) by the effective \(g_{\rm eff}\) that the bob feels when at rest relative to the support. Then \(T = 2\pi\sqrt{\ell/g_{\rm eff}}\).

  • Lift accelerating up at \(a\): \(g_{\rm eff} = g + a\), period shorter.
  • Lift accelerating down at \(a\): \(g_{\rm eff} = g - a\), period longer. Free fall: \(g_{\rm eff} = 0\), no oscillation.
  • Vehicle accelerating horizontally at \(a\): \(g_{\rm eff} = \sqrt{g^2 + a^2}\); the string hangs tilted at \(\tan^{-1}(a/g)\).
  • Bob of density \(\rho\) swinging in a non-viscous liquid of density \(\sigma\): buoyancy gives \(g_{\rm eff} = g(1 - \sigma/\rho)\).
  • Inside an orbiting satellite: \(g_{\rm eff} = 0\).

Length matters: effective length and very long pendulums

  • \(\ell\) is measured from the pivot to the bob’s centre of mass. A hollow bob filled with water that slowly drains: the centre of mass first drops (period rises), then returns to the centre once empty (period falls back).
  • Small length changes: \(\Delta T/T = \tfrac12\,\Delta\ell/\ell\). A metal pendulum lengthens when hot, so the clock runs slow in summer.
  • For a length comparable to the Earth’s radius, \(T = 2\pi\sqrt{\dfrac{1}{g(1/\ell + 1/R)}}\). As \(\ell\to\infty\), \(T\to2\pi\sqrt{R/g}\approx84\) min, the same as a satellite skimming the surface.

  • “A hollow sphere filled with water is used as a bob; water drains out. How does the period change?” (increases, then decreases)
  • “A pendulum clock is taken up a mountain. Does it gain or lose time?” (loses)
Another common mistake

In a horizontally accelerating vehicle, use \(g_{\rm eff} = \sqrt{g^2 + a^2}\), not \(g + a\). The two accelerations are perpendicular.

Another common mistake

Pendulum length runs to the centre of mass of the bob, not to its top or bottom.

Other SHM systems: floating body and tunnel through the Earth

Push a floating log down into water and let go. It bobs up and down. Drop a stone into an imaginary tunnel drilled straight through the Earth and it would swing from one end to the other and back. Both are SHM, and you can find their periods with one recipe: find the restoring force for a small displacement, show it is \(-kx\), and read off \(\omega = \sqrt{k/m}\).

Push the object slightly away from equilibrium and find the extra force that pushes it back. If that force is proportional to displacement, use the SHM form to read off the angular frequency. Keep the model’s density and geometry assumptions.

Other SHM systems: floating body and tunnel through the Earth
\[ T_{\rm float}=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{m}{\sigma Ag}}=2\pi\sqrt{\frac hg},\qquad T_{\rm tunnel}=2\pi\sqrt{\frac Rg} \]
Symbols and assumptions: T periods (s); m floating body’s mass (kg); σ liquid density (kg/m³); A cross-sectional area of the body at the waterline (m²); h depth of the body below the surface when floating at rest (m); g gravity (m/s²); R Earth radius (m). Floating body: vertical sides, no drag. Tunnel: uniform Earth, smooth tunnel, no rotation.
Other useful formulas
Floating body in terms of density
\[ T=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{L\rho}{\sigma g}} \]
Symbols and assumptions: T period (s); L height of the body (m); ρ density of the body (kg/m³); σ density of the liquid (kg/m³), with ρ < σ; g gravity (m/s²). Uniform vertical-sided body floating upright.
Speed at the centre of a tunnel
\[ v_{\max}=\sqrt{gR}\approx8\ \text{km/s} \]
Symbols and assumptions: v_max speed when passing the centre (m/s); g surface gravity (m/s²); R Earth radius (m). Body released from rest at the surface, uniform Earth, no friction.
Watch the assumption

For the floating body, \(h\) is the submerged depth at equilibrium, not the full height of the body.

Go deeper · extra explanation and derivations

Derivation: floating body

  1. A body of mass \(m\) with uniform cross-section \(A\) floats at rest with depth \(h\) submerged: \(mg = \sigma Ahg\).
  2. Push it down by an extra \(x\). The buoyant force becomes \(\sigma A(h+x)g\), so the net upward force is \(\sigma Agx\).
  3. Net force \(F = -\sigma Ag\,x\), which is SHM with \(k = \sigma Ag\).
  4. \(T = 2\pi\sqrt{m/(\sigma Ag)}\). Substituting \(m = \sigma Ah\): \(T = 2\pi\sqrt{h/g}\).

If the body has height \(L\) and density \(\rho\), then \(h = L\rho/\sigma\) and \(T = 2\pi\sqrt{L\rho/(\sigma g)}\).

Derivation: tunnel through the Earth

  1. Inside a uniform Earth, at distance \(r\) from the centre, \(g(r) = gr/R\), directed to the centre.
  2. Along a diametral tunnel, \(r = x\), so \(F = -(mg/R)x\). This is SHM with \(\omega = \sqrt{g/R}\).
  3. \(T = 2\pi\sqrt{R/g}\approx84\) min with \(R = 6.4\times10^6\) m and \(g = 10\) m/s².
  4. Speed at the centre \(= A\omega = R\sqrt{g/R} = \sqrt{gR}\approx8\) km/s, the same as the speed of a satellite skimming the surface.

For a straight tunnel along a chord (not through the centre), only the component of gravity along the tunnel acts. It is \(-(mg/R)\,s\), where \(s\) is the distance from the tunnel’s midpoint. So the period is the same 84 minutes for every chord.

The general recipe

To show any system does SHM and find its period:

  1. Find the equilibrium position.
  2. Displace by a small \(x\) and write the net restoring force (or torque).
  3. Simplify for small \(x\) to the form \(F = -kx\) (or \(\tau = -C\theta\)).
  4. Write \(T = 2\pi\sqrt{m/k}\) (or \(2\pi\sqrt{I/C}\)), with \(m\) the total moving mass.

A small ball rolling without slipping at the bottom of a bowl, a liquid in a U-tube and a pendulum all fit this pattern.

  • Comparisons: “tunnel period equals the period of a satellite near the surface.”
  • Show-that questions: “Prove that the motion is simple harmonic.”

Damping, driving and resonance

Damping transfers oscillator energy to the surroundings and reduces amplitude. A driving force periodically supplies energy. After transients decay, the forced motion follows the driving frequency rather than freely choosing the natural frequency.

Resonance occurs when the driving frequency is near the natural frequency and produces a large response, limited by damping. Greater damping broadens and lowers the resonance response. The ideal undamped formula does not predict a finite steady-state resonant amplitude.

Damping, driving and resonance
\[ m\ddot x+b\dot x+kx=F_0\cos(\omega_dt),\qquad \omega_0=\sqrt{k/m} \]
Symbols and assumptions: x displacement (m); dots time derivatives; m mass (kg); b viscous damping coefficient (kg/s); k stiffness (N/m); F₀ drive amplitude (N); ω_d drive and ω₀ undamped natural angular frequencies (rad/s). This is a linear damped driven model.
Other useful formulas
Exponential decay of a damped oscillator
\[ A(t)=A_0e^{-bt/2m},\qquad E(t)=E_0e^{-bt/m} \]
Symbols and assumptions: A amplitude (m) and E energy (J) at time t (s); A₀, E₀ initial values; b damping constant (kg/s); m mass (kg). Light damping assumed.
Amplitude of forced oscillation
\[ A=\frac{F_0/m}{\sqrt{(\omega_0^2-\omega_d^2)^2+(b\omega_d/m)^2}} \]
Symbols and assumptions: A steady-state amplitude (m); F₀ driving force amplitude (N); m mass (kg); ω₀ natural and ω_d driving angular frequencies (rad/s); b damping constant (kg/s).
Watch the assumption

Forced oscillation frequency is the drive frequency after transients; amplitude depends strongly on the frequency match.

Worked example

Why does pushing a swing at regular well-timed intervals increase its amplitude?

Show solution

Each push can add energy in phase with the motion. Near the swing’s natural frequency, repeated energy input builds amplitude until dissipative loss balances input or the linear approximation fails.

Worked example · Numerical

The amplitude of a damped oscillator falls to half its initial value in 10 s. What fractions of the initial amplitude and energy remain after 30 s?

Show solution
  1. Exponential decay: each 10 s multiplies the amplitude by \(\tfrac12\).
  2. After 30 s (three halvings): \(A = A_0/8\).
  3. Energy \(\propto A^2\): \(E = E_0/64\).

Answer: Amplitude 1/8; energy 1/64

Damped oscillations

With a resistive force \(-bv\), the solution for light damping is

\(x = A_0e^{-bt/2m}\cos(\omega' t + \phi)\), with \(\omega' = \sqrt{\dfrac km - \dfrac{b^2}{4m^2}}\).

  • The amplitude decays exponentially: \(A(t) = A_0e^{-bt/2m}\). It falls by the same fraction in each equal time interval.
  • Energy goes as amplitude squared: \(E(t) = E_0e^{-bt/m}\), so energy decays twice as fast (in rate constant).
  • The time for the amplitude to halve is \(t_{1/2} = 2m\ln2/b\).
  • Damping slightly lowers the frequency (\(\omega' < \omega_0\)). Very heavy damping stops oscillation altogether.

Forced oscillations and resonance

A periodic force \(F_0\cos\omega_dt\) drives the oscillator. After the starting transients die away, it oscillates at the driving frequency \(\omega_d\), with steady amplitude

\(A = \dfrac{F_0/m}{\sqrt{(\omega_0^2 - \omega_d^2)^2 + (b\omega_d/m)^2}}\).

  • If \(\omega_d\) is far from \(\omega_0\), the amplitude is small.
  • Near \(\omega_d = \omega_0\), the first term vanishes and the amplitude is limited only by damping: \(A\approx F_0/(b\omega_0)\). This is resonance.
  • Light damping gives a tall, narrow peak; heavy damping gives a low, broad one.

Resonance in everyday life

  • Soldiers break step when crossing a bridge so that their marching does not drive the bridge at its natural frequency.
  • A radio is tuned by changing its circuit’s natural frequency to match the station.
  • A singer’s note at the natural frequency of a wine glass can make it vibrate strongly enough to crack.
  • Pushing a swing at the right moment each cycle builds a large amplitude with small pushes.

The energy of a damped oscillator falls faster than its amplitude. If the amplitude halves, the energy falls to a quarter.

Combining SHMs and an oscillating liquid column

Two same-frequency SHMs along one line combine into another SHM with an amplitude determined by phase, not simply by adding amplitude magnitudes. Equal perpendicular SHMs a quarter-cycle apart trace a circle.

A displaced liquid column in a uniform U-tube has a restoring pressure difference because one side rises while the other falls. For a total liquid length L and a displacement x on one side, the height difference is 2x. Newton’s law then gives SHM with a period independent of density.

Combining SHMs and an oscillating liquid column
\[ x=a\sin\omega t+b\cos\omega t,\quad A=\sqrt{a^2+b^2},\qquad T_{U}=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{L}{2g}} \]
Symbols and assumptions: a/b coefficients and A resultant amplitude (m); ω angular frequency (rad/s); t time (s); T_U U-tube period (s); L total moving liquid-column length (m); g gravity. U-tube assumes uniform area, incompressible liquid, small motions and negligible viscosity.
Other useful formulas
Resultant phase
\[ \tan\phi=\frac{A_2\sin\delta}{A_1+A_2\cos\delta} \]
Symbols and assumptions: φ phase of the resultant relative to the first SHM (rad); A₁, A₂ amplitudes (m); δ phase difference of the second relative to the first (rad). Same frequency and line.
Equal amplitudes
\[ A=2a\cos\frac{\delta}{2} \]
Symbols and assumptions: A resultant amplitude (m); a each amplitude (m); δ phase difference (rad). Gives 2a at δ = 0, √2 a at δ = π/2, a at δ = 2π/3 and 0 at δ = π.
Watch the assumption

The tube period uses the total moving liquid length, not just the initial level difference.

Show solution

The sine and cosine components differ in phase by π/2. Their resultant amplitude is √(3²+4²)=5 cm.

Adding two SHMs along one line

Two SHMs of the same frequency along the same line, \(x_1 = A_1\sin\omega t\) and \(x_2 = A_2\sin(\omega t+\delta)\), add like vectors (phasors) of lengths \(A_1, A_2\) with angle \(\delta\) between them:

\(A = \sqrt{A_1^2 + A_2^2 + 2A_1A_2\cos\delta}\), \(\tan\phi = \dfrac{A_2\sin\delta}{A_1 + A_2\cos\delta}\).

  • \(\delta = 0\): \(A = A_1 + A_2\). \(\delta = \pi\): \(A = |A_1 - A_2|\). \(\delta = \pi/2\): \(A = \sqrt{A_1^2 + A_2^2}\).
  • Equal amplitudes \(a\) with phase difference \(\delta\): \(A = 2a\cos(\delta/2)\). At \(\delta = 2\pi/3\), \(A = a\).
  • Different frequencies do not give SHM.

Perpendicular SHMs

For \(x = a\sin\omega t\) and \(y = b\sin(\omega t + \delta)\) of the same frequency:

  • \(\delta = 0\) or \(\pi\): a straight line \(y = \pm(b/a)x\). The motion is SHM along a slanted line with amplitude \(\sqrt{a^2+b^2}\).
  • \(\delta = \pi/2\): an ellipse \(x^2/a^2 + y^2/b^2 = 1\); a circle if \(a = b\) (uniform circular motion).
  • Other \(\delta\): a tilted ellipse.

Derivation: the U-tube

  1. Push the liquid down by \(x\) on one side; it rises by \(x\) on the other. The level difference is \(2x\).
  2. The unbalanced pressure \(\rho g(2x)\) acts on the cross-section \(A\): restoring force \(F = -2\rho gAx\).
  3. The whole liquid column moves: mass \(m = \rho AL\), where \(L\) is the total length of liquid.
  4. \(a = F/m = -(2g/L)x\), so \(\omega^2 = 2g/L\) and \(T = 2\pi\sqrt{L/2g}\). Density and area cancel.
  5. If \(h\) is the height of liquid in each arm at rest (with negligible bottom length), \(L = 2h\) and \(T = 2\pi\sqrt{h/g}\).

Floating bodies and a tunnel through the Earth are treated in “Other SHM systems”.

Question patterns
  • “Find the amplitude of x = 3 sin ωt + 4 cos ωt” or “of x₁ = A sin ωt and x₂ = A sin(ωt + π/3).”
  • “Two equal-amplitude SHMs combine to give the same amplitude. Find the phase difference.” (2π/3)
  • “Perpendicular SHMs x = a sin ωt, y = a cos ωt. What is the path?” (circle)
  • “Find the period of liquid in a U-tube with total length L.” Ratio questions when L or ρ changes.
Another common mistake

Amplitudes add as phasors, not as numbers. Two 4 cm SHMs with a phase difference \(\pi/3\) give \(4\sqrt3\approx6.9\) cm, not 8 cm.

Another common mistake

In the U-tube formula, \(L\) is the total length of the liquid column, not the height in one arm.

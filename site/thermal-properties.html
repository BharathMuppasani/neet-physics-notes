1 Basics

Thermal equilibrium and temperature scales

Temperature tells whether bodies in thermal contact exchange energy as heat. Thermal equilibrium means no net heat transfer between them. The zeroth law makes a thermometer possible: bodies separately in equilibrium with a third are in equilibrium with one another.

Heat is energy in transfer because of temperature difference, not a substance stored in a body. Kelvin and Celsius have equal interval sizes but different zeros. Gas laws and radiation formulas require kelvin.

Thermal equilibrium and temperature scales
\[ T_K=t_C+273.15,\qquad \frac{t_F-32}{9}=\frac{t_C}{5} \]
Symbols and assumptions: T_K absolute temperature (K); t_C Celsius reading (°C); t_F Fahrenheit reading (°F). A 1 K temperature interval equals 1 °C; offsets apply to temperatures, not to temperature differences.
Other useful formulas
Converting between any two linear scales
\[ \frac{X-\text{LFP}_X}{\text{UFP}_X-\text{LFP}_X}=\frac{Y-\text{LFP}_Y}{\text{UFP}_Y-\text{LFP}_Y} \]
Symbols and assumptions: X, Y readings of the same temperature on two scales; LFP and UFP lower and upper fixed points of each scale, in that scale’s own units. Valid when both scales vary linearly with the same thermometric property.
Constant-volume gas thermometer
\[ T=273.16\ \text{K}\times\frac{P}{P_{\rm tr}} \]
Symbols and assumptions: T absolute temperature (K); P gas pressure at that temperature (Pa); P_tr pressure of the same gas at the triple point of water (Pa). Fixed volume and fixed amount of a dilute gas.
Watch the assumption

20°C is not twice the absolute temperature of 10°C.

Worked example

Convert 27°C to kelvin and a rise of 10°C to kelvin units.

Show solution

The temperature is 300.15 K (often 300 K in rounded problems). The temperature rise is 10 K, without adding 273.15.

Worked example · Numerical

A faulty thermometer reads 5° in melting ice and 95° in steam at 1 atm. What is the true temperature when it reads 60°?

Show solution
  1. Use the fraction rule. On this thermometer the LFP is 5 and the UFP is 95, so there are 90 divisions.
  2. \(\frac{C-0}{100}=\frac{60-5}{95-5}=\frac{55}{90}\).
  3. \(C=\frac{55}{90}\times100=61.1\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}\).

Answer: About 61.1 °C

Building any temperature scale

A thermometer uses a property that changes steadily with temperature: the length of a mercury thread, the pressure of a gas held at fixed volume, or the resistance of a platinum wire. Two fixed points calibrate it. The lower fixed point (LFP) is the ice point and the upper fixed point (UFP) is the steam point, both at 1 atm.

If the property changes linearly, a reading that sits the same fraction of the way from LFP to UFP means the same temperature on every scale:

\[ \frac{\text{reading}-\text{LFP}}{\text{UFP}-\text{LFP}}\ \text{is the same on every scale.} \]

ScaleIce pointSteam pointDivisions between them
Celsius0 °C100 °C100
Fahrenheit32 °F212 °F180
Kelvin273.15 K373.15 K100
Réaumur0 °R80 °R80

So \(\frac{C}{100}=\frac{F-32}{180}=\frac{K-273.15}{100}=\frac{R}{80}\). The same rule converts readings of a faulty or home-made thermometer: use its own marks for LFP and UFP.

Readings worth remembering

  • Celsius and Fahrenheit agree at −40°. Put \(F=C\) in \(F=\tfrac95C+32\).
  • Fahrenheit and Kelvin agree at about 574.6 (that is 301.4 °C).
  • The Fahrenheit reading is twice the Celsius reading at 160 °C (320 °F).
  • Temperature differences: \(\Delta F=1.8\,\Delta C\) and \(\Delta K=\Delta C\). A 1 °C rise is a 1.8 °F rise, not a 33.8 °F rise.

Gas thermometer, absolute zero and the zeroth law

Hold a gas at fixed volume and plot its pressure against Celsius temperature. Every dilute gas gives a straight line. Extend the lines backwards: they all reach zero pressure at the same point, −273.15 °C. This common point is absolute zero, and it does not depend on which gas you use. That is why the gas scale is a good standard.

The Kelvin scale assigns 273.16 K to the triple point of water, where ice, water and vapour coexist. A constant-volume gas thermometer then reads \(T=273.16\ \text{K}\times P/P_{\rm tr}\).

The zeroth law says: if A and B are each in thermal equilibrium with C, then A and B are in thermal equilibrium with each other. It lets us define temperature as the property that is equal for bodies in equilibrium. The thermometer is the body C.

  • A temperature change in one scale converted to another (no offset).
  • Constant-volume gas thermometer: pressure ratio gives kelvin ratio.
  • Statement questions on the zeroth law and on the meaning of heat and temperature.
Another common mistake

Converting a temperature change with the full formula. A rise of 10 °C is a rise of 18 °F and of 10 K. The offsets 32 and 273.15 belong only to actual readings.

Another common mistake

Saying a hot body “contains more heat”. A body has internal energy. Heat is the energy that flows because of a temperature difference.

2 Core

Linear, area and volume expansion

Heating usually increases average separation between particles and expands a solid. Expansion coefficients compare fractional size change with temperature change. For an isotropic solid and small expansion, each direction expands similarly.

Area depends on two lengths and volume on three, producing β≈2α and γ≈3α to first order. A hole in a plate expands as if it were filled with the same material; heating does not make the surrounding material grow inward into it. A bonded bimetallic strip bends because its layers want different lengths: the more-expanding layer becomes the longer outer, convex side.

Linear, area and volume expansion
\[ \Delta L=\alpha L_0\Delta T,\quad \Delta A=\beta A_0\Delta T,\quad \Delta V=\gamma V_0\Delta T,\quad \beta\approx2\alpha,\quad\gamma\approx3\alpha \]
Symbols and assumptions: L₀/A₀/V₀ initial length (m), area (m²), volume (m³); α/β/γ linear/area/volume coefficients (K⁻¹); ΔT temperature change (K); Δ denotes change. Relations assume isotropy, approximately constant coefficients and small fractional changes.
Other useful formulas
Anisotropic solid
\[ \gamma=\alpha_x+\alpha_y+\alpha_z,\qquad \beta_{xy}=\alpha_x+\alpha_y \]
Symbols and assumptions: α_x, α_y, α_z linear coefficients along three perpendicular axes (K⁻¹); γ volume coefficient (K⁻¹); β_xy area coefficient of a face in the x–y plane (K⁻¹). Small αΔT; for an isotropic solid all α are equal.
Heating a spinning body
\[ \frac{\Delta I}{I}=2\alpha\Delta T,\qquad \frac{\Delta\omega}{\omega}=-2\alpha\Delta T \]
Symbols and assumptions: I moment of inertia about a fixed axis (kg m²); ω angular speed (rad/s); α linear coefficient (K⁻¹); ΔT temperature rise (K). Second result needs zero external torque so that Iω is conserved; small αΔT.
Radius of a bimetallic strip
\[ R\approx\frac{d}{(\alpha_1-\alpha_2)\Delta T} \]
Symbols and assumptions: R radius of curvature of the common interface (m); d thickness of each strip (m); α₁ > α₂ linear coefficients of the two metals (K⁻¹); ΔT temperature rise above the straight state (K). Strips of equal thickness, small αΔT.
Watch the assumption

Large temperature ranges can require temperature-dependent coefficients; the simple linear model is approximate.

Worked example

A 2 m rod with α=12×10⁻⁶ K⁻¹ is heated by 50 K. Find length increase.

Show solution

ΔL=12×10⁻⁶×2×50=0.0012 m=1.2 mm. The final length is 2.0012 m.

Worked example · Ratio

When a metal sphere is heated, its radius increases by 0.2 %. Find the percentage changes in its surface area, volume and density.

Show solution
  1. Small fractional changes multiply into sums: area ∝ r², volume ∝ r³.
  2. Surface area rises by 2 × 0.2 = 0.4 %.
  3. Volume rises by 3 × 0.2 = 0.6 %.
  4. Mass is fixed, so density ∝ 1/V and falls by about 0.6 %.

Answer: Area +0.4 %, volume +0.6 %, density −0.6 %

Worked example · Numerical

A steel ring has an inner diameter of 5.000 cm at 20 °C. It must slide onto a shaft of diameter 5.005 cm. To what temperature must the ring be heated? Take \(\alpha=1.2\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{K^{-1}}\).

Show solution
  1. The hole grows like the steel: \(\Delta d=d\,\alpha\,\Delta T\).
  2. \(\Delta T=\frac{\Delta d}{d\alpha}=\frac{0.005}{5.000\times1.2\times10^{-5}}=83.3\ \mathrm{K}\).
  3. Final temperature \(=20+83.3\approx103\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}\).

Answer: About 103 °C

Worked example · Concept

A thin metal disc spins freely about its axis with no friction. It is heated by 50 K. If \(\alpha=2\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{K^{-1}}\), by what fraction does its angular speed change?

Show solution
  1. No external torque acts, so angular momentum \(I\omega\) is conserved.
  2. \(I\propto MR^2\), so \(\Delta I/I=2\alpha\Delta T=2\times2\times10^{-5}\times50=2\times10^{-3}\).
  3. \(\omega\) must fall by the same fraction to keep \(I\omega\) fixed: \(\Delta\omega/\omega=-2\times10^{-3}\).

Answer: Angular speed decreases by 0.2 %

Why solids expand on heating

Atoms in a solid vibrate about their equilibrium positions. The potential energy between two neighbouring atoms is not symmetric: it rises steeply if they are pushed together and gently if they are pulled apart. When you heat the solid, the vibrations grow. Because the energy curve is lopsided, the atoms spend more time at larger separations, so the average spacing grows. A perfectly symmetric (spring-like) potential would give no expansion at all.

Derivation: β = 2α and γ = 3α

Take a square plate of side \(L_0\) and heat it by \(\Delta T\).

  1. Each side becomes \(L_0(1+\alpha\Delta T)\).
  2. Area \(A=L_0^2(1+\alpha\Delta T)^2=A_0\left(1+2\alpha\Delta T+\alpha^2\Delta T^2\right)\).
  3. \(\alpha\Delta T\) is about \(10^{-3}\) or smaller, so \((\alpha\Delta T)^2\) is about \(10^{-6}\). Drop it. Then \(\Delta A=2\alpha A_0\Delta T\), so \(\beta=2\alpha\).
  4. For a cube, \(V=L_0^3(1+\alpha\Delta T)^3\approx V_0(1+3\alpha\Delta T)\), so \(\gamma=3\alpha\).

Hence \(\alpha:\beta:\gamma=1:2:3\) for an isotropic solid. For a crystal with different coefficients along three axes, the same algebra gives \(\gamma=\alpha_x+\alpha_y+\alpha_z\). The percentage form is quick: if every length grows by p %, area grows by 2p % and volume by 3p %, while density falls by about 3p %.

Consequences to connect

  • Holes and cavities expand exactly like the material that could fill them. A tight ring is heated so it slips over a shaft.
  • Density falls: \(\rho=\rho_0/(1+\gamma\Delta T)\approx\rho_0(1-\gamma\Delta T)\).
  • Moment of inertia depends on (size)², so \(\Delta I/I=2\alpha\Delta T\). A freely spinning disc that is heated slows down, because \(L=I\omega\) stays constant: \(\Delta\omega/\omega=-2\alpha\Delta T\).
  • Bimetallic strip: two metals bonded together bend on heating, with the metal of larger α on the outer (convex) side. Thermostats, fire alarms and flashers use this.
Question patterns
  • “The length of a rod increases by 0.1 %. Find the percentage change in area or volume.”
  • Ratio α : β : γ, or γ of an anisotropic crystal from three given α values.
  • Behaviour of a hole, a ring or a cavity on heating.
  • Which way a bimetallic strip bends on heating and on cooling.
  • Change in angular speed or moment of inertia of a heated rotating disc.
  • Fractional change in density on heating.
Another common mistake

Mixing up the factors: area has two lengths, so β = 2α; volume has three, so γ = 3α. Writing β = 3α is a common slip under time pressure.

Another common mistake

Thinking the higher-α metal ends up on the inside of a heated bimetallic strip. The longer strip must lie on the longer, outer arc.

3 Exam

Thermal stress, pendulum clocks and metal scales

When a heated rod is not free to expand, its supports push back on it. The rod stays at its original length but is now stressed, as if it had been compressed by the amount it wanted to expand. The same small expansion makes pendulum clocks lose time in summer and metal scales read short.

A rod that can expand freely changes length; a rod whose expansion is prevented develops stress. For clocks and measuring scales, first work out which length changes before deciding whether the recorded value rises or falls.

Thermal stress, pendulum clocks and metal scales
\[ \sigma_{\rm th}=Y\alpha\Delta\theta,\quad F=YA\alpha\Delta\theta,\quad \frac{\Delta T}{T}=\tfrac12\alpha\Delta\theta \]
Symbols and assumptions: σ_th thermal stress (Pa); Y Young’s modulus (Pa); α linear coefficient (K⁻¹); Δθ temperature change (K); F force on the supports (N); A cross-section (m²); T pendulum period (s) and ΔT its change. Rigid supports, small αΔθ, stress within the elastic limit.
Other useful formulas
Time lost or gained by a pendulum clock
\[ \Delta t=\tfrac12\,\alpha\,|\Delta\theta|\times86400\ \text{s per day} \]
Symbols and assumptions: Δt time lost (if hotter) or gained (if colder) per day (s); α linear coefficient of the pendulum rod (K⁻¹); Δθ temperature change from the temperature at which the clock is correct (K); 86400 seconds in a day. Small αΔθ.
Correcting a metal-scale reading
\[ L_{\rm true}=L_{\rm read}\,[1+\alpha_s(\theta-\theta_0)] \]
Symbols and assumptions: L_true actual length (m); L_read scale reading (m); α_s linear coefficient of the scale (K⁻¹); θ temperature at measurement and θ₀ calibration temperature (°C or K). The object is measured at θ.
Constant length difference (compensation)
\[ L_1\alpha_1=L_2\alpha_2 \]
Symbols and assumptions: L₁, L₂ lengths of two rods (m); α₁, α₂ their linear coefficients (K⁻¹). Then both expand by the same amount, so L₁ − L₂ is the same at all temperatures.
Watch the assumption

Thermal stress does not depend on length. A long rail and a short rail of the same steel, clamped and heated by the same amount, carry the same stress.

Worked example

A steel rail is clamped at both ends at 20 °C. Find the stress in it at 50 °C. Take Y = 2×10¹¹ Pa and α = 1.2×10⁻⁵ K⁻¹.

Show solution

σ = YαΔθ = 2×10¹¹ × 1.2×10⁻⁵ × 30 = 7.2×10⁷ Pa, compressive. Rails are laid with small gaps so that this stress does not buckle them.

Worked example · Numerical

A pendulum clock with a brass rod (\(\alpha=2\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{K^{-1}}\)) keeps correct time at 20 °C. How much time does it lose per day at 40 °C?

Show solution
  1. Fractional change in period \(=\tfrac12\alpha\Delta\theta=\tfrac12\times2\times10^{-5}\times20=2\times10^{-4}\).
  2. Time lost per day \(=2\times10^{-4}\times86400\approx17.3\) s.
  3. The period is longer when hot, so the clock runs slow.

Answer: About 17.3 s per day

Worked example · Numerical

A clock with a metal pendulum loses 12 s a day at 35 °C and gains 8 s a day at 15 °C. At what temperature does it keep correct time, and what is α?

Show solution

A steel scale is correct at 0 °C. At 40 °C it shows the length of a rod as 50.000 cm. What is the true length? (\(\alpha_{\rm steel}=1.2\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{K^{-1}}\))

Worked example · Ratio

An iron rod and a brass rod must differ in length by 10 cm at every temperature. Given \(\alpha_{\rm iron}=1.2\times10^{-5}\) and \(\alpha_{\rm brass}=1.8\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{K^{-1}}\), find their lengths.

Show solution

Derivation: stress in a clamped rod

  1. Imagine one wall removed. The rod would expand freely by \(\Delta L=L\alpha\Delta\theta\).
  2. The wall must push it back by \(\Delta L\) to keep the length fixed.
  3. Compressive strain \(=\Delta L/L=\alpha\Delta\theta\), independent of L.
  4. Stress \(=Y\times\text{strain}=Y\alpha\Delta\theta\). Force on each wall \(F=YA\alpha\Delta\theta\).

Cooling a rod that is fixed at both ends gives tension of the same size. The elastic energy stored per unit volume is \(\tfrac12\,\text{stress}\times\text{strain}=\tfrac12Y(\alpha\Delta\theta)^2\). This links this chapter with the elasticity chapter.

Pendulum clocks

A pendulum’s period is \(T=2\pi\sqrt{L/g}\). Heating stretches the rod to \(L(1+\alpha\Delta\theta)\), so \(T\) grows to about \(T(1+\tfrac12\alpha\Delta\theta)\).

  • Hot: longer period, fewer swings per day, so the clock runs slow and loses time.
  • Cold: shorter period, so the clock gains time.
  • Time lost or gained per day \(=\tfrac12\alpha\,|\Delta\theta|\times86400\) s.
  • If a clock loses at one temperature and gains at another, you can find both the temperature at which it keeps correct time and α.
  • A compensated pendulum uses two metals so their expansions cancel: \(L_1\alpha_1=L_2\alpha_2\) keeps the difference \(L_1-L_2\) fixed.

Metal scales and everyday stresses

A metal scale is correct at its calibration temperature \(\theta_0\). At a higher temperature each marked centimetre is really a little longer, so the scale reads too small. The true length is \(L_{\rm true}=L_{\rm read}\,[1+\alpha_s(\theta-\theta_0)]\). If the scale and the object are the same material and at the same temperature, the reading stays correct.

Other examples: gaps in railway tracks and bridges, telephone wires that sag in summer, and thick glass that cracks when hot water is poured in, because the inner surface expands before the outer one. Borosilicate (Pyrex) glass has a small α, so it resists such cracking.

Question patterns
  • Thermal stress or force in a rod clamped between walls; ratio for rods of different size.
  • Time lost per day by a pendulum clock when the temperature rises.
  • Clock loses at one temperature and gains at another: find the correct-time temperature.
  • Reading of a steel scale at a different temperature; true length.
  • Lengths of iron and brass rods whose difference stays constant.
Another common mistake

Saying a hot pendulum clock gains time because “longer means more”. A longer pendulum has a longer period, so it makes fewer ticks per day and loses time.

Another common mistake

Correcting a hot metal-scale reading the wrong way. The scale’s marks have spread apart, so the true length is larger than the reading.

Apparent expansion, density and anomalous water

A liquid in a vessel expands along with the vessel. The observed rise reflects the liquid’s volume expansion minus the vessel’s capacity expansion. Therefore apparent expansion is not the liquid’s true expansion coefficient.

Mass remains constant during ordinary thermal expansion, so density changes inversely with volume. Water behaves unusually between 0°C and 4°C: warming it contracts it, and its density is greatest near 4°C.

Apparent expansion, density and anomalous water
\[ \gamma_{\rm app}=\gamma_l-\gamma_v,\qquad \rho_T=\frac{\rho_0}{1+\gamma_l\Delta T} \]
Symbols and assumptions: γ_app apparent, γ_l liquid and γ_v vessel volumetric coefficients (K⁻¹); ρ₀ and ρ_T initial/final densities (kg/m³); ΔT temperature change (K). Assume no mass loss and constant coefficients over the interval; vessel coefficient is for capacity.
Other useful formulas
Overflow from a full vessel
\[ \Delta V_{\rm overflow}=V_0(\gamma_l-\gamma_v)\Delta T \]
Symbols and assumptions: V₀ initial liquid volume = vessel capacity (m³); γ_l liquid and γ_v vessel volume coefficients (K⁻¹), with γ_v = 3α for an isotropic vessel; ΔT common temperature rise (K). Small γΔT.
Upthrust on an immersed solid after heating
\[ F_B\approx F_{B0}\,[1+(\gamma_s-\gamma_l)\Delta T] \]
Symbols and assumptions: F_B, F_B0 upthrust after and before heating (N); γ_s solid and γ_l liquid volume coefficients (K⁻¹); ΔT temperature rise (K). Body fully immersed; small γΔT.
Watch the assumption

Use volume expansion for the vessel, not its linear coefficient without multiplying by three for an isotropic solid.

Worked example

A liquid has γ_l=8×10⁻⁴ K⁻¹ and a vessel has γ_v=3×10⁻⁵ K⁻¹. Find apparent coefficient.

Show solution

γ_app=0.0008−0.00003=7.7×10⁻⁴ K⁻¹. The vessel’s expansion hides part of the liquid’s true expansion.

Worked example · Numerical

A glass flask of volume 500 cm³ is filled to the brim with mercury at 20 °C. Both are heated to 100 °C. How much mercury overflows? Take \(\gamma_{\rm Hg}=1.82\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{K^{-1}}\) and \(\alpha_{\rm glass}=9\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{K^{-1}}\).

Show solution
  1. Vessel volume coefficient: \(\gamma_v=3\alpha=2.7\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{K^{-1}}\).
  2. Apparent coefficient: \(1.82\times10^{-4}-0.27\times10^{-4}=1.55\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{K^{-1}}\).
  3. Overflow \(=500\times1.55\times10^{-4}\times80=6.2\ \mathrm{cm^3}\).

Real and apparent expansion

Fill a flask of capacity \(V_0\) to the brim with a liquid, then heat both by \(\Delta T\).

  1. The liquid’s volume becomes \(V_0(1+\gamma_l\Delta T)\).
  2. The flask’s capacity becomes \(V_0(1+\gamma_v\Delta T)\), because the cavity expands like the glass.
  3. The overflow is the difference: \(\Delta V=V_0(\gamma_l-\gamma_v)\Delta T\). So the observed (apparent) coefficient is \(\gamma_{\rm app}=\gamma_l-\gamma_v\).

If \(\gamma_l>\gamma_v\) the level rises; if they are equal it stays put; if the vessel expands more, the level falls. When a flask is first dipped into hot water, the level dips for a moment: the glass warms and expands before heat reaches the liquid. Using two vessels of known coefficients, \(\gamma_l=\gamma_{\rm app,1}+\gamma_{v1}=\gamma_{\rm app,2}+\gamma_{v2}\) gives the true coefficient.

Density, upthrust and floating bodies

Mass is fixed, so \(\rho=\rho_0/(1+\gamma\Delta T)\). For a solid fully immersed in a liquid, upthrust is \(V_s\rho_lg\). On heating, \(V_s\) grows by \(\gamma_s\Delta T\) while \(\rho_l\) falls by \(\gamma_l\Delta T\):

\[ F_B\approx F_{B0}\,[1+(\gamma_s-\gamma_l)\Delta T]. \]

Liquids usually expand much more than solids, so upthrust decreases and the apparent weight of a sunken body increases on heating. A floating body sinks a little deeper in a warmer liquid, because the submerged volume \(m/\rho_l\) must grow.

Anomalous expansion of water and frozen lakes

  • From 0 °C to 4 °C, water contracts on warming. Its density is greatest, about 1000 kg/m³, near 4 °C. Above 4 °C it expands normally.
  • Ice (about 917 kg/m³) is less dense than water because hydrogen bonds hold the molecules in an open lattice. So ice floats.
  • In winter, surface water cools and sinks until the whole lake is near 4 °C. Further cooling makes the surface water lighter, so it stays on top and freezes there.
  • Ice is a poor conductor, so it insulates the water below. The bottom stays near 4 °C and aquatic life survives.
  • Water pipes burst in severe winters because water expands about 9 % on freezing. Water is also unsuitable as a thermometric liquid near 4 °C, since one volume corresponds to two temperatures.
Question patterns
  • Overflow volume when a full flask of mercury or oil is heated.
  • Find the true coefficient of a liquid, or the α of a vessel, from apparent expansion in two vessels.
  • Change in apparent weight or in the submerged fraction of a body when the liquid is heated.
  • Graph of volume or density of water against temperature from 0 to 10 °C.
  • Assertion–reason on why lakes freeze from the top and fish survive.
Another common mistake

Saying the water at the bottom of a frozen lake is at 0 °C. It stays near 4 °C, the temperature of maximum density.

Another common mistake

Assuming the upthrust on a sunken solid is unchanged on heating. The liquid’s density usually falls faster than the solid’s volume rises, so upthrust decreases.

Specific heat and mixing

Specific heat capacity is energy needed per unit mass for a unit temperature rise without a phase change. Different materials respond differently to the same heat input. Heat capacity applies to the whole sample rather than per kilogram.

For an insulated mixture, energy lost by hotter parts equals energy gained by colder parts, including the container if its heat capacity matters. Check for a phase change before assuming that all heat simply changes temperature.

Specific heat and mixing
\[ Q=mc\Delta T=C\Delta T,\qquad \sum Q_i=0\quad\text{(isolated mixture)} \]
Symbols and assumptions: Q heat added (J); m mass (kg); c specific heat (J/kg K); C=mc sample heat capacity (J/K); ΔT signed temperature change (K). Sum includes all bodies and phase-change terms when needed; neglect heat loss only if stated.
Other useful formulas
Final temperature of a mixture (no phase change)
\[ T_f=\frac{\sum_i m_ic_iT_i}{\sum_i m_ic_i} \]
Symbols and assumptions: m_i masses (kg); c_i specific heats (J/kg K); T_i initial temperatures (°C or K, same scale for all); T_f final common temperature. Insulated system, no phase change, no chemical reaction.
Water equivalent of a container
\[ W=\frac{m\,c}{c_w} \]
Symbols and assumptions: W water equivalent (kg); m container mass (kg); c its specific heat (J/kg K); c_w specific heat of water (J/kg K). Add W to the water mass in heat-balance equations.
Heat with temperature-dependent specific heat
\[ Q=\int_{T_1}^{T_2}m\,c(T)\,dT \]
Symbols and assumptions: Q heat supplied (J); m mass (kg); c(T) specific heat as a function of temperature (J/kg K); T₁, T₂ initial and final temperatures (K or °C consistently). No phase change in the range.
Watch the assumption

An average of temperatures is valid only for equal heat capacities and no phase change.

Show solution

Heat capacity, specific heat and water equivalent

  • Heat capacity \(C=Q/\Delta T\) belongs to a whole object (J/K).
  • Specific heat \(c=Q/(m\Delta T)\) belongs to a material (J/kg K).
  • Molar heat capacity \(C_m=Mc\) is per mole (J/mol K). For gases it depends on the process, which the thermodynamics chapter uses.
  • Water equivalent \(W=mc/c_w\) of a calorimeter is the mass of water that would need the same heat for the same temperature rise. Add it to the water mass in the heat balance.
  • Units: 1 cal = 4.186 J (often 4.2 J). Water: \(c_w=4186\ \mathrm{J/kg\,K}=1\ \mathrm{cal/g\,^\circ C}\). Ice: about 2100 J/kg K = 0.5 cal/g °C.

Water has an unusually large specific heat. It warms and cools slowly, which keeps coastal climates mild and makes it a good coolant in car radiators and hot-water bottles.

Method of mixtures as a weighted average

With no phase change and no heat loss, heat lost by the hot bodies equals heat gained by the cold ones. Solving gives

\[ T_f=\frac{\sum m_ic_iT_i}{\sum m_ic_i}. \]

So the final temperature is an average weighted by heat capacity. It always lies between the extreme starting temperatures, and closer to the body with the larger \(mc\). A simple average is correct only when the heat capacities are equal. Treat the calorimeter as one more body with its own \(mc\).

When specific heat changes with temperature

If \(c\) depends on \(T\), add up small steps: \(Q=\int_{T_1}^{T_2}m\,c(T)\,dT\). For \(c=a+bT\), this gives \(Q=m\left[a(T_2-T_1)+\tfrac b2\left(T_2^2-T_1^2\right)\right]\). The mean specific heat over the range is \(Q/(m\Delta T)\).

Latent heat and phase-change accounting

During a phase change at fixed pressure, energy can alter molecular arrangement without changing temperature. Specific latent heat is the energy per unit mass needed for the change. Melting and vaporisation have different latent heats.

In a mixed heating problem, calculate each stage separately: warm the initial phase, perform the phase change, then warm the new phase if energy remains. Insufficient heat may leave a two-phase mixture at the transition temperature.

Latent heat and phase-change accounting
\[ Q_{\rm phase}=mL,\qquad Q_{\rm total}=\sum mc\Delta T+\sum mL \]
Symbols and assumptions: m mass undergoing the stage (kg); L specific latent heat (J/kg); c stage-specific heat (J/kg K); Q energy (J). Phase-change signs are positive for melting/vaporisation and negative for freezing/condensation in heat-added accounting.
Other useful formulas
Heating curve with a constant-power heater
\[ \frac{dT}{dt}=\frac{P}{mc},\qquad t_{\rm plateau}=\frac{mL}{P} \]
Symbols and assumptions: dT/dt slope of a sloping part (K/s); P heater power absorbed by the sample (W); m mass (kg); c specific heat of the phase present (J/kg K); t_plateau duration of a flat part (s); L latent heat of that change (J/kg). No heat loss.
Standard latent heats of water at 1 atm
\[ L_f=3.36\times10^5\ \mathrm{J/kg}=80\ \mathrm{cal/g},\qquad L_v=2.26\times10^6\ \mathrm{J/kg}=540\ \mathrm{cal/g} \]
Symbols and assumptions: L_f latent heat of fusion of ice at 0 °C; L_v latent heat of vaporisation of water at 100 °C. Use the value the question gives if it differs slightly (for example 3.34×10⁵ J/kg).

Heating a mixture of ice and water at 0°C first melts ice rather than immediately raising its temperature.

Reading a heating curve

Heat a block of ice at −20 °C with a heater of constant power P and plot temperature against time (or heat supplied). The graph has five parts:

  1. Ice warms from −20 °C to 0 °C. Slope \(dT/dt=P/(mc_{\rm ice})\).
  2. Melting plateau at 0 °C. Ice and water coexist. Length \(=mL_f/P\).
  3. Water warms from 0 °C to 100 °C. Slope \(P/(mc_w)\), about half the ice slope because \(c_w\approx2c_{\rm ice}\).
  4. Boiling plateau at 100 °C. It is about 6.7 times longer than the melting plateau, because \(L_v/L_f=2.26\times10^6/3.36\times10^5\approx6.7\).
  5. Steam warms above 100 °C, again steeply because \(c_{\rm steam}\approx2000\ \mathrm{J/kg\,K}\).

Two rules read any heating curve: a steeper sloping part means a smaller heat capacity, and a longer flat part means a larger latent heat.

Where the energy goes during a phase change

During melting or boiling, the supplied energy pulls molecules apart against their attraction. Potential energy rises while average kinetic energy, and so temperature, stays the same. Freezing and condensation release the same latent heat back.

This is why steam burns are worse than boiling-water burns. One gram of steam at 100 °C gives 540 cal as it condenses and up to 100 cal more as the water cools to 0 °C: 640 cal in total, against 100 cal from water at 100 °C.

Effect of pressure on melting and boiling points

  • Boiling point rises with pressure. A pressure cooker at about 2 atm boils water near 120 °C, so food cooks faster. At high altitude water boils below 100 °C and food cooks slowly.
  • The melting point of ice falls when pressure rises, because ice shrinks on melting. A loaded wire passes slowly through an ice block, and the ice refreezes above it (regelation).
  • For most substances, such as wax, the solid is denser than the liquid, so their melting point rises with pressure.
  • Some solids, like camphor and dry ice, change straight to vapour at ordinary pressure (sublimation). At the triple point of water (273.16 K, about 611 Pa) all three phases coexist.

Mixing ice, water and steam: finding the final state

When ice, water or steam are mixed, you cannot write one heat-balance equation straight away. You first need to know which phases remain at the end. The safe method compares the heat the hot part can give with the heat the cold part needs to reach the transition temperature and change phase completely.

Before solving for a final temperature, check whether the available heat is enough to complete the phase change. If ice remains in equilibrium with water, the temperature stays at 0°C. Account for melting or condensation before using a mixing formula.

Mixing ice, water and steam: finding the final state
\[ Q_{\rm avail}=m_wc_w(T_w-0),\qquad Q_{\rm need}=m_ic_i(0-T_i)+m_iL_f \]
Symbols and assumptions: Q_avail heat the water can give while cooling to 0 °C (J); Q_need heat the ice needs to warm to 0 °C and melt completely (J); m_w, m_i masses (kg); c_w, c_i specific heats (J/kg K); T_w water temperature and T_i ice temperature (°C, T_i ≤ 0); L_f latent heat of fusion (J/kg). Insulated container of negligible heat capacity unless stated.
Other useful formulas
Steam needed to just melt ice
\[ m_s\,(L_v+c_w\times100)=m_iL_f \]
Symbols and assumptions: m_s mass of steam at 100 °C (kg); m_i mass of ice at 0 °C (kg); L_v, L_f latent heats of vaporisation and fusion (J/kg); c_w specific heat of water (J/kg K); 100 is the temperature drop in K. Final state all water at 0 °C, no losses.
Mass of ice melted when not all of it melts
\[ m_{\rm melt}=\frac{Q_{\rm avail}-m_ic_i(0-T_i)}{L_f} \]
Symbols and assumptions: m_melt mass of ice that melts (kg); Q_avail heat the hot water gives in cooling to 0 °C (J); m_i, c_i, T_i mass, specific heat and starting temperature of the ice; L_f latent heat of fusion (J/kg). Valid when 0 ≤ m_melt ≤ m_i; the final temperature is then 0 °C.
Watch the assumption

Writing m_wc_w(T_w − T) = m_iL_f + m_ic_wT before checking that all the ice melts. If the hot water cannot supply enough heat, this equation gives a negative T, which is impossible with ice still present.

Worked example

100 g of ice at 0 °C is mixed with 100 g of water at 80 °C in an insulated container. Find the final state (c_w = 1 cal/g °C, L_f = 80 cal/g).

Show solution

The water can give 100 × 1 × 80 = 8000 cal while cooling to 0 °C. Melting all the ice needs 100 × 80 = 8000 cal. These are exactly equal, so all the ice just melts and the final state is 200 g of water at 0 °C.

Steam in the mix

One gram of steam at 100 °C gives 540 cal on condensing and up to 100 cal more on cooling to 0 °C: 640 cal in total. One gram of ice at 0 °C needs 80 cal to melt. So 1 g of steam can melt 8 g of ice, leaving 9 g of water at 0 °C.

For steam + cold water, check the other end. The water can absorb at most \(m_wc_w(100-T_w)\) before it reaches 100 °C. If the steam’s latent heat is larger than that, only part of the steam condenses and the final temperature is 100 °C.

Can a mixture end below 0 °C?

Yes, if the ice is very cold and there is little water. Example: 100 g of ice at −50 °C with 10 g of water at 0 °C (\(c_{\rm ice}=0.5\) cal/g °C).

  • Warming all the ice to 0 °C needs \(100\times0.5\times50=2500\) cal.
  • Freezing all the water releases only \(10\times80=800\) cal. So all the water freezes.
  • Now 110 g of ice settles at T: \(800+10\times0.5\times(0-T)=100\times0.5\times(T+50)\), giving \(T\approx-30.9\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}\).

Conduction and thermal resistance

Conduction transfers energy through a material without bulk motion of the material. In a steady uniform slab, heat flows from the hot face to the cold face at a rate proportional to area and temperature difference and inversely proportional to thickness.

Thermal resistance makes composite slabs easier to analyse. Series layers carry the same heat rate and their temperature drops add, so their resistances add. Parallel paths share the temperature difference and their heat rates add.

Conduction and thermal resistance
\[ \dot Q=\frac{\kappa A(T_h-T_c)}{L},\qquad R_{th}=\frac{L}{\kappa A},\qquad R_{\rm series}=\sum R_i \]
Symbols and assumptions: Q̇ heat-transfer rate (W); κ conductivity (W/m K); A cross-sectional area (m²); L thickness (m); T_h/T_c face temperatures (K or consistent °C differences); R_th resistance (K/W). Assume steady one-dimensional conduction, constant κ and negligible side losses.
Other useful formulas
Equivalent conductivity of composite slabs
\[ \kappa_{\rm series}=\frac{L_1+L_2}{L_1/\kappa_1+L_2/\kappa_2},\qquad \kappa_{\rm parallel}=\frac{\kappa_1A_1+\kappa_2A_2}{A_1+A_2} \]
Symbols and assumptions: κ₁, κ₂ conductivities (W/m K); L₁, L₂ thicknesses in series (m), equal cross-section; A₁, A₂ areas side by side (m²), equal length. Steady state, no side losses.
Junction temperature of two slabs in series
\[ T_J=\frac{\frac{\kappa_1}{L_1}T_1+\frac{\kappa_2}{L_2}T_2}{\frac{\kappa_1}{L_1}+\frac{\kappa_2}{L_2}} \]
Symbols and assumptions: T_J junction temperature; T₁, T₂ outer face temperatures (°C or K consistently); κ conductivities (W/m K); L thicknesses (m). Equal areas, steady state.
Time for lake ice to thicken
\[ t=\frac{\rho L}{2\kappa\theta}\left(x_2^2-x_1^2\right) \]
Symbols and assumptions: t time (s); ρ density of ice (kg/m³); L latent heat of fusion (J/kg); κ conductivity of ice (W/m K); θ magnitude of air temperature below 0 °C (K); x₁, x₂ initial and final ice thickness (m). Water below at 0 °C; steady conduction at each instant.

Steady state and the temperature profile

Fourier’s law in differential form is \(H=\frac{dQ}{dt}=-\kappa A\frac{dT}{dx}\). The minus sign says heat flows down the temperature gradient. When one end of a rod is first heated, each part absorbs heat to warm up. Later the temperatures stop changing: this is the steady state. Then no part stores heat, so the same heat current H crosses every cross-section. For a uniform rod the temperature then falls linearly along its length.

Conduction behaves like current in a resistor:

Heat conductionElectric current
Heat current H (W)Current I (A)
Temperature difference ΔT (K)Potential difference V (V)
\(R_{th}=L/(\kappa A)\) (K/W)\(R=\rho L/A\) (Ω)
\(H=\Delta T/R_{th}\)\(I=V/R\)

κ has unit W m⁻¹ K⁻¹ and dimensions [M L T⁻³ K⁻¹].

Composite slabs: series and parallel

Series (layers one after another): the same H flows through each layer and the temperature drops add. So \(R=R_1+R_2\). The larger drop occurs across the layer with the larger resistance, which for equal L and A is the poorer conductor. The equivalent conductivity is

\[ \kappa_{\rm eq}=\frac{L_1+L_2}{L_1/\kappa_1+L_2/\kappa_2},\qquad \kappa_{\rm eq}=\frac{2\kappa_1\kappa_2}{\kappa_1+\kappa_2}\ \text{for equal thickness.} \]

Parallel (side by side between the same two faces): each path has the same ΔT and the heat currents add. So \(1/R=1/R_1+1/R_2\) and, for equal lengths, \(\kappa_{\rm eq}=\frac{\kappa_1A_1+\kappa_2A_2}{A_1+A_2}\).

Two identical rods carry 4 times as much heat side by side as end to end, because the resistance changes from 2R to R/2.

Growth of ice on a lake

Air above a lake is at −θ °C. A layer of ice of thickness x has formed; the water below it is at 0 °C.

  1. Heat conducted up through the ice in time dt: \(\frac{\kappa A\theta}{x}\,dt\).
  2. This heat comes from water freezing at the bottom of the ice. A new layer dx has mass \(\rho A\,dx\) and releases \(\rho A L\,dx\).
  3. Equate: \(\frac{\kappa A\theta}{x}dt=\rho AL\,dx\), so \(dt=\frac{\rho L}{\kappa\theta}x\,dx\).
  4. Integrate: \(t=\frac{\rho L}{2\kappa\theta}\left(x_2^2-x_1^2\right)\).

Time grows as the square of thickness. Going from 0 to 2x takes four times as long as 0 to x, so 2x after x takes three times as long. Thick ice insulates the water and slows further freezing.

Ingenhousz’s experiment: identical rods coated with wax are dipped into hot water. In the steady state, the length along which wax melts satisfies \(\ell^2\propto\kappa\), so \(\kappa_1/\kappa_2=\ell_1^2/\ell_2^2\).

9 Basics

Convection and thermal radiation

Convection transfers heat through moving fluid: heated fluid often becomes less dense and rises under gravity, while cooler fluid replaces it. Radiation transfers energy electromagnetically and can operate through vacuum.

All bodies emit thermal radiation; a cooler environment also radiates toward the body. Net radiative loss subtracts the incoming contribution. A black body is an ideal perfect absorber and emitter, while emissivity describes departures from that ideal.

Convection and thermal radiation
\[ P_{\rm net}=\varepsilon\sigma A(T^4-T_s^4),\qquad \lambda_{\max}T=b \]
Symbols and assumptions: P_net outward radiative power (W); ε emissivity (0 to 1); σ Stefan–Boltzmann constant (W/m² K⁴); A radiating area (m²); T body and T_s surrounding absolute temperatures (K); λ_max peak wavelength (m); b Wien constant (m K). The net formula assumes large uniform surroundings and applicable grey-body emissivity.
Other useful formulas
Convective heat loss (approximate)
\[ H=hA\,(T-T_0) \]
Symbols and assumptions: H heat loss rate (W); h convective heat-transfer coefficient (W/m² K), set by the fluid and its speed; A exposed area (m²); T surface temperature and T₀ fluid temperature far away (K or °C differences). Empirical; valid for moderate temperature differences.
Watch the assumption

Fourth-power radiation and Wien’s law require kelvin, never Celsius.

Emitted power scales as T⁴, increasing sixteenfold. Peak wavelength scales as 1/T, halving. Net power must also account for surroundings.

How convection currents form

Heat a pan of water from below. The bottom layer warms, expands and becomes less dense. Buoyancy lifts it, and cooler, denser water sinks to take its place. The result is a circulating current that carries energy with the moving fluid. This is natural convection.

Natural convection needs gravity, because buoyancy needs weight. In an orbiting spacecraft or a freely falling lift there is no natural convection: a candle flame there becomes a dim sphere, starved of fresh air. Forced convection uses a fan or pump instead: a room fan, a car’s radiator coolant, blood pumped by the heart.

The rate of convective loss from a surface is roughly \(H=hA(T-T_0)\), where h depends on the fluid and how fast it moves. This linear form is one basis of Newton’s law of cooling.

Convection in daily life and nature

  • Sea breeze (day): land heats faster than the sea because of its lower specific heat. Air over the land rises and cooler air from the sea flows in along the surface.
  • Land breeze (night): land cools faster, so the sea is warmer. The flow reverses and air moves from land to sea.
  • Trade winds and monsoons: large-scale convection driven by unequal heating of the Earth.
  • Heating elements sit at the bottom of a kettle. Freezers and air conditioners sit high up, so cooled air sinks through the space.
  • A chimney works because hot gases inside are lighter than outside air.

Radiation laws: Stefan–Boltzmann, Wien and Kirchhoff

Every body above absolute zero emits electromagnetic radiation, and the amount rises steeply with temperature. A perfect black body absorbs all radiation that falls on it and, at a given temperature, emits the most any body can. Real surfaces emit a fraction ε, the emissivity, of the black-body amount.

Use kelvin for radiation laws. Stefan–Boltzmann gives total emitted power; Wien gives the peak wavelength. For net heat loss, subtract the radiation absorbed from the surroundings rather than using emitted power alone.

Radiation laws: Stefan–Boltzmann, Wien and Kirchhoff
\[ E=\varepsilon\sigma T^4,\quad P_{\rm net}=\varepsilon\sigma A\,(T^4-T_0^4),\quad \lambda_mT=b \]
Symbols and assumptions: E power emitted per unit area (W/m²); ε emissivity (0 to 1; 1 for a black body); σ = 5.67×10⁻⁸ W m⁻² K⁻⁴; T body and T₀ surroundings temperatures (K); A surface area (m²); λ_m wavelength of peak emission (m); b ≈ 2.9×10⁻³ m K (Wien constant). Kelvin is essential.
Other useful formulas
Kirchhoff’s law
\[ \frac{E_\lambda}{a_\lambda}=E_{\lambda,\rm black},\qquad \varepsilon=a \]
Symbols and assumptions: E_λ emissive power of a body at wavelength λ (W/m² per m); a_λ its absorptivity at λ (0 to 1); E_λ,black black-body emissive power at the same λ and T; ε emissivity and a absorptivity of a grey surface. Same temperature for the comparison.
Rate of cooling by radiation
\[ -\frac{dT}{dt}=\frac{\varepsilon\sigma A}{mc}\left(T^4-T_0^4\right) \]
Symbols and assumptions: dT/dt rate of change of temperature (K/s); ε emissivity; σ Stefan–Boltzmann constant (W m⁻² K⁻⁴); A area (m²); m mass (kg); c specific heat (J/kg K); T, T₀ body and surroundings temperatures (K). Uniform body temperature; radiation only.
Luminosity of a star
\[ L=4\pi R^2\sigma T^4 \]
Symbols and assumptions: L total power radiated (W); R radius of the star (m); σ Stefan–Boltzmann constant; T surface temperature (K). Star treated as a spherical black body.
Watch the assumption

Using Celsius in T⁴. Heating a black body from 27 °C to 327 °C multiplies its emitted power by (600/300)⁴ = 16, not by (327/27)⁴.

Worked example

Show solution

T = b/λ_m = 2.9×10⁻³ / 4.8×10⁻⁷ ≈ 6.0×10³ K. This is close to the surface temperature of the Sun.

Absorptivity, emissivity and Kirchhoff’s law

Radiation falling on a surface is partly absorbed (fraction a), reflected (r) and transmitted (t), with \(a+r+t=1\). A black body has \(a=1\). Emissivity \(\varepsilon\) compares a surface’s emission with a black body’s at the same temperature.

Kirchhoff’s law: at a given temperature and wavelength, the ratio of emissive power to absorptivity is the same for all bodies and equals the black-body emissive power. So \(\varepsilon=a\): good absorbers are good emitters, and good reflectors are poor emitters.

  • A plate with a black pattern on white china, heated in a dark room, shows the black pattern glowing brighter.
  • A thermos flask has silvered walls: they reflect radiation and emit little.
  • Light-coloured clothes are cooler in summer because they absorb less sunlight.
  • A small hole in a blackened cavity behaves as a near-perfect black body (Ferry’s black body). Inside a furnace, all objects glow alike and their details vanish.

Stefan–Boltzmann law and the rate of cooling

A body emits \(\varepsilon\sigma AT^4\) and absorbs \(\varepsilon\sigma AT_0^4\) from surroundings at \(T_0\). Even at \(T=T_0\) it still emits; it simply absorbs the same amount, so the net power is zero (Prevost’s theory of exchanges).

The rate of fall of temperature is \(-\frac{dT}{dt}=\frac{\varepsilon\sigma A}{mc}(T^4-T_0^4)\). Two common comparisons:

  • Solid spheres of the same material at the same T: \(A/m=\frac{4\pi r^2}{\rho\cdot\frac43\pi r^3}=\frac{3}{\rho r}\), so the cooling rate \(\propto1/r\). The smaller sphere cools faster.
  • A hollow and a solid sphere of the same radius and material: same emission at first, but the hollow one has less mass, so it cools faster.

A star of radius R radiates a total power (luminosity) \(L=4\pi R^2\sigma T^4\). For a small excess, \(T^4-T_0^4\approx4T_0^3(T-T_0)\), which leads to Newton’s law of cooling in the next section.

Wien’s law and the shape of the spectrum

Plot the power emitted per unit wavelength against wavelength (see the figure). At a higher temperature:

  • the curve lies higher at every wavelength, so curves at different temperatures never cross;
  • the peak moves to a shorter wavelength, \(\lambda_m\propto1/T\);
  • the area under the curve, which is the total power per unit area, grows as \(T^4\).

So heated iron glows dull red, then orange, then yellow-white. Blue stars are hotter than red stars. The Sun peaks near 500 nm, giving about 5800 K. The human body at 310 K peaks near 9.4 μm, in the infrared, which is why thermal cameras work in the infrared.

Newton’s cooling law and measurement

For a small temperature excess under approximately constant heat-transfer conditions, the heat-loss rate is proportional to the excess above the surroundings. As the object cools, the excess decreases and cooling slows.

Combine the proportional loss rate with the sample’s heat capacity to obtain an exponential decay of temperature excess. The approximation can come from convection or a small-excess expansion of radiation, but is unreliable for very large temperature differences or changing surroundings.

Newton’s cooling law and measurement
\[ \frac{dT}{dt}=-\lambda(T-T_s),\qquad T-T_s=(T_0-T_s)e^{-\lambda t} \]
Symbols and assumptions: T/T₀ current/initial temperature and T_s constant surroundings temperature (K or °C with differences); t time (s); λ positive cooling constant (s⁻¹) incorporating heat transfer and heat capacity. Assumes small excess and constant conditions.
Other useful formulas
Newton’s law: average-temperature form
\[ \frac{T_1-T_2}{t}=K\left[\frac{T_1+T_2}{2}-T_0\right] \]
Symbols and assumptions: T₁, T₂ temperatures at the start and end of the interval (°C or K); t time taken (s or min); T₀ surroundings temperature; K cooling constant (per unit time) for that body. Small excess, constant surroundings.
Newton’s law: exact form
\[ \ln\frac{T_1-T_0}{T_2-T_0}=kt \]
Symbols and assumptions: T₁, T₂ temperatures at the start and end; T₀ surroundings temperature; k cooling constant (s⁻¹); t time (s). From dT/dt = −k(T − T₀) with constant k.
Comparing specific heats by cooling
\[ \frac{m_1c_1+W}{t_1}=\frac{m_2c_2+W}{t_2} \]
Symbols and assumptions: m₁, m₂ masses of the two liquids (kg); c₁, c₂ specific heats (J/kg K); W heat capacity of the identical calorimeters (J/K); t₁, t₂ times to cool through the same temperature range (s). Same surface, same surroundings.
Watch the assumption

Equal successive time intervals produce equal fractional decreases in excess temperature, not equal temperature drops.

Worked example

An object’s excess temperature halves every 5 minutes. Starting 40°C above surroundings, what excess remains after 10 minutes?

Show solution

Two halving intervals leave 40×(1/2)²=10°C above surroundings. Add the surroundings temperature only if actual temperature is requested.

Show solution

  2. Second interval: \(\frac{50-40}{t}=\frac{1}{30}\left(45-25\right)=\frac{2}{3}\).

From Stefan’s law to Newton’s law

A body at T in surroundings at \(T_0\) loses heat by radiation at the net rate \(\varepsilon\sigma A(T^4-T_0^4)\).

  1. Factorise: \(T^4-T_0^4=(T^2+T_0^2)(T+T_0)(T-T_0)\).
  2. If T is close to \(T_0\), the first two brackets are about \(2T_0^2\times2T_0=4T_0^3\).
  3. So the loss rate is about \(4\varepsilon\sigma AT_0^3\,(T-T_0)\): proportional to the excess temperature.
  4. Divide by the heat capacity mc: \(\frac{dT}{dt}=-k(T-T_0)\) with \(k=\frac{4\varepsilon\sigma AT_0^3}{mc}\).

So k is larger for a larger surface and a smaller heat capacity. The law holds only for small excess temperatures (a few tens of kelvin), though forced convection also gives a roughly linear loss.

Exact form and average-temperature form

Exact form. Integrating gives \(\ln\frac{T_1-T_0}{T_2-T_0}=kt\). Equal time intervals give equal fractional drops in the excess, as in the figure.

Average form (the one used in most NEET problems). Replace the changing rate by its value at the mean temperature of the interval:

\[ \frac{T_1-T_2}{t}=K\left[\frac{T_1+T_2}{2}-T_0\right]. \]

The two forms agree closely when the drop \(T_1-T_2\) is small compared with the excess. Use the average form unless the question asks for the logarithmic one or gives ln values.

Cooling graphs and comparing specific heats

  • T against t: an exponential decay that approaches \(T_0\) and never crosses it. Steepest at the start.
  • \(\ln(T-T_0)\) against t: a straight line of slope −k.
  • Rate of cooling against \((T-T_0)\): a straight line through the origin.
  • Rate of cooling against T: a straight line that meets the T axis at \(T_0\).

Specific heat by cooling. Put equal volumes of water and another liquid in identical calorimeters and let them cool through the same range. Same surface and same temperatures give the same rate of heat loss, so \(\frac{m_1c_1+W}{t_1}=\frac{m_2c_2+W}{t_2}\), where W is the calorimeter’s heat capacity.

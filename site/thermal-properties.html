Thermal Properties of Matter

Separate temperature from heat, then explain expansion, calorimetry, phase changes and heat transfer. Use absolute temperature whenever a law requires it.

1 Core

Thermal equilibrium and temperature scales

Temperature tells whether bodies in thermal contact exchange energy as heat. Thermal equilibrium means no net heat transfer between them. The zeroth law makes a thermometer possible: bodies separately in equilibrium with a third are in equilibrium with one another.

Heat is energy in transfer because of temperature difference, not a substance stored in a body. Kelvin and Celsius have equal interval sizes but different zeros. Gas laws and radiation formulas require kelvin.

Thermal equilibrium and temperature scales
\[ T_K=t_C+273.15,\qquad \frac{t_F-32}{9}=\frac{t_C}{5} \]
Symbols and assumptions: T_K absolute temperature (K); t_C Celsius reading (°C); t_F Fahrenheit reading (°F). A 1 K temperature interval equals 1 °C; offsets apply to temperatures, not to temperature differences.
Watch the assumption

20°C is not twice the absolute temperature of 10°C.

Worked example

Convert 27°C to kelvin and a rise of 10°C to kelvin units.

Show solution

The temperature is 300.15 K (often 300 K in rounded problems). The temperature rise is 10 K, without adding 273.15.

Check the idea

2 Core

Linear, area and volume expansion

Heating usually increases average separation between particles and expands a solid. Expansion coefficients compare fractional size change with temperature change. For an isotropic solid and small expansion, each direction expands similarly.

Area depends on two lengths and volume on three, producing β≈2α and γ≈3α to first order. A hole in a plate expands as if it were filled with the same material; heating does not make the surrounding material grow inward into it. A bonded bimetallic strip bends because its layers want different lengths: the more-expanding layer becomes the longer outer, convex side.

Linear, area and volume expansion
\[ \Delta L=\alpha L_0\Delta T,\quad \Delta A=\beta A_0\Delta T,\quad \Delta V=\gamma V_0\Delta T,\quad \beta\approx2\alpha,\quad\gamma\approx3\alpha \]
Symbols and assumptions: L₀/A₀/V₀ initial length (m), area (m²), volume (m³); α/β/γ linear/area/volume coefficients (K⁻¹); ΔT temperature change (K); Δ denotes change. Relations assume isotropy, approximately constant coefficients and small fractional changes.
Watch the assumption

Large temperature ranges can require temperature-dependent coefficients; the simple linear model is approximate.

Worked example

A 2 m rod with α=12×10⁻⁶ K⁻¹ is heated by 50 K. Find length increase.

Show solution

ΔL=12×10⁻⁶×2×50=0.0012 m=1.2 mm. The final length is 2.0012 m.

Check the idea

3 Core

Apparent expansion, density and anomalous water

A liquid in a vessel expands along with the vessel. The observed rise reflects the liquid’s volume expansion minus the vessel’s capacity expansion. Therefore apparent expansion is not the liquid’s true expansion coefficient.

Mass remains constant during ordinary thermal expansion, so density changes inversely with volume. Water behaves unusually between 0°C and 4°C: warming it contracts it, and its density is greatest near 4°C.

Apparent expansion, density and anomalous water
\[ \gamma_{\rm app}=\gamma_l-\gamma_v,\qquad \rho_T=\frac{\rho_0}{1+\gamma_l\Delta T} \]
Symbols and assumptions: γ_app apparent, γ_l liquid and γ_v vessel volumetric coefficients (K⁻¹); ρ₀ and ρ_T initial/final densities (kg/m³); ΔT temperature change (K). Assume no mass loss and constant coefficients over the interval; vessel coefficient is for capacity.
Watch the assumption

Use volume expansion for the vessel, not its linear coefficient without multiplying by three for an isotropic solid.

Worked example

A liquid has γ_l=8×10⁻⁴ K⁻¹ and a vessel has γ_v=3×10⁻⁵ K⁻¹. Find apparent coefficient.

Show solution

γ_app=0.0008−0.00003=7.7×10⁻⁴ K⁻¹. The vessel’s expansion hides part of the liquid’s true expansion.

Check the idea

4 Core

Specific heat and mixing

Specific heat capacity is energy needed per unit mass for a unit temperature rise without a phase change. Different materials respond differently to the same heat input. Heat capacity applies to the whole sample rather than per kilogram.

For an insulated mixture, energy lost by hotter parts equals energy gained by colder parts, including the container if its heat capacity matters. Check for a phase change before assuming that all heat simply changes temperature.

Specific heat and mixing
\[ Q=mc\Delta T=C\Delta T,\qquad \sum Q_i=0\quad\text{(isolated mixture)} \]
Symbols and assumptions: Q heat added (J); m mass (kg); c specific heat (J/kg K); C=mc sample heat capacity (J/K); ΔT signed temperature change (K). Sum includes all bodies and phase-change terms when needed; neglect heat loss only if stated.
Watch the assumption

An average of temperatures is valid only for equal heat capacities and no phase change.

Worked example

Mix 0.2 kg of water at 80°C with 0.3 kg at 20°C in an insulated negligible-capacity container. Find final temperature.

Show solution

Common specific heat cancels: 0.2(80−T)=0.3(T−20). Thus 16−0.2T=0.3T−6 and T=44°C.

Check the idea

5 Core

Latent heat and phase-change accounting

During a phase change at fixed pressure, energy can alter molecular arrangement without changing temperature. Specific latent heat is the energy per unit mass needed for the change. Melting and vaporisation have different latent heats.

In a mixed heating problem, calculate each stage separately: warm the initial phase, perform the phase change, then warm the new phase if energy remains. Insufficient heat may leave a two-phase mixture at the transition temperature.

Latent heat and phase-change accounting
\[ Q_{\rm phase}=mL,\qquad Q_{\rm total}=\sum mc\Delta T+\sum mL \]
Symbols and assumptions: m mass undergoing the stage (kg); L specific latent heat (J/kg); c stage-specific heat (J/kg K); Q energy (J). Phase-change signs are positive for melting/vaporisation and negative for freezing/condensation in heat-added accounting.
Watch the assumption

Heating a mixture of ice and water at 0°C first melts ice rather than immediately raising its temperature.

Worked example

How much heat melts 0.10 kg of ice at 0°C, with L_f=3.34×10⁵ J/kg?

Show solution

Q=0.10×3.34×10⁵=3.34×10⁴ J. This only melts the ice; warming the resulting water requires additional heat.

Check the idea

6 Core

Conduction and thermal resistance

Conduction transfers energy through a material without bulk motion of the material. In a steady uniform slab, heat flows from the hot face to the cold face at a rate proportional to area and temperature difference and inversely proportional to thickness.

Thermal resistance makes composite slabs easier to analyse. Series layers carry the same heat rate and their temperature drops add, so their resistances add. Parallel paths share the temperature difference and their heat rates add.

Conduction and thermal resistance
\[ \dot Q=\frac{\kappa A(T_h-T_c)}{L},\qquad R_{th}=\frac{L}{\kappa A},\qquad R_{\rm series}=\sum R_i \]
Symbols and assumptions: Q̇ heat-transfer rate (W); κ conductivity (W/m K); A cross-sectional area (m²); L thickness (m); T_h/T_c face temperatures (K or consistent °C differences); R_th resistance (K/W). Assume steady one-dimensional conduction, constant κ and negligible side losses.
Watch the assumption

Conductivity is a material property; conductance κA/L also depends on geometry.

Worked example

A slab has κ=0.5 W/m K, A=2 m², L=0.10 m and temperature difference 20 K. Find heat rate.

Show solution

Q̇=0.5×2×20/0.10=200 W. Doubling the thickness halves this rate.

Check the idea

7 Core

Convection and thermal radiation

Convection transfers heat through moving fluid: heated fluid often becomes less dense and rises under gravity, while cooler fluid replaces it. Radiation transfers energy electromagnetically and can operate through vacuum.

All bodies emit thermal radiation; a cooler environment also radiates toward the body. Net radiative loss subtracts the incoming contribution. A black body is an ideal perfect absorber and emitter, while emissivity describes departures from that ideal.

Convection and thermal radiation
\[ P_{\rm net}=\varepsilon\sigma A(T^4-T_s^4),\qquad \lambda_{\max}T=b \]
Symbols and assumptions: P_net outward radiative power (W); ε emissivity (0 to 1); σ Stefan–Boltzmann constant (W/m² K⁴); A radiating area (m²); T body and T_s surrounding absolute temperatures (K); λ_max peak wavelength (m); b Wien constant (m K). The net formula assumes large uniform surroundings and applicable grey-body emissivity.
Watch the assumption

Fourth-power radiation and Wien’s law require kelvin, never Celsius.

Worked example

An ideal black body’s temperature doubles. How do emitted power per area and peak wavelength change?

Show solution

Emitted power scales as T⁴, increasing sixteenfold. Peak wavelength scales as 1/T, halving. Net power must also account for surroundings.

Check the idea

8 Core

Newton’s cooling law and measurement

For a small temperature excess under approximately constant heat-transfer conditions, the heat-loss rate is proportional to the excess above the surroundings. As the object cools, the excess decreases and cooling slows.

Combine the proportional loss rate with the sample’s heat capacity to obtain an exponential decay of temperature excess. The approximation can come from convection or a small-excess expansion of radiation, but is unreliable for very large temperature differences or changing surroundings.

Newton’s cooling law and measurement
\[ \frac{dT}{dt}=-\lambda(T-T_s),\qquad T-T_s=(T_0-T_s)e^{-\lambda t} \]
Symbols and assumptions: T/T₀ current/initial temperature and T_s constant surroundings temperature (K or °C with differences); t time (s); λ positive cooling constant (s⁻¹) incorporating heat transfer and heat capacity. Assumes small excess and constant conditions.
Watch the assumption

Equal successive time intervals produce equal fractional decreases in excess temperature, not equal temperature drops.

Worked example

An object’s excess temperature halves every 5 minutes. Starting 40°C above surroundings, what excess remains after 10 minutes?

Show solution

Two halving intervals leave 40×(1/2)²=10°C above surroundings. Add the surroundings temperature only if actual temperature is requested.

Check the idea

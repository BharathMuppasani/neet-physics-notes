1 Basics

Physical quantities, SI units and conversion

A physical quantity combines a numerical value and a unit. The number changes when the unit changes, while the measured quantity does not. SI uses seven base quantities: length, mass, time, electric current, temperature, amount of substance and luminous intensity.

Derived units are combinations of base units. Convert each factor before substituting into a formula: cm² carries a squared conversion factor, and a cubic centimetre is a millionth of a cubic metre.

Physical quantities, SI units and conversion
\[ 1\,\mathrm{N}=1\,\mathrm{kg\,m\,s^{-2}},\quad 1\,\mathrm{J}=1\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2\,s^{-2}},\quad 1\,\mathrm{km\,h^{-1}}=\frac5{18}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}} \]
Symbols and assumptions: N is newton (force); J is joule (energy); kg, m and s are SI mass, length and time units. Kelvin, ampere, mole and candela are the other base units.
Other useful formulas
Astronomical distance units
\[ 1\ \text{ly}=9.46\times10^{15}\ \text{m},\quad 1\ \text{AU}=1.496\times10^{11}\ \text{m},\quad 1\ \text{pc}=3.08\times10^{16}\ \text{m}=3.26\ \text{ly} \]
Symbols and assumptions: ly is the light year (distance light travels in one year), AU is the mean Earth–Sun distance, pc is the parsec (distance at which 1 AU subtends 1 arc second). All three are lengths.
Energy units
\[ 1\ \text{kWh}=3.6\times10^{6}\ \text{J},\quad 1\ \text{eV}=1.6\times10^{-19}\ \text{J},\quad 1\ \text{erg}=10^{-7}\ \text{J} \]
Symbols and assumptions: kWh is the commercial unit of electrical energy; eV is the energy gained by an electron through 1 V; erg is the CGS unit of energy.
Watch the assumption

Convert areas and volumes with the square or cube of the length conversion, respectively.

Worked example

A density is 2.7 g/cm³. Convert it to kg/m³.

Show solution

One gram is 10⁻³ kg and one cm³ is 10⁻⁶ m³. Thus the density is 2.7×10³ = 2700 kg/m³.

Worked example · Numerical

Express 1 kWh in joules and in electron-volts.

Show solution
  1. 1 kW = 1000 W = 1000 J/s and 1 h = 3600 s.
  2. 1 kWh = 1000 × 3600 = \(3.6\times10^{6}\) J.
  3. In eV: \(\dfrac{3.6\times10^{6}}{1.6\times10^{-19}}=2.25\times10^{25}\) eV.

Answer: \(3.6\times10^{6}\) J = \(2.25\times10^{25}\) eV

Worked example · Concept

Which of these is not a unit of energy: erg, electron-volt, kilowatt-hour, watt?

Show solution
  1. erg is the CGS energy unit; eV and kWh are energy units too.
  2. The watt is J/s, a rate of energy transfer. It is a unit of power.

Answer: Watt

Worked example · Numerical

How many astronomical units make one light year?

Show solution
  1. \(\dfrac{1\ \text{ly}}{1\ \text{AU}}=\dfrac{9.46\times10^{15}}{1.496\times10^{11}}\).
  2. This is about \(6.3\times10^{4}\).

2 Basics

Measuring large and small distances: parallax

Very large distances cannot be measured with a tape. Instead, view the object from two points a known distance \(b\) apart. The lines of sight meet at the object at a small angle \(\theta\), the parallax angle. Then the distance is \(D=b/\theta\).

For a small parallax angle, divide the baseline by the angle in radians to estimate distance. For apparent size, multiply distance by angular size in radians. A degree value must be converted before substitution.

Measuring large and small distances: parallax
\[ D=\frac{b}{\theta},\qquad d=\alpha D \]
Symbols and assumptions: D is the distance to the object (m), b the baseline (m), θ the parallax angle (rad); d is the size of the object (m) and α its angular diameter (rad). Valid when θ and α are small (D ≫ b).
Other useful formulas
Echo and film thickness
\[ D=\frac{vt}{2},\qquad t_{\text{film}}=\frac{V}{A} \]
Symbols and assumptions: v is the pulse speed (m/s) and t the round-trip time (s); V is the volume of oleic acid in the film (m³) and A the film area (m²).
Angle conversions
\[ 1^\circ=\frac{\pi}{180}\ \text{rad},\quad 1^{\prime\prime}=\frac{\pi}{180\times3600}\ \text{rad}\approx4.85\times10^{-6}\ \text{rad} \]
Symbols and assumptions: ° is degree, ″ is arc second (1/3600 of a degree). Parallax formulas need radians.
Watch the assumption

The angles must be in radians. Convert first: \(1^\circ=\pi/180\) rad, \(1^{\prime}=2.91\times10^{-4}\) rad and \(1^{\prime\prime}=4.85\times10^{-6}\) rad.

Worked example

The Moon is viewed from two points on opposite sides of the Earth (baseline = Earth's diameter, \(1.276\times10^{7}\) m). The parallax angle is 1°54'. Find the Moon's distance.

Show solution

\(\theta=1.9^\circ=1.9\times\pi/180=3.32\times10^{-2}\) rad. \(D=b/\theta=1.276\times10^{7}/3.32\times10^{-2}\approx3.85\times10^{8}\) m.

Worked example · Numerical

The Sun's angular diameter is 1920″ and its distance is \(1.496\times10^{11}\) m. Find its diameter.

Show solution
  1. \(\alpha=1920\times4.85\times10^{-6}=9.31\times10^{-3}\) rad.
  2. \(d=\alpha D=9.31\times10^{-3}\times1.496\times10^{11}\).
  3. \(d\approx1.39\times10^{9}\) m.

Answer: About \(1.39\times10^{9}\) m

More solved problem patterns
Worked example · Numerical

A RADAR pulse sent to the Moon returns after 2.56 s. Find the Moon's distance. Take \(c=3\times10^{8}\) m/s.

Show solution
  1. The pulse travels to the Moon and back, so \(2D=ct\).
  2. \(D=\dfrac{3\times10^{8}\times2.56}{2}=3.84\times10^{8}\) m.

Answer: \(3.84\times10^{8}\) m

Worked example · Numerical

1 cm³ of oleic acid is dissolved in alcohol to make 20 cm³. Then 1 cm³ of this is diluted to 20 cm³. One drop (1/20 cm³) of the final solution spreads into a film of area 1250 cm². Estimate the molecular size.

Show solution
  1. Concentration of oleic acid: \(\tfrac1{20}\times\tfrac1{20}=\tfrac1{400}\).
  2. Oleic acid in one drop: \(\tfrac1{20}\times\tfrac1{400}=1.25\times10^{-4}\) cm³.
  3. Thickness \(=V/A=1.25\times10^{-4}/1250=1\times10^{-7}\) cm \(=1\times10^{-9}\) m.

Answer: About \(10^{-9}\) m

Parallax and the parsec

Hold a pencil at arm's length and close each eye in turn. The pencil appears to jump against the background. The jump is parallax, and it is smaller for farther objects. Astronomers use two positions of the Earth, or two observatories, as the two "eyes".

A parsec is the distance at which 1 AU subtends 1 arc second: \(D=\dfrac{1.496\times10^{11}}{4.85\times10^{-6}}\approx3.08\times10^{16}\) m. A star with parallax \(p\) arc seconds (baseline 1 AU) is at \(1/p\) parsec.

Echo methods and small lengths

RADAR and SONAR: a pulse goes to the object and comes back. If the round trip takes \(t\), the distance is \(D=vt/2\). The factor 2 accounts for the return journey.

Size of a molecule (oleic acid film): a known tiny volume \(V\) of oleic acid spreads on water into a film one molecule thick, of area \(A\). The thickness is \(t=V/A\), about \(10^{-9}\) m.

Lengths in nature span from about \(10^{-15}\) m (a nucleus) to \(10^{26}\) m (the observable universe).

Order of magnitude

Write the number as \(a\times10^{b}\) with \(0.5\lt a\le5\). Then \(b\) is its order of magnitude. If \(a\) is above 5, round up to \(10^{b+1}\). So \(3\times10^8\) m/s has order 8, while the Earth's mass, \(5.97\times10^{24}\) kg, has order 25.

Question patterns
  • "The Sun's angular diameter is 1920″ and its distance … Find its diameter."
  • "A RADAR / SONAR pulse returns after t seconds. Find the distance."
  • "1 parsec equals …" and "parallax of 0.5″ means …".
  • "Order of magnitude of …"
Another common mistake

In echo problems the measured time is for the round trip. Forgetting to halve it doubles the distance.

Another common mistake

Parallax angle and distance are inversely related. A smaller parallax means a farther star.

3 Core

Dimensions and dimensional consistency

Dimensions describe the type of a quantity rather than its chosen unit. Both metres and centimetres measure length and have dimension L. Every term added in a physical equation must have the same dimensions.

A dimensionally incorrect equation is impossible. A dimensionally correct one can still have a wrong numerical coefficient or omit physical effects. Angles, ratios of like quantities and arguments of logarithms are dimensionless.

Dimensions and dimensional consistency
\[ [F]=MLT^{-2},\quad [E]=ML^2T^{-2},\quad [G]=M^{-1}L^3T^{-2} \]
Symbols and assumptions: Square brackets denote dimensions; M, L and T represent mass, length and time; F is force, E is energy and G is the gravitational constant. Dimensional exponents are independent of the SI unit choice.
Other useful formulas
Constants in van der Waals equation
\[ \left(P+\frac{a}{V^2}\right)(V-b)=RT:\quad [a]=ML^5T^{-2},\ [b]=L^3 \]
Symbols and assumptions: P is pressure (Pa), V is volume (m³), a and b are van der Waals constants, R is the gas constant, T is temperature (K). Dimensions follow from adding like quantities.
Watch the assumption

Dimensional analysis cannot distinguish a factor of 1/2 from 2 or determine a sine dependence.

Worked example

Find the dimensions of pressure.

Show solution

Pressure is force/area, so \([p]=MLT^{-2}/L^2=ML^{-1}T^{-2}\). Its SI unit is Pa = N/m².

Worked example · Numerical

In \(x=at+bt^2\), \(x\) is distance and \(t\) is time. Find the dimensions of \(a\), \(b\) and \(a/b\).

Show solution
  1. Each term must be a length. \([at]=L\Rightarrow[a]=LT^{-1}\).
  2. \([bt^2]=L\Rightarrow[b]=LT^{-2}\).
  3. \([a/b]=\dfrac{LT^{-1}}{LT^{-2}}=T\).

Answer: \([a]=LT^{-1}\), \([b]=LT^{-2}\), \([a/b]=T\)

More solved problem patterns
Worked example · Concept

A wave is \(y=A\sin(\omega t-kx)\). Find the dimensions of \(\omega/k\) and name the quantity.

Show solution
  1. The argument of sine is dimensionless.
  2. So \([\omega]=T^{-1}\) and \([k]=L^{-1}\).
  3. \([\omega/k]=LT^{-1}\). This is the wave speed.

Answer: \(LT^{-1}\), the wave speed

Worked example · Statement

Statement I: \(s=ut+\tfrac14at^2\) is dimensionally correct. Statement II: It is the correct equation of motion. Judge both.

Show solution
  1. Each term has dimension L, so statement I is true.
  2. The correct coefficient is \(\tfrac12\), so statement II is false.
  3. Dimensional checks cannot catch a wrong number.

Answer: I true, II false

Homogeneity and the arguments of functions

Only like quantities can be added, subtracted or equated. So every term in a correct equation has the same dimensions. Arguments of \(\sin\), \(\cos\), \(e^x\) and \(\log\) must be dimensionless.

  • In \(y=A\sin(\omega t-kx)\): \([\omega]=T^{-1}\), \([k]=L^{-1}\), so \(\omega/k\) has dimensions \(LT^{-1}\), a speed.
  • In van der Waals' equation \(\left(P+\dfrac{a}{V^2}\right)(V-b)=RT\): \(b\) is a volume (\(L^3\)) and \(a/V^2\) is a pressure, so \([a]=ML^{-1}T^{-2}\times L^6=ML^5T^{-2}\).

Uses and limits of dimensional analysis

Uses: checking equations, converting units between systems, and finding how a quantity depends on others.

Limits:

  1. Pure numbers such as \(\tfrac12\) or \(2\pi\) cannot be found.
  2. Relations with \(\sin\), \(\cos\), \(e^x\) or \(\log\) cannot be derived.
  3. Equations with added terms, like \(s=ut+\tfrac12at^2\), cannot be derived (only checked).
  4. In mechanics only three equations (M, L, T) exist, so at most three unknown powers can be found.
  5. Quantities with the same dimensions (work and torque) cannot be told apart.
Question patterns
  • "Which pair has the same dimensions?" (energy density and pressure, Planck constant and angular momentum, work and torque).
  • "In \(F=a\sqrt x+bt^2\), find the dimensions of a/b."
  • "Dimensions of \(a\) and \(b\) in van der Waals' equation."
  • Statement questions on what dimensional analysis can and cannot do.
Another common mistake

Same dimensions do not mean the same quantity. Work and torque are both \(ML^2T^{-2}\); torque is written in N m, never in joules.

Another common mistake

Dimensionless is not the same as unitless. An angle is dimensionless but has a unit, the radian. Strain is dimensionless and unitless.

Check the idea

4 Exam

Converting between unit systems: n₁u₁ = n₂u₂

A physical quantity does not change when you change units. Only its number changes: \(n_1u_1=n_2u_2\). A bigger unit gives a smaller number.

The physical quantity stays the same when its unit changes. Convert each base unit with the appropriate power: area needs a squared length conversion and density contains both mass and cubed length conversions.

Converting between unit systems: n₁u₁ = n₂u₂
\[ n_2=n_1\left(\frac{M_1}{M_2}\right)^a\left(\frac{L_1}{L_2}\right)^b\left(\frac{T_1}{T_2}\right)^c \]
Symbols and assumptions: n₁, n₂ are the numerical values in systems 1 and 2; M₁, L₁, T₁ and M₂, L₂, T₂ are the base units of mass, length and time in each system, expressed in a common unit; a, b, c are the dimensional exponents of the quantity.
Other useful formulas
New units built from scaled base units
\[ u_{\text{new}}=\alpha^a\beta^b\gamma^c\,u_{\text{SI}},\qquad n_{\text{new}}=\frac{n_{\text{SI}}}{\alpha^a\beta^b\gamma^c} \]
Symbols and assumptions: α, β, γ are the new units of mass, length and time expressed in kg, m and s; a, b, c are the dimensional exponents of the quantity; n is its numerical value.
Useful SI–CGS factors
\[ 1\ \text{N}=10^5\ \text{dyne},\quad 1\ \text{J}=10^7\ \text{erg},\quad 1\ \text{Pa}=10\ \text{dyne cm}^{-2},\quad 1\ \text{kg m}^{-3}=10^{-3}\ \text{g cm}^{-3} \]
Symbols and assumptions: dyne and erg are the CGS units of force and energy (g, cm, s). Each follows from n₁u₁ = n₂u₂.
Watch the assumption

The ratios are (old unit / new unit). Inverting them is the commonest mistake. Check: a bigger new unit must give a smaller number.

Worked example

Convert 1 joule into ergs.

Show solution

For energy, \(a=1\), \(b=2\), \(c=-2\). \(n_2=1\times\left(\frac{1\ \text{kg}}{1\ \text{g}}\right)\left(\frac{1\ \text{m}}{1\ \text{cm}}\right)^2\left(\frac{1\ \text{s}}{1\ \text{s}}\right)^{-2}=10^3\times10^4=10^7\). So 1 J = \(10^7\) erg.

Worked example · Numerical

Convert \(G=6.67\times10^{-11}\ \text{N m}^2\text{kg}^{-2}\) into CGS units.

Show solution
  1. \([G]=M^{-1}L^3T^{-2}\).
  2. \(n_2=6.67\times10^{-11}\times\left(\frac{1000\ \text{g}}{1\ \text{g}}\right)^{-1}\left(\frac{100\ \text{cm}}{1\ \text{cm}}\right)^{3}\left(\frac{1\ \text{s}}{1\ \text{s}}\right)^{-2}\).
  3. \(=6.67\times10^{-11}\times10^{-3}\times10^{6}=6.67\times10^{-8}\).

Answer: \(6.67\times10^{-8}\ \text{dyne cm}^2\text{g}^{-2}\)

More solved problem patterns
Worked example · Numerical

In a new system, the unit of mass is 10 kg, length 10 m and time 10 s. Express 100 J in the new unit of energy.

Show solution
  1. Energy is \(ML^2T^{-2}\). New unit = \(10\times10^2\times10^{-2}=10\) J.
  2. \(n_{\text{new}}=100/10=10\).

Answer: 10 new units

Worked example · Concept

If energy E, velocity V and time T are taken as base quantities, find the dimensions of mass, length and momentum.

Show solution
  1. From \(E=\tfrac12mv^2\): \([M]=[EV^{-2}]\).
  2. From \(v=\)distance/time: \([L]=[VT]\).
  3. Momentum \(=mv\): \([EV^{-2}][V]=[EV^{-1}]\).

Answer: \([M]=EV^{-2}\), \([L]=VT\), \([p]=EV^{-1}\)

Why the number scales inversely with the unit

A length of 2 m is 200 cm. The centimetre is 100 times smaller, so the number is 100 times larger. In general \(Q=n_1u_1=n_2u_2\), so \(n_2/n_1=u_1/u_2\).

For a quantity of dimensions \(M^aL^bT^c\), \(u_1/u_2=(M_1/M_2)^a(L_1/L_2)^b(T_1/T_2)^c\). Each base unit enters with the power it has in the dimensional formula.

A new system with scaled base units

Suppose the new unit of mass is \(\alpha\) kg, the unit of length \(\beta\) m and the unit of time \(\gamma\) s. Then the new unit of a quantity \(M^aL^bT^c\) is \(\alpha^a\beta^b\gamma^c\) times its SI unit, and

\[n_{\text{new}}=\frac{n_{\text{SI}}}{\alpha^a\beta^b\gamma^c}.\]

Example: if length and time units both double, \(g=9.8\ \text{m/s}^2\) (\(LT^{-2}\)) becomes \(9.8/(2\times2^{-2})=19.6\) new units.

Choosing different base quantities

Some questions make, say, force F, velocity V and time T the base quantities. Then express mass using a known formula: \(F=ma=m\,V/T\), so \([M]=[FV^{-1}T]\). Length is \([VT]\). Any other quantity follows by substitution. If energy E, velocity V and time T are base: \(E=\tfrac12mv^2\) gives \([M]=[EV^{-2}]\).

Question patterns
  • "Convert G = \(6.67\times10^{-11}\) N m² kg⁻² into CGS units."
  • "In a new system the unit of mass is α kg, length β m, time γ s. 1 J equals …"
  • "If force, velocity and time are fundamental quantities, the dimensions of mass are …"
  • "The value of g is 9.8 m/s². What is it if the units of length and time are doubled?"
Another common mistake

If both length and time units double, acceleration in new units is larger, not smaller: \(n_2=n_1\times2^{-1}\times2^{2}=2n_1\). Check the sign of each exponent.

Another common mistake

Changing the unit never changes the dimensions of a quantity, only its numerical value.

Check the idea

5 Exam

Constructing a dependence using dimensions

If a quantity depends on a few known variables, dimensional powers can constrain its form. Guess a product of powers, equate exponents of each base dimension, and solve the resulting equations.

This method works when the list of relevant variables is complete and there is no unresolved dimensionless dependence. The coefficient must be obtained from theory or experiment. For a pendulum, dimensions identify the length and gravity scaling but cannot give 2π.

Constructing a dependence using dimensions
\[ T_p=C\ell^a g^b\ \Longrightarrow\ a=\tfrac12,\ b=-\tfrac12,\quad T_p=C\sqrt{\ell/g} \]
Symbols and assumptions: Tₚ is pendulum period (s); ℓ is length (m); g is gravitational acceleration (m/s²); C is dimensionless; a and b are exponents. This analysis assumes no other relevant dimensional input.
Other useful formulas
Stokes drag and spring period
\[ F=6\pi\eta rv,\qquad T=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{m}{k}} \]
Symbols and assumptions: F drag force (N), η viscosity (Pa s), r sphere radius (m), v speed (m/s); T period (s), m mass (kg), k spring constant (N/m). Dimensions give the powers; 6π and 2π come from theory.
Planck length from h, c and G
\[ \ell_P=\sqrt{\frac{hG}{c^3}} \]
Symbols and assumptions: h is Planck’s constant (J s), G the gravitational constant (N m² kg⁻²), c the speed of light (m/s). The only combination of these with dimension L.
Watch the assumption

A dimensionless input such as the swing angle can affect C; dimensional analysis alone cannot establish amplitude independence.

Worked example

Suppose wave speed depends only on string tension F and linear density μ. Find the dependence.

Show solution

Let v ∝ Fᵃμᵇ. Matching mass gives a+b=0; time gives −2a=−1, so a=1/2 and b=−1/2. Hence v ∝ √(F/μ).

Worked example · Numerical

The drag force on a small sphere depends on viscosity \(\eta\), radius \(r\) and speed \(v\). Find the relation.

Show solution
  1. Let \(F=k\eta^ar^bv^c\). Dimensions: \(MLT^{-2}=(ML^{-1}T^{-1})^a L^b(LT^{-1})^c\).
  2. M: \(a=1\). T: \(-a-c=-2\Rightarrow c=1\).
  3. L: \(-a+b+c=1\Rightarrow b=1\).
  4. So \(F=k\eta rv\). Theory gives \(k=6\pi\).

Answer: \(F\propto\eta rv\)

More solved problem patterns
Worked example · Numerical

Find the combination of \(h\), \(c\) and \(G\) that has the dimensions of length.

Show solution
  1. \([h]=ML^2T^{-1}\), \([c]=LT^{-1}\), \([G]=M^{-1}L^3T^{-2}\). Let \(\ell=h^ac^bG^d\).
  2. M: \(a-d=0\). L: \(2a+b+3d=1\). T: \(-a-b-2d=0\).
  3. With \(d=a\): \(b=-3a\) and \(5a+b=1\Rightarrow2a=1\Rightarrow a=\tfrac12\).
  4. So \(a=d=\tfrac12\), \(b=-\tfrac32\).

Answer: \(\ell\propto\sqrt{hG/c^3}\)

Worked example · Concept

Why can dimensional analysis check \(s=ut+\tfrac12at^2\) but not derive it?

Show solution
  1. Checking only needs every term to have dimension L, which they do.
  2. Deriving assumes a single product of powers. Here the answer is a sum of two different products.
  3. The method also cannot give the factor \(\tfrac12\).

Answer: The equation is a sum of terms with a numerical coefficient.

Go deeper · extra explanation and derivations

The method step by step

  1. List every quantity the result may depend on. Leave out none and add none.
  2. Write \(Q=k\,a^xb^yc^z\) with \(k\) a dimensionless constant.
  3. Write the dimensions of each side and equate the powers of M, L and T.
  4. Solve the three equations for \(x\), \(y\), \(z\).
  5. Get \(k\) from theory or experiment.

Results NEET expects you to derive

  • Stokes' drag \(F\propto\eta rv\) (viscosity, radius, speed).
  • Centripetal force \(F\propto mv^2/r\).
  • Mass on a spring \(T\propto\sqrt{m/k}\).
  • Orbital period \(T\propto r^{3/2}(GM)^{-1/2}\).
  • Energy of an oscillator \(E\propto m\omega^2A^2\).
  • Planck length \(\ell\propto\sqrt{hG/c^3}\).
Question patterns
  • "The time period of a planet depends on G, M and r. Find the relation."
  • "Velocity of ripples depends on surface tension, density and wavelength."
  • "Using h, c and G as base quantities, find the dimension of length / time / mass."
  • "Which relation cannot be derived by dimensional analysis?"
Another common mistake

With four unknown powers and only M, L, T, the method fails. You need an extra physical argument, or the problem is badly posed.

6 Core

Significant figures and rounding

Significant figures indicate the precision represented by a measured number. Leading zeros only locate the decimal point; zeros between nonzero digits are significant. Trailing zeros after a decimal point are significant.

For addition or subtraction, round to the least precise decimal place. For multiplication or division, round to the smallest number of significant figures among measured inputs. Keep extra digits during intermediate steps and round the final result once.

Significant figures and rounding
\[ 0.00450=4.50\times10^{-3}\quad(3\ \text{significant figures}) \]
Symbols and assumptions: 4.50 is the measured coefficient and the power of ten fixes scale. Exact counts and defined conversion factors do not limit significant figures; measurement precision does.
Watch the assumption

Writing 1500 without context is ambiguous; scientific notation makes intended precision explicit.

Worked example

Evaluate 12.11 m + 0.3 m with appropriate precision.

Show solution

The unrounded sum is 12.41 m. The second measurement is only precise to tenths, so report 12.4 m, not 12.41 m.

Worked example · Numerical

A cube has edge \(1.2\times10^{-2}\) m. Find its volume and total surface area with correct significant figures.

Show solution
  1. Volume \(a^3=(1.2\times10^{-2})^3=1.728\times10^{-6}\) m³.
  2. Surface area \(6a^2=6\times1.44\times10^{-4}=8.64\times10^{-4}\) m². The 6 is exact.
  3. The edge has 2 significant figures, so round both to 2.

Answer: \(1.7\times10^{-6}\) m³ and \(8.6\times10^{-4}\) m²

Counting rules in one place

  1. All non-zero digits are significant.
  2. Zeros between non-zero digits are significant (2.005 has 4).
  3. Leading zeros are never significant (0.0032 has 2).
  4. Trailing zeros after a decimal point are significant (3.200 has 4).
  5. Trailing zeros in a whole number are ambiguous (4700 m). Scientific notation removes the doubt: \(4.7\times10^3\) has 2, \(4.700\times10^3\) has 4.
  6. A change of unit does not change the count: 2.308 cm = 23.08 mm = 0.02308 m, all four figures.
  7. Exact numbers (counts, the 2 in \(2\pi r\), defined conversions) have unlimited significant figures.

Rounding off (NCERT rule)

  • Dropped digit more than 5: raise the last kept digit (2.746 → 2.75).
  • Dropped digit less than 5: keep it (2.743 → 2.74).
  • Dropped digit exactly 5: leave the kept digit unchanged if it is even, raise it if it is odd. So 2.745 → 2.74 and 2.735 → 2.74.

Keep one extra digit in intermediate steps. Round only the final answer.

Combining measurements

Multiplication and division: the answer keeps as many significant figures as the least precise input. Addition and subtraction: the answer keeps as many decimal places as the input with the fewest decimal places.

Example: a box of mass 2.3 kg gets two gold pieces of 20.15 g and 20.17 g. Total = 2.3403 kg, reported as 2.3 kg. The difference between the gold pieces is 0.02 g, reported as 0.02 g.

Question patterns
  • "Number of significant figures in 0.06900 / 6.320 × 10⁴ / 4700."
  • "Area of a plate of 2.0 cm × 3.15 cm to correct significant figures."
  • "Round 2.745 / 2.735 to three significant figures."
  • Mixed operations: sum then product, or a cube's volume and surface area.
Another common mistake

Converting 2.30 kg to grams as "2300 g" hides the precision. Write \(2.30\times10^3\) g to keep three significant figures.

Another common mistake

For addition, do not count significant figures. 12.11 + 0.3 is limited by decimal places, giving 12.4.

7 Core

Accuracy, precision and error estimates

Accuracy means closeness to the accepted value; precision means repeatability or narrow spread. Repeated measurements can reduce random scatter but do not automatically remove a calibration bias. A mean gives a useful best estimate when random errors dominate.

For repeated readings, the mean absolute deviation is one elementary error estimate. Instrument resolution and known systematic errors must also be considered. Report an absolute uncertainty in the same unit as the quantity; relative uncertainty is a ratio.

Accuracy, precision and error estimates
\[ \bar x=\frac1N\sum x_i,\quad \Delta x=\frac1N\sum|x_i-\bar x|,\quad \text{percentage uncertainty}=100\frac{\Delta x}{|\bar x|} \]
Symbols and assumptions: xᵢ are readings; N is their count; x̄ is the mean; Δx is the mean absolute deviation, in the unit of x. The percentage formula assumes a nonzero mean and is not a universal statistical confidence interval.
Other useful formulas
Reporting a measured value
\[ a=\bar a\pm\overline{\Delta a},\qquad \text{percentage error}=\frac{\overline{\Delta a}}{\bar a}\times100\% \]
Symbols and assumptions: ā is the mean of the readings, Δā is the mean absolute error, in the unit of a. The percentage error is a pure number.
Watch the assumption

Closely clustered readings can all be wrong if the instrument has a zero error.

Worked example

Readings are 9.8, 10.0 and 10.2 cm. Find mean and mean absolute deviation.

Show solution

The mean is 10.0 cm. Deviations are 0.2, 0 and 0.2 cm; their average is 0.133… cm, approximately 0.13 cm before choosing reporting precision.

Worked example · Numerical

The period of a pendulum is read as 2.63 s, 2.56 s, 2.42 s, 2.71 s and 2.80 s. Find the mean, the mean absolute error and the percentage error.

Show solution
  1. Mean: \((2.63+2.56+2.42+2.71+2.80)/5=2.624\approx2.62\) s.
  2. Absolute errors from 2.624: 0.006, 0.064, 0.204, 0.086, 0.176 s.
  3. Mean absolute error: \(0.536/5=0.107\approx0.11\) s.
  4. Percentage error: \(0.11/2.62\times100\approx4\%\).

Answer: \(T=2.62\pm0.11\) s, about 4%

Kinds of error

  • Systematic errors push every reading the same way. They come from the instrument (zero error, faulty calibration), the method (ignoring heat loss) or the observer (always reading from one side, parallax). Repeating does not remove them. They need a correction.
  • Random errors scatter readings above and below the true value. Averaging many readings reduces them.
  • Least count error comes from the resolution of the instrument. A finer instrument reduces it.

Absolute, mean, relative and percentage error

  1. Mean value: \(\bar a=\dfrac{1}{n}\sum a_i\), taken as the best estimate.
  2. Absolute error of each reading: \(\Delta a_i=|a_i-\bar a|\).
  3. Mean absolute error: \(\overline{\Delta a}=\dfrac1n\sum|\Delta a_i|\).
  4. Relative error: \(\overline{\Delta a}/\bar a\); percentage error: \(\times100\%\).
  5. Report as \(\bar a\pm\overline{\Delta a}\).

Accuracy is not precision

Accuracy is how close the result is to the true value. Precision is how close repeated readings are to each other, and how fine the instrument's resolution is. A precise instrument with a zero error gives readings that agree with one another but are all wrong.

Question patterns
  • "Find the mean value, mean absolute error and percentage error" from a list of readings.
  • "Zero error of an instrument is a … error."
  • "Which can be reduced by taking more readings?" (random error).
  • Statement questions on accuracy versus precision.
Another common mistake

Averaging many readings cannot remove a systematic error. Ten readings from a ruler with a worn end are all short by the same amount.

Another common mistake

Personal error, such as always reading a scale at an angle, is a systematic error, not a random one.

8 Exam

Propagation of measurement uncertainty

A calculated quantity inherits uncertainties from its measured inputs. For a worst-case estimate, absolute uncertainties add in sums, while fractional uncertainties add with the absolute values of the powers in products and quotients.

These are first-order bounds for small uncertainties, not a claim that independent random errors always add linearly. A squared radius therefore contributes twice its fractional uncertainty to area; this makes radius measurements especially important.

Propagation of measurement uncertainty
\[ z=x\pm y:\ \Delta z\leq\Delta x+\Delta y;\qquad z=Cx^a y^b:\ \frac{\Delta z}{|z|}\lesssim |a|\frac{\Delta x}{|x|}+|b|\frac{\Delta y}{|y|} \]
Symbols and assumptions: x, y and z are measured/calculated quantities; Δ denotes an absolute uncertainty; C is exact here; a and b are powers. The product formula is a first-order worst-case estimate for small relative errors and nonzero x, y.
Other useful formulas
General power law error
\[ Z=\frac{A^pB^q}{C^r}\ \Rightarrow\ \frac{\Delta Z}{Z}=p\frac{\Delta A}{A}+q\frac{\Delta B}{B}+r\frac{\Delta C}{C} \]
Symbols and assumptions: A, B, C are measured quantities with absolute errors ΔA, ΔB, ΔC; p, q, r are the magnitudes of their powers. Worst-case, first-order estimate for small errors.
Resistors in parallel
\[ \frac{\Delta R}{R^2}=\frac{\Delta R_1}{R_1^2}+\frac{\Delta R_2}{R_2^2} \]
Symbols and assumptions: R is the parallel combination of R₁ and R₂ (Ω); Δ denotes absolute error. Comes from differentiating 1/R = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂.
Watch the assumption

Uncertainties do not cancel merely because the measured quantities are subtracted.

Worked example

For density m/(πr²ℓ), errors in m, r and ℓ are 1%, 2% and 1%. Estimate the maximum percentage error.

Show solution

Add the weighted fractional uncertainties: 1+2×2+1=6%. π is an exact mathematical constant and adds no measurement uncertainty.

Worked example · Numerical

Two resistors, \(R_1=100\pm3\ \Omega\) and \(R_2=200\pm4\ \Omega\), are joined (a) in series and (b) in parallel. Find the combined resistance with its error.

Show solution
  1. Series: \(R=300\ \Omega\), \(\Delta R=3+4=7\ \Omega\).
  2. Parallel: \(R=\dfrac{100\times200}{300}=66.7\ \Omega\).
  3. \(\Delta R=R^2\left(\dfrac{3}{100^2}+\dfrac{4}{200^2}\right)=4444\times(3\times10^{-4}+1\times10^{-4})=1.8\ \Omega\).

Answer: (a) \(300\pm7\ \Omega\) (b) \(66.7\pm1.8\ \Omega\)

Where the rules come from

Sum or difference. If \(Z=A\pm B\), the worst case is \((A\pm\Delta A)\pm(B\pm\Delta B)\), so \(\Delta Z=\Delta A+\Delta B\). Errors always add.

Product. If \(Z=AB\), then \((A\pm\Delta A)(B\pm\Delta B)\approx AB\pm(B\Delta A+A\Delta B)\), dropping the tiny \(\Delta A\Delta B\). Dividing by \(Z=AB\): \(\Delta Z/Z=\Delta A/A+\Delta B/B\). Quotients give the same result.

Power. \(Z=A^n\) is a product of \(n\) copies of \(A\), so \(\Delta Z/Z=n\,\Delta A/A\). This holds for fractional and negative \(n\) using \(|n|\).

Which measurement deserves the most care

The quantity with the largest power dominates. In \(g=4\pi^2\ell/T^2\), the error in T counts twice. In the volume of a sphere, the radius error counts three times. So time the pendulum over many oscillations and measure the radius with the finest instrument.

Differences of nearly equal numbers

Subtraction keeps absolute errors but shrinks the value, so the percentage error can blow up. Temperatures 20.0 ± 0.5 °C and 50.0 ± 0.5 °C differ by 30 ± 1 °C (3.3%). If they were 49.0 and 50.0 °C, the difference 1 ± 1 °C would be 100% uncertain.

Question patterns
  • "\(P=a^3b^2/(cd)\). Errors in a, b, c, d are 1%, 2%, 3%, 4%. Percentage error in P?"
  • "Error in kinetic energy if mass and speed have errors …"
  • "Two resistors with errors are joined in series / parallel. Find the error."
  • "Which quantity should be measured most accurately?" (the one with the highest power).
Another common mistake

In \(Z=A-B\), the error is \(\Delta A+\Delta B\), never \(\Delta A-\Delta B\).

Another common mistake

A power in the denominator still adds its error. In \(\rho=m/V\), the error in V adds to the error in m.

Vernier calipers, screw gauge and zero correction

A fine scale subdivides what the main scale cannot resolve. For a direct vernier, the least count is the difference between one main-scale division and one vernier-scale division. A screw gauge converts rotation into a small linear motion.

Combine the main reading with the coinciding vernier or circular reading. Then subtract the zero error: an instrument that reads positive when it should read zero is reading too high. Check calibration before and after an experiment.

Vernier calipers, screw gauge and zero correction
\[ LC_v=1\,MSD-1\,VSD,\quad LC_s=\frac{p}{N},\quad x_{\rm true}=x_{\rm observed}-e_0 \]
Symbols and assumptions: LCᵥ is direct-vernier least count; MSD and VSD are scale division lengths; LCₛ is screw-gauge least count; p is pitch (advance per full turn); N is divisions per turn; e₀ is signed zero error. All lengths use consistent units.
Other useful formulas
Instrument readings
\[ \text{Vernier: } x=\text{MSR}+n\times LC,\qquad \text{Screw gauge: } x=\text{LSR}+n\times\frac{p}{N} \]
Symbols and assumptions: MSR main-scale reading; LSR linear (sleeve) scale reading; n the coinciding vernier mark or the circular division on the reference line; p pitch; N divisions on the circular scale. All lengths in the same unit. Subtract the zero error afterwards.
Vernier least count, general form
\[ N\ \text{VSD}=(N-1)\ \text{MSD}\ \Rightarrow\ LC=\frac{1\ \text{MSD}}{N} \]
Symbols and assumptions: N is the number of vernier divisions; MSD and VSD are the sizes of one main-scale and one vernier-scale division. For other designs, use LC = 1 MSD − 1 VSD directly.
Watch the assumption

Zero correction has the opposite sign to zero error. A negative zero error requires adding its magnitude.

Worked example

A screw gauge has pitch 0.5 mm and 50 divisions. Observed diameter is 2.36 mm and zero error is +0.02 mm. Find corrected diameter.

Show solution

The least count is 0.5/50=0.01 mm. Corrected diameter is 2.36−0.02=2.34 mm.

Worked example · Numerical

A vernier has 20 divisions that equal 19 main-scale divisions, and 1 MSD = 0.5 mm. Find its least count.

Show solution
  1. \(1\ \text{VSD}=\tfrac{19}{20}\times0.5=0.475\) mm.
  2. \(LC=0.5-0.475=0.025\) mm.
  3. Check with the shortcut: \(LC=\text{MSD}/N=0.5/20=0.025\) mm.

Answer: 0.025 mm

Why a vernier works

Suppose \(N\) vernier divisions span \((N-1)\) main-scale divisions. Then

\[1\ \text{VSD}=\frac{N-1}{N}\ \text{MSD},\qquad LC=1\ \text{MSD}-1\ \text{VSD}=\frac{1\ \text{MSD}}{N}.\]

With 10 vernier divisions over 9 mm, LC = 0.1 mm = 0.01 cm. Each vernier mark is 0.1 mm shorter than a main mark, so the mark that lines up tells you how many tenths of a millimetre to add.

Reading: main-scale reading (the last main mark before the vernier zero) + (number of the coinciding vernier mark × LC).

Screw gauge: pitch and least count

One full turn of the thimble moves the spindle by one pitch. With \(N\) divisions on the circular scale, LC = pitch/N. A pitch of 0.5 mm and 50 divisions give LC = 0.01 mm.

Reading: linear-scale reading (last visible mark on the sleeve) + (circular division on the reference line × LC).

Backlash error: a worn screw can turn a little without moving the spindle. Always turn in one direction while measuring. The ratchet stops you from over-tightening.

Question patterns
  • "10 VSD = 9 MSD and 1 MSD = 1 mm. Least count?"
  • "Pitch 0.5 mm, 50 circular divisions, linear reading … circular reading …. Find the diameter."
  • Figure-reading questions with a vernier or screw gauge.
  • "Backlash error is reduced by …"
Another common mistake

The main-scale reading is the main mark just before the vernier zero, not the main mark that coincides with a vernier mark.

Another common mistake

Least count of a screw gauge uses the pitch, not 1 mm. If the pitch is 0.5 mm and there are 50 divisions, LC = 0.01 mm, not 0.02 mm.

Zero error and instrument reading practice

An instrument has a zero error when it does not read zero with its jaws or studs closed. Find that error first, then correct every reading: true value = observed reading − zero error.

First calculate the instrument’s observed reading. Then subtract the signed zero error. A positive zero error makes readings too high; a negative one means the correction is added.

Zero error and instrument reading practice
\[ e_{+}=+n\,LC,\qquad e_{-}=-(N-n)\,LC,\qquad x_{\text{true}}=x_{\text{obs}}-e \]
Symbols and assumptions: n is the coinciding vernier mark (or circular division on the reference line) with the jaws closed; N is the total number of vernier or circular divisions; LC is the least count; e is the signed zero error.
Other useful formulas
Corrected reading
\[ x_{\text{true}}=(\text{MSR}+n\times LC)-e_0 \]
Symbols and assumptions: MSR is the main or linear scale reading; n the coinciding vernier mark or circular division; LC the least count; e₀ the signed zero error (positive if the instrument over-reads). All in the same unit.
Watch the assumption

For a negative zero error, the coinciding mark \(n\) is counted from the vernier zero, but the error is \((N-n)\) divisions, not \(n\).

Worked example

A vernier (LC = 0.01 cm, 10 divisions) has its zero to the left of the main zero when the jaws are closed, with the 6th vernier mark coinciding. A rod reads 2.53 cm. Find its true length.

Show solution

Zero error \(e=-(10-6)\times0.01=-0.04\) cm. True length \(=2.53-(-0.04)=2.57\) cm.

Worked example · Numerical

A vernier (LC = 0.1 mm) has a positive zero error with the 4th mark coinciding. A rod gives MSR = 3.2 cm and the 7th mark coinciding. Find the true length.

Show solution
  1. Zero error \(e=+4\times0.01=+0.04\) cm.
  2. Observed reading \(=3.2+7\times0.01=3.27\) cm.
  3. True length \(=3.27-0.04=3.23\) cm.

Answer: 3.23 cm

Positive and negative zero error on a vernier

  • Positive: jaws closed, vernier zero to the right of the main zero. The instrument already reads a little. If the 4th vernier mark coincides (LC 0.1 mm), \(e=+0.4\) mm.
  • Negative: vernier zero to the left of the main zero. If the 6th mark coincides on a 10-division vernier, the vernier zero is \((10-6)\times0.1=0.4\) mm short of zero, so \(e=-0.4\) mm.

Zero correction is minus the zero error. Add the correction, or subtract the error. Both give the same true value.

Zero error on a screw gauge

Close the studs gently with the ratchet. Look at the circular scale.

  • If its zero has passed the reference line, so that division \(n\) sits on the line, the gauge reads \(+n\times LC\) with nothing between the studs. This is a positive error. On a standard gauge, the circular zero then lies below the reference line.
  • If its zero has not reached the line, so that division \(n\) near the end of the scale sits on it, the error is \(-(N-n)\times LC\). The circular zero lies above the line.

A full reading routine

  1. Find the least count.
  2. Close the jaws or studs and find the signed zero error.
  3. Take the main or linear scale reading.
  4. Add the vernier or circular division × LC.
  5. Subtract the zero error.
  6. Repeat at different positions and take the mean.

  • "Jaws closed: vernier zero to the right / left and the nth mark coincides. Find the zero error and the corrected reading."
  • "Screw gauge reads … when the studs touch. Find the diameter of a wire."
  • Figure-based reading of a screw gauge or vernier.
  • "Zero correction for a negative zero error is …"
Another common mistake

A negative zero error means the instrument under-reads, so the correction is added. 5.12 cm with error −0.03 cm gives 5.15 cm.

Another common mistake

In a screw gauge with pitch 0.5 mm, check whether the half-millimetre mark below the reference line is visible. Missing it puts the answer 0.5 mm too low.

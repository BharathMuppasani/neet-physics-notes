1 Basics

State variables and the first law

A thermodynamic state is specified by equilibrium variables such as pressure, volume and temperature. Internal energy is a state function: its change depends only on the endpoints. Heat and work describe transfers along a process and depend on its path.

We take heat Q positive into the gas and work W positive when done by the gas. Energy conservation then says that input heat increases internal energy or supplies outward work. Compression gives negative gas work under this convention.

State variables and the first law
\[ \Delta U=Q-W,\qquad \Delta U=nC_V\Delta T\quad\text{(ideal gas, constant }C_V) \]
Symbols and assumptions: ΔU internal-energy change (J); Q heat added (J); W work done by gas (J); n gas amount (mol); C_V molar constant-volume heat capacity (J/mol K); ΔT temperature change (K). Ideal-gas U depends only on T; capacities can vary with temperature.
Other useful formulas
Internal energy and capacities from degrees of freedom
\[ U=\frac{f}{2}nRT,\quad C_V=\frac{f}{2}R,\quad C_P=\left(\frac{f}{2}+1\right)R,\quad \gamma=1+\frac{2}{f} \]
Symbols and assumptions: U internal energy (J); f active degrees of freedom per molecule (3 monatomic, 5 rigid diatomic, 6 rigid non-linear polyatomic); n amount (mol); R = 8.31 J/mol K; T absolute temperature (K); C_V, C_P molar heat capacities (J/mol K); γ = C_P/C_V (no unit). Ideal gas, vibrations not excited.
Watch the assumption

Do not mix the work-by-gas sign convention with a formula written for work-on-gas.

Worked example

A gas receives 500 J heat and does 200 J work. Find internal-energy change.

Show solution

ΔU=500−200=300 J. The gas retains part of the supplied energy and transfers the rest as work.

Worked example · Concept

A gas goes from state A to state B along path 1, absorbing 80 J of heat and doing 30 J of work. (a) Along path 2 from A to B it does 10 J of work. How much heat does it absorb? (b) It returns from B to A along path 3, and 20 J of work is done on it. Find the heat exchanged on path 3.

Show solution
  1. Along path 1: \(\Delta U_{AB} = Q - W = 80 - 30 = 50\) J.
  2. \(U\) is a state function, so \(\Delta U_{AB} = 50\) J on path 2 as well. Then \(Q_2 = \Delta U + W = 50 + 10 = 60\) J.
  3. Returning B to A: \(\Delta U_{BA} = -50\) J. Work done on the gas is 20 J, so work by the gas is \(W = -20\) J.
  4. \(Q_3 = \Delta U + W = -50 + (-20) = -70\) J. The negative sign means the gas releases 70 J.

Answer: (a) 60 J absorbed; (b) 70 J released on the return path.

Worked example · Numerical

2 mol of a diatomic ideal gas (\(C_V = 5R/2\)) warms by 10 K while doing 100 J of work. Find the heat supplied. Take R = 8.3 J/mol K.

Show solution
  1. \(\Delta U = nC_V\Delta T = 2 \times \tfrac{5}{2}\times 8.3 \times 10 = 415\) J. This uses \(C_V\) even though the process is not isochoric.
  2. First law: \(Q = \Delta U + W = 415 + 100 = 515\) J.

Answer: 515 J

Worked example · Numerical

1 g of water at 100 °C turns into 1671 cm³ of steam at 1 atm (1.013 × 10⁵ Pa). The volume of 1 g of water is 1 cm³ and the latent heat is 2256 J/g. Find the change in internal energy.

Show solution
  1. Heat absorbed: \(Q = mL = 1 \times 2256 = 2256\) J.
  2. Work done by the expanding steam against the atmosphere: \(W = P\Delta V = 1.013\times10^{5} \times (1671 - 1)\times10^{-6} \approx 169\) J.
  3. \(\Delta U = Q - W = 2256 - 169 \approx 2087\) J. Most of the latent heat goes into separating the molecules; a small part pushes back the atmosphere.

Answer: About 2087 J (≈ 2.09 kJ).

Zeroth law, state variables and path quantities

The zeroth law says: if bodies A and B are each in thermal equilibrium with a third body C, then A and B are in thermal equilibrium with each other. This is why a thermometer works, and it lets us define temperature as the property that is equal for bodies in thermal equilibrium.

A gas in equilibrium has a well-defined pressure \(P\), volume \(V\) and temperature \(T\), linked by an equation of state such as \(PV=nRT\). Any quantity fixed by the state alone is a state function. Quantities that depend on how the gas got there are path functions.

State functionsPath functions
\(P, V, T, U\) (and entropy). Change between two states is the same for every path. Change over a full cycle is zero.Heat \(Q\) and work \(W\). They describe energy in transit, so they differ from path to path and need not be zero over a cycle.

Intensive variables (\(P, T\), density) do not depend on the amount of gas. Extensive variables (\(V, U\), mass) double when you double the system. During a fast, turbulent process the gas has no single \(P\) or \(T\), so only the start and end states can be plotted on a \(P\)–\(V\) diagram.

Sign conventions side by side

NCERT physics takes \(W\) as work done by the gas. Chemistry books (and some physics books) take \(W\) as work done on the gas. Both describe the same physics.

Another common mistake

\(\Delta U = nC_V\Delta T\) holds for any ideal-gas process. In an isobaric process, students write \(\Delta U = nC_P\Delta T\). That is the heat, not the internal-energy change.

Another common mistake

\(Q = 0\) does not mean \(\Delta T = 0\) (adiabatic compression heats the gas), and \(\Delta T = 0\) does not mean \(Q = 0\) (isothermal expansion absorbs heat). Heat and temperature are different things.

Pressure–volume work and paths

A gas does work when it moves a piston against external pressure. For a quasistatic reversible process, the gas pressure nearly matches the external pressure and the work is the area under the gas p–V curve.

Expansion gives positive work, compression negative work. Two processes connecting the same endpoints can enclose different areas and therefore have different W and Q while sharing the same ΔU. In a general irreversible process, use external resisting pressure for boundary work.

Pressure–volume work and paths
\[ W=\int_{V_i}^{V_f}p_{\rm ext}\,dV,\quad W=p(V_f-V_i)\quad\text{(constant pressure)} \]
Symbols and assumptions: W work by gas (J); p_ext external resisting pressure (Pa); V_i/V_f initial/final volumes (m³); p constant pressure. A reversible quasistatic path permits p_ext≈p_gas; gas p–V area is not automatically valid for arbitrary irreversible paths.
Other useful formulas
Work along a straight-line P–V path
\[ W=\tfrac{1}{2}(P_1+P_2)(V_2-V_1) \]
Symbols and assumptions: W work by gas (J); P₁, P₂ pressures at the two ends (Pa); V₁, V₂ initial and final volumes (m³). Valid when the path is a straight line on a P–V diagram; W is negative if V₂ < V₁.
Net work in a cycle
\[ W_{\rm net}=\pm\,(\text{area enclosed on } P\text{–}V)=Q_{\rm net} \]
Symbols and assumptions: W_net net work by the gas per cycle (J); plus sign for a clockwise loop and minus for anticlockwise, with P on the vertical axis and V on the horizontal axis; Q_net net heat absorbed per cycle (J).
Watch the assumption

A litre is 10⁻³ m³; pressure–volume area then has joule units.

Worked example

A gas expands from 2 to 5 litres against constant 10⁵ Pa. Find work.

Show solution

ΔV=3×10⁻³ m³, so W=10⁵×3×10⁻³=300 J.

Worked example · Graph

A gas goes along a straight line on the \(P\)–\(V\) diagram from (1 L, 100 kPa) to (3 L, 300 kPa). Find the work done by the gas.

Show solution
  1. The area under a straight line is a trapezium: \(W = \tfrac12(P_1+P_2)(V_2-V_1)\).
  2. \(W = \tfrac12(100 + 300)\times10^{3}\ \text{Pa} \times (3-1)\times10^{-3}\ \text{m}^3\).
  3. \(W = 2\times10^{5} \times 2\times10^{-3} = 400\) J. Volume increases, so the sign is positive.

Answer: 400 J done by the gas.

Worked example · Graph

A gas goes round the rectangle A(1 L, 200 kPa) → B(3 L, 200 kPa) → C(3 L, 100 kPa) → D(1 L, 100 kPa) → A on a \(P\)–\(V\) diagram. Find the net work and the net heat absorbed per cycle. What changes if the cycle runs the other way?

Show solution
  1. A→B: expansion at 200 kPa, \(W = 2\times10^{5}\times 2\times10^{-3} = 400\) J. B→C and D→A are vertical, \(W = 0\).
  2. C→D: compression at 100 kPa, \(W = 10^{5}\times(-2\times10^{-3}) = -200\) J.
  3. Net \(W = 400 - 200 = 200\) J. This equals the rectangle area, and the loop is clockwise, so it is positive.
  4. Over a cycle \(\Delta U = 0\), so \(Q_{\rm net} = W = 200\) J. Reversing the direction gives \(W = Q_{\rm net} = -200\) J.

Answer: +200 J work and +200 J net heat absorbed; both become −200 J if reversed.

Worked example · Concept

A gas goes from state 1 (high P, small V) to state 2 (low P, large V) by three paths: (a) expand at constant \(P_1\), then cool at constant volume; (b) cool at constant volume first, then expand at constant \(P_2\); (c) a straight line. Rank the work and the heat absorbed.

Show solution
  1. Path (a) expands at the higher pressure: \(W_a = P_1(V_2 - V_1)\), the largest area.
  2. Path (b) expands at the lower pressure: \(W_b = P_2(V_2 - V_1)\), the smallest area.
  3. Path (c) is the trapezium, between the two: \(W_c = \tfrac12(P_1+P_2)(V_2-V_1)\).
  4. \(\Delta U\) is the same for all three paths, so \(Q = \Delta U + W\) has the same ranking as \(W\).

Answer: \(W_a > W_c > W_b\), and \(Q_a > Q_c > Q_b\).

Why work is the area under the P–V curve

Let the gas push a piston of area \(A\) through a small distance \(dx\). The force is \(PA\), so the work is \(dW = PA\,dx = P\,dV\). Adding up the strips from \(V_1\) to \(V_2\):

\[ W = \int_{V_1}^{V_2} P\,dV = \text{area under the } P\text{–}V \text{ curve} \]
  • Moving right (expansion): \(dV > 0\), so \(W > 0\).
  • Moving left (compression): \(W < 0\). The area is the same size but counted negative.
  • Vertical line (isochoric): no area, \(W = 0\).
  • Two paths between the same end points enclose different areas, so \(W\) (and \(Q\)) differ while \(\Delta U\) is the same.

For a sudden, irreversible change, the gas pressure is not defined during the process. Then use the external pressure: \(W = P_{\rm ext}\Delta V\) when \(P_{\rm ext}\) is constant.

Cyclic process: the loop area

Going round a closed loop, the expansion part adds area and the compression part subtracts area. What is left is the area enclosed by the loop.

  • Clockwise loop on a \(P\)–\(V\) diagram (P up, V across): net work by the gas is positive. The gas expands at high pressure and is compressed at low pressure. This is a heat engine.
  • Anticlockwise loop: net work is negative. Work is done on the gas. This is a refrigerator.
  • Because \(\Delta U = 0\) over a cycle, net heat absorbed equals the net work: \(Q_{\rm net} = W_{\rm net}\).

The rule "clockwise means positive" holds only with \(P\) on the vertical axis and \(V\) on the horizontal axis. If the axes are swapped (V vertical), clockwise means negative work. On \(P\)–\(T\) or \(V\)–\(T\) graphs the loop area is not work at all. Convert to \(P\)–\(V\) first.

Reading work from other graphs

  • On a \(V\)–\(T\) graph, a straight line through the origin means \(V \propto T\): isobaric. Its work is \(nR\Delta T\).
  • On a \(P\)–\(T\) graph, a straight line through the origin means \(P \propto T\): isochoric. Its work is zero.
  • A straight line on a \(P\)–\(V\) graph that is not horizontal: the area is a trapezium, \(W = \tfrac12(P_1+P_2)(V_2-V_1)\).
  • Unit check: 1 kPa × 1 L = 10³ Pa × 10⁻³ m³ = 1 J. But 1 atm × 1 L ≈ 101.3 J.
Question patterns
  • "An ideal gas is taken through the cycle shown. Net work done / net heat absorbed is …" Find the enclosed area and its sign from the direction.
  • "Which of the paths shown requires the maximum work?" Compare the areas under each path.
  • Straight-line paths on P–V: area of a trapezium or triangle.
  • Unit traps: litres, kPa and atm mixed in the same question.
  • Cycles drawn on V–T or P–T axes, asking for the sign of the work.
Another common mistake

The area under a \(P\)–\(T\) or \(V\)–\(T\) graph is not work. Only a \(P\)–\(V\) area gives work directly.

Another common mistake

If the graph has \(V\) on the vertical axis and \(P\) on the horizontal axis, a clockwise loop means negative net work. Check the axes before applying the clockwise rule.

Isochoric and isobaric heating

In an isochoric process volume stays fixed, so no expansion work is done and all added heat changes internal energy. In an isobaric process, part of the heat also provides expansion work, giving a larger heat requirement for the same ideal-gas temperature rise.

For an ideal gas at constant pressure, pΔV=nRΔT. Applying the first law gives the molar heat-capacity relation C_P−C_V=R. This relation is specific to an ideal gas; it is not a universal identity for every material.

Isochoric and isobaric heating
\[ Q_V=nC_V\Delta T,\quad Q_P=nC_P\Delta T,\quad W_P=nR\Delta T,\quad C_P-C_V=R \]
Symbols and assumptions: Q_V/Q_P heat added at fixed volume/pressure (J); n amount (mol); C_V/C_P molar capacities (J/mol K); ΔT temperature rise (K); W_P gas work (J); R gas constant. Assume ideal gas and constant capacities over the interval.
Other useful formulas
Energy split at constant pressure
\[ \frac{\Delta U}{Q}=\frac{1}{\gamma},\qquad \frac{W}{Q}=1-\frac{1}{\gamma} \]
Symbols and assumptions: Q heat supplied at constant pressure (J); ΔU rise in internal energy (J); W work by gas (J); γ = C_P/C_V (no unit). Ideal gas, isobaric process only.
Specific heats per unit mass
\[ c_P-c_V=\frac{R}{M} \]
Symbols and assumptions: c_P, c_V specific heats per unit mass (J/kg K); R = 8.31 J/mol K; M molar mass (kg/mol). Ideal gas. The lighter the gas, the larger the difference.
Watch the assumption

Isochoric means fixed volume; it does not mean fixed temperature or zero heat.

Worked example

One mole of monatomic ideal gas warms by 20 K at fixed pressure. Find Q, W and ΔU in terms of R.

Show solution

C_P=5R/2 and C_V=3R/2. Thus Q=50R J, W=20R J and ΔU=30R J; Q−W=ΔU.

Worked example · Ratio

70 J of heat is given to a diatomic ideal gas at constant pressure. How much goes into work and how much into internal energy?

Show solution
  1. For a diatomic gas \(\gamma = 7/5\), so \(\Delta U/Q = 1/\gamma = 5/7\) and \(W/Q = 2/7\).
  2. \(\Delta U = \tfrac{5}{7}\times 70 = 50\) J and \(W = \tfrac{2}{7}\times 70 = 20\) J.
  3. Check: \(50 + 20 = 70\) J, as the first law requires.

Answer: W = 20 J, ΔU = 50 J.

Worked example · Numerical

2 mol of a monatomic ideal gas is heated at constant volume from 300 K to 350 K. The same amount of heat is then given to an identical sample at constant pressure. Find its temperature rise.

Show solution
  1. At constant volume: \(Q = nC_V\Delta T = 2\times\tfrac32 R\times 50 = 150R\).
  2. At constant pressure: \(\Delta T = Q/(nC_P) = 150R/(2\times\tfrac52 R) = 30\) K.
  3. The ratio of the rises is \(50/30 = 5/3 = \gamma\). Part of the heat at constant pressure goes into work, so the gas warms less.

Answer: 30 K

Worked example · Graph

A fixed mass of gas is studied at two constant volumes \(V_1\) and \(V_2\). On a \(P\)–\(T\) graph (T in kelvin) both lines pass through the origin, and the line for \(V_1\) is steeper. Which volume is larger?

Show solution
  1. From \(PV = nRT\), \(P = (nR/V)\,T\). The slope of an isochore on a \(P\)–\(T\) graph is \(nR/V\).
  2. A larger slope means a smaller volume. So \(V_1 < V_2\).
  3. In the same way, on a \(V\)–\(T\) graph the slope of an isobar is \(nR/P\), so the steeper isobar is at lower pressure.

Answer: \(V_2\) is larger (\(V_1 < V_2\)).

Why C_P is larger than C_V (Mayer's relation)

  1. Constant volume: \(W = 0\), so \(Q = \Delta U = nC_V\Delta T\). This defines \(C_V\).
  2. Constant pressure: \(Q = \Delta U + P\Delta V\). For an ideal gas \(P\Delta V = nR\Delta T\), so \(Q = nC_V\Delta T + nR\Delta T\).
  3. By definition this heat is \(nC_P\Delta T\). Hence \(C_P = C_V + R\), i.e. \(C_P - C_V = R\).

Per unit mass the relation reads \(c_P - c_V = R/M\), where \(M\) is the molar mass in kg/mol. So it differs from gas to gas.

Isothermal processes and free expansion

An ideal gas undergoing an isothermal process has unchanged internal energy because temperature is fixed. Heat and work can still be nonzero. For reversible expansion, heat from a reservoir replaces the energy transferred as work.

Free expansion into vacuum is different: the external pressure is zero, so boundary work is zero. If also insulated, Q=0 and ΔU=0. An ideal gas then has the same final temperature, but the irreversible process must not be assigned the reversible logarithmic work.

Isothermal processes and free expansion
\[ W_{\rm rev}=nRT\ln\frac{V_f}{V_i},\quad Q=W_{\rm rev},\quad \Delta U=0\quad\text{(ideal isothermal)} \]
Symbols and assumptions: W_rev reversible isothermal work (J); n amount (mol); R gas constant; T fixed absolute temperature (K); V_i/V_f volumes (m³); ln is natural logarithm. Its argument is dimensionless. Q=W applies to the ideal isothermal energy balance.
Other useful formulas
Isothermal work in other forms
\[ W=nRT\ln\frac{P_1}{P_2}=2.303\,nRT\log_{10}\frac{V_2}{V_1} \]
Symbols and assumptions: W work by gas (J); n amount (mol); R = 8.31 J/mol K; T constant absolute temperature (K); P₁, P₂ initial and final pressures; V₁, V₂ initial and final volumes. Reversible isothermal ideal-gas process.
Isothermal slope and bulk modulus
\[ \left(\frac{dP}{dV}\right)_{T}=-\frac{P}{V},\qquad B_T=P \]
Symbols and assumptions: P pressure (Pa); V volume (m³); B_T isothermal bulk modulus (Pa). Ideal gas at constant temperature.
Watch the assumption

Constant temperature does not mean no heat transfer; insulation means no heat transfer.

Worked example

One mole expands reversibly at 300 K to twice its volume. Find work using R=8.31.

Show solution

W=1×8.31×300×ln2≈1728 J. ΔU=0, so the same heat must enter from the reservoir.

Worked example · Numerical

2 mol of an ideal gas at 27 °C is compressed isothermally from 1 atm to 4 atm. Find the work done by the gas and the heat exchanged. Take R = 8.31 J/mol K and ln 2 = 0.693.

Show solution
  1. T = 27 + 273 = 300 K. \(W = nRT\ln(P_1/P_2) = 2\times 8.31\times 300 \times \ln(1/4)\).
  2. \(\ln(1/4) = -2\ln 2 = -1.386\). So \(W = 4986 \times (-1.386) \approx -6.91\times10^{3}\) J.
  3. \(\Delta U = 0\), so \(Q = W \approx -6.91\) kJ. The gas releases about 6.91 kJ of heat to the reservoir.

Answer: W ≈ −6.91 kJ (6.91 kJ of work done on the gas); about 6.91 kJ of heat is released.

Worked example · Ratio

The same gas sample, starting from the same state, is expanded isothermally (i) to twice its volume and (ii) to four times its volume. Find \(W_1 : W_2\). If instead (ii) were done at double the temperature but to twice the volume, what would the ratio be?

Show solution

  2. At fixed volume ratio, \(W \propto T\). Doubling \(T\) doubles the work, so the ratio is again \(1 : 2\).

Answer: 1 : 2 in both cases.

Worked example · Concept

An insulated box is split into two equal halves. One half holds an ideal gas at pressure P and temperature T. The other half is a vacuum. The partition is removed. Find the final pressure and temperature, and W, Q and ΔU. A student uses \(PV^\gamma = \) const. What is wrong?

Show solution

  2. First law: \(\Delta U = 0\). For an ideal gas this means the final temperature is T.
  3. With T unchanged and volume doubled, \(P_f = P/2\).
  4. \(PV^\gamma = \) const needs a slow (quasistatic) reversible adiabatic process. Free expansion is sudden and irreversible, so \(P/2^\gamma\) is wrong.

Answer: \(P_f = P/2\), \(T_f = T\), and W = Q = ΔU = 0.

Derivation: work in a reversible isothermal process

  1. Along an isotherm \(P = nRT/V\) with \(T\) constant.
  2. \(W = \int_{V_1}^{V_2} P\,dV = nRT\int_{V_1}^{V_2}\frac{dV}{V} = nRT\ln\frac{V_2}{V_1}\).
  3. Since \(P_1V_1 = P_2V_2\), \(V_2/V_1 = P_1/P_2\). So \(W = nRT\ln\frac{P_1}{P_2}\).
  4. With common logarithms: \(W = 2.303\,nRT\log_{10}\frac{V_2}{V_1}\).

\(\Delta U = 0\), so \(Q = W\). In expansion the gas absorbs heat equal to the work it does. In compression \(W < 0\) and the gas gives out the same amount of heat.

What makes a process isothermal

  • Thin conducting (diathermic) walls, good contact with a large reservoir, and a slow process so heat has time to flow.
  • Heat flows but \(T\) does not change, so the molar heat capacity \(C = Q/(n\Delta T)\) is infinite.
  • Slope on \(P\)–\(V\): from \(PV = \) const, \(\dfrac{dP}{dV} = -\dfrac{P}{V}\).
  • Isothermal bulk modulus: \(B_T = -V\dfrac{dP}{dV} = P\).

Link to the speed of sound. Newton assumed sound compressions are isothermal and used \(v = \sqrt{P/\rho}\). For air at 1.013 × 10⁵ Pa and 1.293 kg/m³ this gives about 280 m/s, well below the measured 332 m/s. Laplace noted that the compressions are too fast for heat to flow, so they are adiabatic: \(v = \sqrt{\gamma P/\rho} \approx 331\) m/s.

Free expansion is not isothermal expansion

In Joule's free expansion, a gas in an insulated container rushes into an evacuated part. It pushes nothing, so \(W = 0\). The walls are insulated, so \(Q = 0\). Hence \(\Delta U = 0\).

  • For an ideal gas the final temperature equals the initial temperature, and \(P_fV_f = P_iV_i\).
  • For a real gas with attractive forces, the temperature usually drops a little.
  • The process is irreversible and fast. The intermediate states are not equilibrium states, so it has no \(P\)–\(V\) curve. Neither \(nRT\ln(V_2/V_1)\) nor \(PV^\gamma = \) const applies.

Isothermal expansion and free expansion share the same end states for an ideal gas. They are different paths with different \(Q\) and \(W\).

Adiabatic change and connected-cylinder reasoning

Adiabatic means no heat crosses the boundary. A rapid expansion or good insulation can approximately achieve it. Expansion work then comes from internal energy and lowers the temperature of an ideal gas; compression raises it.

The pV^γ relation requires a reversible quasistatic adiabatic ideal-gas process. In connected rigid insulated cylinders, choose both gases as the system: no external boundary work and no external heat imply total internal-energy conservation. A valve opening is generally irreversible, so do not apply pV^γ to each side during mixing.

Derivation of PV^γ = constant (outline)

  1. Adiabatic: \(dQ = 0\), so \(dU = -dW\), that is \(nC_V\,dT = -P\,dV\).
  2. Differentiate \(PV = nRT\): \(P\,dV + V\,dP = nR\,dT\).
  3. Substitute \(n\,dT = -P\,dV/C_V\): \(P\,dV + V\,dP = -\dfrac{R}{C_V}P\,dV = -(\gamma-1)P\,dV\), using \(R/C_V = \gamma - 1\).
  4. Rearrange: \(\gamma P\,dV + V\,dP = 0\), so \(\dfrac{dP}{P} = -\gamma\dfrac{dV}{V}\).
  5. Integrate: \(\ln P = -\gamma\ln V + \) const, so \(PV^\gamma = \) constant.

Using \(P = nRT/V\) gives the other forms: \(TV^{\gamma-1} = \) const and \(P^{1-\gamma}T^{\gamma} = \) const (equivalently \(T \propto P^{(\gamma-1)/\gamma}\)).

Derivation of adiabatic work

Write \(P = K/V^\gamma\) where \(K = P_1V_1^\gamma = P_2V_2^\gamma\). Then

\[ W = \int_{V_1}^{V_2}\frac{K}{V^\gamma}\,dV = \frac{K}{1-\gamma}\left[V_2^{1-\gamma} - V_1^{1-\gamma}\right] = \frac{P_1V_1 - P_2V_2}{\gamma - 1} = \frac{nR(T_1 - T_2)}{\gamma - 1} \]

Check with the first law: \(W = -\Delta U = -nC_V(T_2 - T_1) = \dfrac{nR(T_1 - T_2)}{\gamma - 1}\), since \(C_V = R/(\gamma-1)\). In expansion \(T_2 < T_1\) and \(W > 0\); in compression \(W < 0\) and the gas heats up.

Adiabatic versus isothermal: slopes and work

Slope of an isotherm: \(dP/dV = -P/V\). Slope of an adiabat: \(dP/dV = -\gamma P/V\). Through the same point the adiabat is steeper by a factor \(\gamma\). For the same reason the adiabatic bulk modulus is \(B_S = \gamma P\), while the isothermal one is \(B_T = P\).

Polytropic processes and molar heat capacity of a process

Many exam processes are neither isothermal nor adiabatic. A process that obeys \(PV^n = \) constant is called polytropic. The index n picks out the familiar cases: n = 0 is isobaric, n = 1 isothermal, n = γ adiabatic, and very large n is isochoric. In this section μ is the amount of gas in moles, because n is already the index.

Read the given pressure–volume relation before selecting a process formula. Find the endpoint temperatures with the ideal-gas law, then calculate work and internal-energy change. Use the first law to obtain heat; it is not automatically nCᵥΔT.

Polytropic processes and molar heat capacity of a process
\[ PV^{n}=\text{constant},\quad W=\frac{\mu R(T_1-T_2)}{n-1}=\frac{P_1V_1-P_2V_2}{n-1},\quad C=C_V+\frac{R}{1-n} \]
Symbols and assumptions: P pressure (Pa); V volume (m³); n polytropic index (no unit, n ≠ 1 in the work and C formulas); μ amount (mol); R = 8.31 J/mol K; T₁, T₂ initial and final absolute temperatures (K); W work by gas (J); C molar heat capacity of the process (J/mol K); C_V molar heat capacity at constant volume. Ideal gas, quasistatic process.
Other useful formulas
Molar heat capacity in terms of γ
\[ C=\frac{R}{\gamma-1}-\frac{R}{n-1} \]
Symbols and assumptions: C molar heat capacity of the process (J/mol K); R = 8.31 J/mol K; γ = C_P/C_V of the gas; n polytropic index, n ≠ 1. C is negative for 1 < n < γ.
Heat capacity for any process law
\[ C=C_V+\frac{P}{\mu}\frac{dV}{dT} \]
Symbols and assumptions: C molar heat capacity along the process (J/mol K); C_V molar heat capacity at constant volume; P pressure (Pa); μ amount (mol); dV/dT rate of change of volume with temperature along the process (m³/K). Ideal gas, quasistatic process.

The extra term is \(R/(1-n)\), with \(1 - n\) in the denominator. Writing \(R/(n-1)\) flips its sign. Check with n = 0: the formula must give \(C_V + R = C_P\).

Worked example

One mole of a monatomic ideal gas is heated in a process where \(P \propto V\). Find its molar heat capacity.

Show solution

\(P \propto V\) means \(PV^{-1} = \) const, so n = −1. Then \(C = C_V + R/(1-(-1)) = \tfrac32R + \tfrac12R = 2R\).

Two more useful facts. Using \(P = \mu RT/V\), the process \(PV^n = \) const is the same as \(TV^{n-1} = \) const. And the slope of the curve on a \(P\)–\(V\) diagram is \(dP/dV = -nP/V\), so a larger n gives a steeper curve through the same point.

Derivation: work and heat capacity of a polytropic process

  1. Write \(P = K/V^n\) with \(K = P_1V_1^n = P_2V_2^n\).
  2. \(W = \int_{V_1}^{V_2} KV^{-n}\,dV = \dfrac{K\left(V_2^{1-n} - V_1^{1-n}\right)}{1-n} = \dfrac{P_1V_1 - P_2V_2}{n-1} = \dfrac{\mu R(T_1 - T_2)}{n-1}\) for \(n \ne 1\).
  3. \(\Delta U = \mu C_V(T_2 - T_1)\) for any ideal-gas process.
  4. \(Q = \Delta U + W = \mu\left(C_V - \dfrac{R}{n-1}\right)(T_2 - T_1)\).
  5. Defining \(Q = \mu C\Delta T\): \(C = C_V + \dfrac{R}{1-n} = \dfrac{R}{\gamma - 1} - \dfrac{R}{n-1}\).

Fractions of the heat: \(\dfrac{\Delta U}{Q} = \dfrac{C_V}{C}\) and \(\dfrac{W}{Q} = \dfrac{R/(1-n)}{C}\). For \(P \propto V\) with a monatomic gas, \(C = 2R\), so 3/4 of the heat goes to U and 1/4 to work.

A general shortcut for any process law: \(C = C_V + \dfrac{P}{\mu}\dfrac{dV}{dT}\). It comes from \(dQ = \mu C_V\,dT + P\,dV\).

Negative heat capacity and converting process laws

For \(1 < n < \gamma\), \(R/(1-n)\) is negative and larger in size than \(C_V\), so \(C < 0\). Then Q and ΔT have opposite signs. In such an expansion, the gas absorbs heat but does even more work than the heat it gets. The extra comes from U, so the gas cools while heat flows in.

To find C for a law written in T, turn it into \(PV^n = \) const using \(PV = \mu RT\):

  • \(V \propto T^2\): \(T \propto V^{1/2}\), so \(P \propto T/V \propto V^{-1/2}\). Thus \(PV^{1/2} = \) const, n = 1/2, \(C = C_V + 2R\).
  • \(PT = \) const: \(P \propto 1/T\) and \(T \propto PV\), so \(P^2V = \) const, that is \(PV^{1/2} = \) const. Again n = 1/2.
  • \(P = kV^2\): \(PV^{-2} = \) const, n = −2, \(C = C_V + R/3\).
  • \(V \propto T^k\) in general: \(TV^{n-1} = \) const gives \(n = 1 - 1/k\).

Cycles, heat engines and efficiency

A thermodynamic cycle returns to its initial state, so the net internal-energy change is zero. Net heat input equals net work output. On a p–V plot a clockwise loop represents positive net work by the gas.

A heat engine absorbs energy from a hot source, delivers some as work, and rejects the remainder to a colder sink. Efficiency uses work divided by heat absorbed from the hot source, not divided by the net heat input.

Cycles, heat engines and efficiency
\[ \Delta U_{\rm cycle}=0,\quad W=Q_h-Q_c,\quad \eta=\frac{W}{Q_h}=1-\frac{Q_c}{Q_h} \]
Symbols and assumptions: W net cycle work (J); Q_h positive heat absorbed from hot source (J); Q_c positive magnitude rejected to cold sink (J); η dimensionless efficiency. The engine returns to its starting state after each cycle.
Other useful formulas
Carnot heat ratio
\[ \frac{Q_2}{Q_1}=\frac{T_2}{T_1},\qquad Q_1=nRT_1\ln\frac{V_B}{V_A} \]
Symbols and assumptions: Q₁ heat absorbed from the source per cycle (J); Q₂ heat rejected to the sink (J, magnitude); T₁, T₂ source and sink temperatures (K); V_A, V_B volumes at the start and end of the hot isothermal expansion. Reversible Carnot cycle, ideal gas.
Efficiency of any cycle
\[ \eta=\frac{W_{\rm net}}{\sum Q_{\rm absorbed}} \]
Symbols and assumptions: η efficiency (no unit); W_net net work per cycle (J), the loop area; ΣQ_absorbed sum of only the positive heat inputs over the cycle (J). The net heat equals W_net and must not be used in the denominator.
Watch the assumption

Q_c is defined here as a positive magnitude; heat-added signs in a process equation must still be handled consistently.

Worked example

Show solution

W=800−500=300 J. Efficiency is 300/800=0.375=37.5%.

Show solution

  2. For an ideal gas \(nC_V\Delta T = \tfrac32\Delta(PV)\) and \(nC_P\Delta T = \tfrac52\Delta(PV)\) at constant P.
  3. A→B (isochoric, P rises): \(Q = \tfrac32(2P_0V_0 - P_0V_0) = 1.5P_0V_0\), absorbed.
  4. B→C (isobaric expansion): \(Q = \tfrac52(4P_0V_0 - 2P_0V_0) = 5P_0V_0\), absorbed.

Bookkeeping for an engine cycle

A heat engine has three parts: a hot source at \(T_1\), a working substance (the gas) and a cold sink at \(T_2\). Per cycle it takes \(Q_1\), gives out work \(W\) and rejects \(Q_2\). (The lesson above writes these as \(Q_h\) and \(Q_c\).)

\[ \eta = \frac{W}{Q_1} = \frac{Q_1 - Q_2}{Q_1} = 1 - \frac{Q_2}{Q_1} \]

Method for a cycle drawn on a graph:

  1. Find \(W\) as the enclosed area, with its sign from the direction.
  2. Find \(Q\) for each step: \(nC_V\Delta T\) (isochoric), \(nC_P\Delta T\) (isobaric), \(nRT\ln(V_2/V_1)\) (isothermal), 0 (adiabatic).
  3. Add only the positive \(Q\) values. That sum is \(Q_1\).
  4. \(\eta = W/Q_1\). Dividing by the net heat would always give 100%, which is wrong.

The Carnot cycle and its efficiency

A Carnot engine uses an ideal gas through four reversible steps:

  1. A→B: isothermal expansion at \(T_1\). The gas absorbs \(Q_1 = nRT_1\ln(V_B/V_A)\).
  2. B→C: adiabatic expansion. T falls from \(T_1\) to \(T_2\).
  3. C→D: isothermal compression at \(T_2\). The gas rejects \(Q_2 = nRT_2\ln(V_C/V_D)\).
  4. D→A: adiabatic compression. T rises from \(T_2\) back to \(T_1\).

The two adiabats give \(T_1V_B^{\gamma-1} = T_2V_C^{\gamma-1}\) and \(T_1V_A^{\gamma-1} = T_2V_D^{\gamma-1}\). Dividing, \(V_B/V_A = V_C/V_D\). So the logarithms cancel and

\[ \frac{Q_2}{Q_1} = \frac{T_2}{T_1}, \qquad \eta_{\rm Carnot} = 1 - \frac{T_2}{T_1} \]

The adiabatic works B→C and D→A are equal and opposite (each is \(nR(T_1-T_2)/(\gamma-1)\)), so they cancel. The net work is \(Q_1 - Q_2\).

Second law, Carnot limits and refrigerators

The first law conserves energy but does not decide which processes occur spontaneously. The second law rules out converting heat from a single reservoir entirely into cyclic work and requires external work to pump heat from cold to hot.

A reversible Carnot engine sets the maximum efficiency between two temperatures. Real engines have irreversible losses. A refrigerator’s coefficient of performance compares heat removed from the cold region with supplied work; it can exceed one because it measures moved heat, not an energy-conversion fraction.

An efficiency fraction cannot exceed one; refrigerator COP can because work helps move additional heat.

Worked example · Numerical

A Carnot engine has efficiency 1/6. When the sink temperature is lowered by 62 K, its efficiency becomes 1/3. Find the source and sink temperatures.

Show solution
  1. First condition: \(1 - T_2/T_1 = 1/6\), so \(T_2 = \tfrac56T_1\).
  2. Second condition: \(1 - (T_2 - 62)/T_1 = 1/3\), so \(T_2 - 62 = \tfrac23T_1\).
  3. Subtract: \(\tfrac56T_1 - \tfrac23T_1 = 62\), so \(\tfrac16T_1 = 62\) and \(T_1 = 372\) K.
  4. \(T_2 = \tfrac56\times372 = 310\) K. Check: \(1 - 248/372 = 1/3\).

Answer: Source 372 K (99 °C), sink 310 K (37 °C).

Worked example · Numerical

An ideal refrigerator keeps its freezer at −23 °C in a room at 27 °C. It must remove 1000 J of heat per second. Find its COP, the power input and the heat given to the room per second.

Show solution
  1. In kelvin: \(T_2 = 250\) K, \(T_1 = 300\) K.
  2. \(\beta = T_2/(T_1 - T_2) = 250/50 = 5\).
  3. Power \(= Q_2/\beta = 1000/5 = 200\) W.
  4. Heat to the room \(= Q_2 + W = 1000 + 200 = 1200\) J each second.

Answer: COP = 5, input 200 W, 1200 J/s given to the room.

Worked example · Concept

A Carnot engine works between 500 K and 300 K. You may either raise the source by 100 K or lower the sink by 100 K. Which gives the higher efficiency, and why?

Show solution
  1. Now: \(\eta = 1 - 300/500 = 40\%\).
  2. Raise the source to 600 K: \(\eta = 1 - 300/600 = 50\%\).
  3. Lower the sink to 200 K: \(\eta = 1 - 200/500 = 60\%\).
  4. \(\eta\) depends on the ratio \(T_2/T_1\). Lowering \(T_2\) changes the numerator directly. Raising \(T_1\) acts through a larger denominator, which is less effective because \(T_2 < T_1\).

Answer: Lowering the sink (60% against 50%).

Kelvin–Planck, Clausius and reversibility

  • Kelvin–Planck statement: No process is possible whose sole result is the absorption of heat from a reservoir and its complete conversion into work. So no engine has 100% efficiency; some heat must go to a sink.
  • Clausius statement: No process is possible whose sole result is the transfer of heat from a colder body to a hotter body. So a refrigerator needs work input; its COP is finite.
  • The two statements are equivalent: breaking one would let you break the other.

A reversible process is quasistatic (a chain of equilibrium states) and free of dissipation. It can be run backwards with both system and surroundings restored. Friction, free expansion, heat flow across a finite temperature difference and mixing of gases are irreversible.

Carnot's theorem: no engine working between two given temperatures is more efficient than a reversible engine, and all reversible engines between the same two temperatures have the same efficiency, \(1 - T_2/T_1\). \(\eta = 1\) would need \(T_2 = 0\) K, which cannot be reached.

Refrigerators and heat pumps

Run an engine backwards. Work \(W\) is put in, heat \(Q_2\) is taken from the cold body and \(Q_1 = Q_2 + W\) is given to the hot body.

Refrigeratorheat \(Q_2\) removed from cold side\(\beta = Q_2/W\)\(T_2/(T_1 - T_2)\)
Heat pumpheat \(Q_1\) delivered to warm side\(Q_1/W\)\(T_1/(T_1 - T_2)\)

Engine reversed: for a reversible machine between the same reservoirs, the same \(Q_1, Q_2, W\) appear with arrows reversed. Then \(\beta = Q_2/W = (Q_1 - W)/W = 1/\eta - 1\), so

\[ \beta = \frac{1-\eta}{\eta}, \qquad \text{COP}_{\rm heat\ pump} = \frac{Q_1}{W} = \beta + 1 = \frac{1}{\eta} \]

Raise the source or lower the sink?

\(\eta = 1 - T_2/T_1\). Change each temperature by a small amount:

\[ \frac{\partial\eta}{\partial T_1} = \frac{T_2}{T_1^2}, \qquad \frac{\partial\eta}{\partial T_2} = -\frac{1}{T_1} \]

Since \(T_2 < T_1\), \(T_2/T_1^2 < 1/T_1\). So lowering the sink by \(\Delta T\) raises the efficiency more than raising the source by the same \(\Delta T\).

Example: between 500 K and 300 K, \(\eta = 40\%\). Raising the source by 100 K gives \(1 - 300/600 = 50\%\). Lowering the sink by 100 K gives \(1 - 200/500 = 60\%\).

For a refrigerator, a colder inside (smaller \(T_2\)) or a hotter room makes \(T_1 - T_2\) larger, so \(\beta = T_2/(T_1 - T_2)\) gets smaller. Cooling to very low temperatures costs a lot of work.

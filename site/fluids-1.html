9.1 Basics

What is a fluid?

Push a book sideways and it slides as one piece: it keeps its shape. Push water sideways and it simply flows. That is the whole difference. A fluid is anything that flows. Liquids and gases are both fluids.

Why do fluids flow? A solid can resist a sideways (shearing) push by deforming a little and pushing back. A fluid at rest cannot. Even a tiny sideways force makes its layers slide over each other. So a fluid at rest can push on a surface only at right angles to it. This one fact explains a lot of the chapter.

  • Liquids have a fixed volume but no fixed shape. They are nearly incompressible, so in this chapter we treat their density as constant.
  • Gases fill whatever container they are in and are easily compressed.
Definition

Density \( \rho = \dfrac{m}{V} \), SI unit kg m⁻³, dimensions [ML⁻³].
Relative density (specific gravity) \( = \dfrac{\rho}{\rho_{\text{water at }4^\circ\text{C}}} \). It is a pure number with no unit. Mercury has RD 13.6, so \( \rho_{Hg} = 13.6 \times 1000 = 13600 \) kg m⁻³.

Density and relative density
\[ \rho = \frac{m}{V}, \qquad \text{RD} = \frac{\rho}{1000\ \text{kg m}^{-3}} \]
1 g cm⁻³ = 1000 kg m⁻³. Water: 1000; seawater: 1030; ice: about 917 (problems often use 900); mercury: 13600; air: about 1.2–1.3 kg m⁻³.
Mixing two liquids
\[ \rho_{\text{equal }V} = \frac{\rho_1 + \rho_2}{2}, \qquad \rho_{\text{equal }m} = \frac{2\rho_1\rho_2}{\rho_1 + \rho_2} \]
Always use total mass ÷ total volume. The equal-mass result (harmonic mean) is always the smaller one.
What "ideal fluid" means in questions

Unless a question says otherwise, take the fluid to be incompressible (constant ρ), non-viscous (no friction between layers), with steady flow that is irrotational. Bernoulli's equation needs all four. Viscosity, Stokes' law and surface tension are in Fluids II.

Worked example · Numerical

Two liquids of densities 0.8 g cm⁻³ and 1.2 g cm⁻³ are mixed. Find the density of the mixture if (a) equal volumes are mixed, (b) equal masses are mixed.

Show solution
  1. (a) Take volume \(V\) of each. Mass = \(0.8V + 1.2V = 2V\), total volume \(2V\), so \(\rho = 1.0\) g cm⁻³.
  2. (b) Take mass \(m\) of each. Volume = \(m/0.8 + m/1.2 = m(1.25 + 0.833) = 2.083m\). So \(\rho = 2m / 2.083m = 0.96\) g cm⁻³.
  3. Check with the formula: \(2(0.8)(1.2)/(2.0) = 0.96\). ✓

Answer: (a) 1.0 g cm⁻³ = 1000 kg m⁻³; (b) 0.96 g cm⁻³ = 960 kg m⁻³

9.2 Basics

Pressure and how it grows with depth

A sharp knife cuts but a blunt one does not, even though your hand pushes just as hard. A camel walks on sand without sinking because its feet are wide. School-bag straps are made wide so they do not dig into your shoulders. In each case what matters is not just the force but how much area it is spread over. That quantity is pressure.

Definition

Pressure is the normal force per unit area: \( P = \dfrac{F_\perp}{A} \). More precisely, \( P = \lim_{\Delta A\to 0} \dfrac{\Delta F}{\Delta A} \) is the pressure at a point.
SI unit: pascal, 1 Pa = 1 N m⁻². Dimensions [ML⁻¹T⁻²], the same as stress and energy per unit volume.

Three facts about pressure in a fluid at rest. All three are tested:

  1. Pressure is a scalar. It has no direction. What has a direction is the force the fluid exerts on a surface, and that force is always normal (perpendicular) to the surface.
  2. At a point, pressure is the same in every direction. A tiny probe at one point feels the same push from above, below and the sides. (NCERT shows this with a small triangular wedge of fluid: the forces balance only if \(P_a = P_b = P_c\).)
  3. Pressure increases with depth. Deeper water has more water above it pushing down.
Exam trap · your Test-05 Q3

"Pressure at a point in a fluid acts vertically downward" is false. Pressure has no direction; it acts equally in all directions at a point. "The force exerted by a fluid at rest on a surface is normal to the surface" is true. So in that A–R question the assertion is false and the reason is true.

Why \(P = P_0 + \rho g h\)

Imagine a vertical cylinder of liquid with cross-section \(A\) and height \(h\), its top at the surface. It is in equilibrium, so the upward push on its bottom must hold up the downward push on its top plus its own weight:

\( P A = P_0 A + mg = P_0 A + (\rho A h) g \;\Rightarrow\; P = P_0 + \rho g h \).

Note that \(A\) cancels. Pressure depends only on vertical depth, not on how wide the vessel is, what shape it has, or how much liquid there is.

Pressure at depth h
\[ P = P_0 + \rho g h \]
\(P_0\) = pressure at the free surface (usually atmospheric, 1.013 × 10⁵ Pa), ρ = liquid density, h = vertical depth below the surface.
Between any two points
\[ P_2 - P_1 = \rho g (h_2 - h_1) \]
Only the vertical separation matters. Points at the same level in the same connected liquid at rest have the same pressure.

Shape does not matter: the hydrostatic paradox

Fill three differently shaped vessels with the same base area to the same height. The pressure at the bottom, and so the force on the base, is the same in all three, even though they hold very different amounts of water. How can the base of the narrow-necked vessel feel more than the weight of the water in it? Because its sloping walls push down on the water. In the flared vessel the walls hold some of the water up.

Force on a side wall: why dams are thick at the bottom

Force on a vertical wall of width w, liquid depth H
\[ F = \underbrace{\frac{\rho g H}{2}}_{\text{average pressure}} \times (H w) = \frac{\rho g H^2 w}{2} \]
Gauge force (the atmosphere usually acts on both sides and cancels). The resultant acts at H/3 above the base. On the base itself, F = ρgH × (base area).
Worked example · Numerical

Find the absolute and gauge pressure 20 m below the surface of a lake. (\(P_0 = 1.01\times10^5\) Pa, \(g = 10\) m s⁻²)

Show solution
  1. Gauge: \(\rho g h = 1000 \times 10 \times 20 = 2.0\times10^5\) Pa.
  2. Absolute: \(P_0 + \rho g h = 1.01\times10^5 + 2.0\times10^5 = 3.01\times10^5\) Pa ≈ 3 atm.

Answer: gauge 2.0 × 10⁵ Pa, absolute 3.01 × 10⁵ Pa

Worked example · Numerical

A dam holds water 6 m deep along a width of 10 m. Find the total force of the water on the dam. (\(g = 10\) m s⁻²)

Show solution
  1. Average gauge pressure = \(\rho g H/2 = 1000 \times 10 \times 6 / 2 = 3\times10^4\) Pa.
  2. Area = \(6 \times 10 = 60\) m², so \(F = 3\times10^4 \times 60 = 1.8\times10^6\) N.

Answer: 1.8 × 10⁶ N (using ρgH over the whole wall would wrongly double it)

Worked example · Energy (like Test-05 Q4)

Two identical tanks, each of base area 3 m², stand side by side. One has water 8 m deep and the other 2 m deep. They are joined at the bottom by a thin pipe and the levels equalise. How much work does gravity do? (\(g = 10\) m s⁻²)

Show solution
  1. Final level = (8 + 2)/2 = 5 m in each tank.
  2. Effectively a 3 m thick layer (from 5 to 8 m) moves from the first tank into the second (from 2 to 5 m). Mass = \(1000 \times 3 \times 3 = 9000\) kg.
  3. Its centre of mass falls from 6.5 m to 3.5 m, a drop of 3 m.
  4. \(W = mg\Delta h = 9000 \times 10 \times 3 = 2.7\times10^5\) J.
  5. Shortcut: \(W = \rho g A (h_1 - h_2)^2/4\). In Test-05 Q4 (2 m², 6 m and 4 m) this gives \(1000\cdot10\cdot2\cdot4/4 = 2\times10^4\) J.

Answer: 2.7 × 10⁵ J

9.2.3 Core

Atmospheric pressure, gauge pressure and the U-tube

We live at the bottom of an ocean of air several hundred kilometres deep. Its weight presses on everything with about 10⁵ N on every square metre, roughly the weight of a 10-tonne truck on each m². We don't get crushed because the fluids inside our body push back just as hard.

You use this every day. When you drink through a straw, you don't "pull" the juice up. You lower the pressure inside the straw, and the atmosphere pressing on the juice in the glass pushes it up. A suction hook sticks because you squeeze the air out from behind it and the atmosphere then holds it against the wall.

Measuring it: the mercury barometer

Barometer
\[ P_0 = \rho_{Hg}\, g\, h \]
At sea level h = 0.76 m: \(13600 \times 9.8 \times 0.76 = 1.013\times10^5\) Pa. With water the column would be 10.3 m tall. The height is the vertical height. It does not depend on the tube's width or tilt; tilting only makes the column longer along the tube.

Gauge pressure vs absolute pressure

Absolute pressure P

The actual total pressure, measured from a perfect vacuum. Example: at 20 m depth in water, P ≈ 3 atm.

Gauge pressure P − P₀

The pressure above atmospheric. Tyre gauges and BP machines read this. At 20 m depth the gauge pressure is ρgh ≈ 2 atm. A negative gauge pressure means a partial vacuum (suction).

Open-tube manometer
\[ P_{\text{gas}} = P_0 + \rho g h \qquad (h = \text{difference in liquid levels}) \]
If the open side stands higher, the gas pressure is above atmospheric (h positive). If the open side is lower, the gas pressure is below atmospheric (subtract).

U-tube with two liquids that don't mix

Pour oil into one arm of a U-tube containing water. The oil floats on the water and pushes the water up the other arm. To solve these, use one rule: in the same liquid, at the same level, at rest, the pressure is the same. Choose the level of the oil–water interface. Below it, both arms contain water.

\(P_0 + \rho_1 g h_1 = P_0 + \rho_2 g h_2 \;\Rightarrow\; \rho_1 h_1 = \rho_2 h_2\), with both heights measured from the interface level.

Exam trap

In two-liquid U-tubes, students measure heights from the bottom of the tube. Always measure from the interface level. Also read carefully whether the question asks for the water column above the interface, or the difference between the two free surfaces.

Worked example · U-tube

Water is in a U-tube. Oil (ρ = 900 kg m⁻³) is poured into the left arm until the oil column is 12 cm long. How far above the oil–water interface does the water stand in the right arm, and which free surface is higher?

Show solution
  1. Equal pressure at the interface level: \(900 \times 12 = 1000 \times h_2\).
  2. \(h_2 = 10.8\) cm above the interface.
  3. The oil surface is 12 − 10.8 = 1.2 cm higher than the water surface.

Answer: 10.8 cm; the oil surface is 1.2 cm higher

Worked example · Manometer

An open mercury manometer attached to a gas cylinder shows the mercury 20 cm higher in the open arm. Find the gauge and absolute pressure of the gas in pascal. (P₀ = 1.013 × 10⁵ Pa, g = 9.8 m s⁻²)

Show solution
  1. Gauge = \(\rho g h = 13600 \times 9.8 \times 0.20 = 2.67\times10^4\) Pa.
  2. Absolute = \(1.013\times10^5 + 0.267\times10^5 = 1.28\times10^5\) Pa (= 96 cm of Hg).

Answer: gauge 2.67 × 10⁴ Pa, absolute 1.28 × 10⁵ Pa

9.2.4 Core

Pascal's law and hydraulic machines

Squeeze the end of a toothpaste tube and paste comes out at the nozzle, far from your fingers. Press one side of a water-filled balloon and it bulges everywhere else. An enclosed liquid passes any extra push on to every part of itself. That is Pascal's law, and it lets a mechanic lift a car with one hand.

Pascal's law (two forms)

(1) The pressure in a fluid at rest is the same at all points at the same height. (If gravity is ignored, it is the same everywhere.)
(2) A change in pressure applied to an enclosed incompressible fluid is transmitted undiminished to every point of the fluid and to the walls of the container.

The hydraulic lift

Two pistons of areas \(A_1\) (small) and \(A_2\) (large) sit on the same enclosed liquid. Push the small one with \(F_1\). That adds pressure \(P = F_1/A_1\), which reaches the large piston unchanged, so it is pushed up with \(F_2 = P A_2\).

Force multiplication
\[ \frac{F_1}{A_1} = \frac{F_2}{A_2} \;\Rightarrow\; F_2 = F_1 \frac{A_2}{A_1} = F_1\left(\frac{d_2}{d_1}\right)^2 \]
Mechanical advantage = A₂/A₁. For round pistons, area ∝ (diameter)², so square the diameter ratio.
No free energy
\[ A_1 x_1 = A_2 x_2, \qquad F_1 x_1 = F_2 x_2 \]
The liquid volume pushed out of the small cylinder equals the volume entering the big one. The big piston rises only x₂ = x₁A₁/A₂, so work in = work out (ideal machine).
Exam trap · your Test-05 Q5

Arms of diameter 1.4 cm and 7 cm, 20 N applied. The diameter ratio is 5, but force scales with area, so the factor is 5² = 25 and F₂ = 500 N. The answer 100 N comes from using the diameter ratio directly. Q1 is the same idea: syringes of 1 cm and 3 cm, 10 N → 10 × 9 = 90 N. If radii are given, square them the same way.

Hydraulic brakes. A light push on the brake pedal moves a piston in a small master cylinder. The pressure travels through the brake oil to larger pistons at each wheel, which press the brake pads with a much larger force. Because the pressure is the same everywhere in the oil, all wheels brake equally.

Worked example · Numerical

In a hydraulic press the piston diameters are 2 cm and 10 cm. A force of 30 N is applied to the small piston and it moves down 50 cm. Find the force on the large piston and how far it rises.

Show solution
  1. Area ratio \(= (10/2)^2 = 25\), so \(F_2 = 30 \times 25 = 750\) N.
  2. Volume is conserved: \(x_2 = x_1 \times A_1/A_2 = 50/25 = 2\) cm.
  3. Check: work in = \(30 \times 0.5 = 15\) J; work out = \(750 \times 0.02 = 15\) J. ✓

Answer: 750 N; the large piston rises 2 cm

Worked example · Numerical

A car lift has a large piston of area 0.2 m² carrying a car of mass 1600 kg. What force must be applied on the small piston of area 0.002 m²? What is the pressure in the oil? (g = 10 m s⁻²)

Show solution
  1. Load \(F_2 = 1600 \times 10 = 1.6\times10^4\) N. Pressure \(= F_2/A_2 = 8\times10^4\) Pa.
  2. \(F_1 = P A_1 = 8\times10^4 \times 0.002 = 160\) N.

Answer: 160 N; pressure 8 × 10⁴ Pa (gauge)

Worked example · Statement I / II

Statement I: A hydraulic lift multiplies force. Statement II: A hydraulic lift multiplies energy, because the output force is larger than the input force.

Show solution
  1. I is correct: F₂ = F₁A₂/A₁.
  2. II is incorrect: the large piston moves a smaller distance, so F₁x₁ = F₂x₂. No machine creates energy.

Answer: Statement I correct, Statement II incorrect

Buoyancy, Archimedes and floating

In a swimming pool you can lift a friend with one arm. A steel ship weighing thousands of tonnes floats, while a steel nail sinks. A bucket of water feels lighter while it is still under the surface of the well. All of these come from one upward push, the buoyant force (upthrust).

Where does it come from? Think of a cube held under water. Pressure pushes on all six faces. The side pushes cancel. But the bottom face is deeper than the top face, so it is pushed harder: \(F_B = (P_{bottom} - P_{top})A = \rho_l g h A = \rho_l g V\). The net push is upward and equals the weight of a cube of liquid the same size. The material of the body does not appear anywhere in this result.

Archimedes' principle

A body wholly or partly immersed in a fluid experiences an upward buoyant force equal to the weight of the fluid it displaces. The force acts at the centre of buoyancy, the centre of mass of the displaced fluid.

Exam trap · your Test-05 Q15

Buoyant force = weight of the liquid displaced. It is not "the weight of the body" (that equality holds only for a floating body), not "the volume of the body", and not "the weight of the body minus the weight of the liquid". For a fully immersed body it does not depend on depth or on what the body is made of.

Buoyant force
\[ F_B = \rho_l\, V_{sub}\, g \]
ρ_l = density of the liquid, V_sub = submerged volume. F_B vs V_sub is a straight line through the origin with slope ρ_l g, and it becomes flat once the body is fully under (Test-05 Q36).
Floating (equilibrium)
\[ \rho_b V g = \rho_l V_{sub} g \;\Rightarrow\; \frac{V_{sub}}{V} = \frac{\rho_b}{\rho_l} \]
Fraction submerged = density ratio. Ice (900) in water: 90% under. Wood (600) in water: 60% under.
Apparent weight (fully immersed)
\[ W_{app} = W - F_B = W\left(1 - \frac{\rho_l}{\rho_b}\right) \]
Loss of weight = F_B. Relative density of a solid = W_air ÷ (W_air − W_water).
Body at the interface of two liquids
\[ \rho_b V = \rho_1 V_1 + \rho_2 V_2 \]
V₁ + V₂ = V. The body's density is the volume-weighted average of the two liquids.

Level-change puzzles (Test-05 Q12 type)

Use one idea: a floating object displaces its own weight of water; a sunk object displaces only its own volume. For anything denser than water, displacing its weight takes more water than displacing its volume.

Exam trap · your Test-05 Q12

A block carrying a coin floats in a tank. The coin slips off and sinks. Two things happen: the block rises (it is lighter, so less of it needs to be under water, and L, its height above the water, increases), and the water level h falls (the coin now displaces only its own volume). Students often say "the level stays the same". It doesn't.

More things that get asked

  • Extra load to sink a floater (Q11): extra mass needed = ρ_l × (unsubmerged volume). A 1 m³ cube half under water needs 1000 × 0.5 = 500 kg more.
  • Burning candle (Q6): the fraction submerged stays ρ_c/ρ_w. If the candle shortens at rate r, the submerged length shrinks at (ρ_c/ρ_w)r, so the bottom rises at that rate. Half-submerged, burning 4 cm/h → bottom rises 2 cm/h.
  • Hollow and variable-density bodies (Q9, Q13): use the average density = total mass ÷ total volume. "Just floats" (fully submerged at rest) means ρ_avg = ρ_l. Worked out in Problem patterns.
  • Body on a spring, fully submerged (Q10): if it hangs from a spring, kx = mg − F_B. If it is tied to the bottom by a spring (a floater held under), kx = F_B − mg.
  • Accelerating container: in a lift accelerating up with a, replace g by g + a. Weight and buoyancy both scale, so the fraction submerged does not change. In free fall there is no buoyancy at all.
  • Rising body (like a bubble, Q14): with drag neglected, net upward force F_B − W = ma gives a = (ρ_l/ρ_b − 1)g. For a = g/100, ρ_l/ρ_b = 1.01.
Worked example · Numerical (like Q8, Q11)

A cube of side 0.5 m floats in water with 40% of its volume submerged. (a) What is its density? (b) What extra mass placed on it will just submerge it completely?

Show solution
  1. (a) Fraction submerged = ρ_b/ρ_w ⇒ ρ_b = 0.4 × 1000 = 400 kg m⁻³.
  2. (b) Volume = 0.125 m³. Unsubmerged part = 0.6 × 0.125 = 0.075 m³.
  3. To push it all under, the extra weight must equal the extra buoyancy: Δm = 1000 × 0.075 = 75 kg.

Answer: 400 kg m⁻³; 75 kg

Worked example · Ratio (like Q7, Q45)

A sphere of density d floats at the interface of two immiscible liquids of densities 0.8d (upper) and 1.4d (lower), fully submerged. Find the ratio of its volume in the lower liquid to that in the upper liquid.

Show solution
  1. Let a fraction x be in the lower liquid: \(d = 1.4d\,x + 0.8d(1 - x)\).
  2. \(1 = 0.8 + 0.6x \Rightarrow x = 1/3\).
  3. Lower : upper = 1/3 : 2/3 = 1 : 2.
  4. General form (Q45): with liquids ρ (upper) and nρ (lower) and fraction p in the lower one, \(d = p(n\rho) + (1-p)\rho = \{1 + (n-1)p\}\rho\).

Answer: 1 : 2

Worked example · Apparent weight

A stone of mass 2 kg and density 2500 kg m⁻³ is fully immersed in water. Find the buoyant force and its apparent weight. (g = 10 m s⁻²)

Show solution
  1. Volume = 2/2500 = 8 × 10⁻⁴ m³.
  2. F_B = 1000 × 8 × 10⁻⁴ × 10 = 8 N.
  3. Apparent weight = 20 − 8 = 12 N. Check: W(1 − ρ_l/ρ_b) = 20(1 − 0.4) = 12 N. ✓

Answer: 8 N; 12 N

9.3 Basics

Streamline, laminar and turbulent flow

Watch smoke rising from an incense stick. For the first few centimetres it rises as a smooth, steady thread. Higher up it suddenly breaks into swirls and eddies. A slow river glides smoothly; white-water rapids churn. These are the two kinds of flow: streamline (smooth) and turbulent (chaotic).

Steady flow

The flow is steady if the velocity of the fluid at each fixed point in space does not change with time. Different points can have different velocities. A single particle can speed up or slow down as it moves from one point to another.

Exam trap · your Test-05 Q16

"In steady flow the velocity of every fluid particle is constant" is false. Steady means constant velocity at a point, not for a particle. A particle entering a narrow section speeds up (continuity), yet the flow is still perfectly steady, because every particle that passes that point has the same velocity there.

Streamline. In steady flow, the path a particle follows is a streamline. Every particle passing through a given point follows the same streamline. The tangent to a streamline at any point gives the direction of the velocity there.

  • Two streamlines never cross. At a crossing a particle would have two velocities at once, which is impossible.
  • Crowded streamlines mean fast flow; widely spaced streamlines mean slow flow. (You will see this in the continuity and aerofoil sims.)
  • Tube of flow: a bundle of streamlines. No fluid crosses its side walls, so whatever mass enters one end must leave the other. That gives the equation of continuity.

Streamline / laminar

Smooth, orderly; layers (laminae) slide past each other. Velocity at a point is constant in time. Happens at low speeds. Example: slow honey, gentle stream, the low part of incense smoke.

Turbulent

Irregular, with eddies and swirls; velocity at a point changes randomly. Happens above a critical speed. Examples: rapids, wake of a ship, smoke higher up, blood flow past a narrowed artery. Turbulence dissipates energy.

Link to Fluids II: Reynolds number

Whether a flow is laminar or turbulent depends on \( R_e = \dfrac{\rho v d}{\eta} \) (d = size of pipe or object, η = viscosity). Roughly, Re < 1000 is laminar and Re > 2000 is turbulent. Details are in Fluids II. For Test-05 Q18: a smaller model (smaller d) needs a larger v to reach the same Re, so turbulence starts at a higher speed for the small model.

Worked example · Assertion–Reason

A: Water flowing through a pipe that narrows can be in steady flow even though each particle speeds up in the narrow part. R: Steady flow requires the velocity at every point of space to remain constant in time.

Show solution
  1. R is the definition of steady flow, so R is true.
  2. Speeding up as a particle moves from one point to another does not change the velocity at any fixed point over time. So A is true, and R explains it.

Answer: both true, R is the correct explanation of A

Worked example · Concept

Which of these flows is most likely turbulent? (a) honey poured slowly (b) water in a thin capillary (c) a river in flood through a rocky gorge (d) blood in a capillary.

Show solution
  1. Turbulence needs a high Re = ρvd/η: high speed, large size, low viscosity.
  2. Honey (very high η), capillaries (tiny d) and blood in capillaries (tiny d, slow) are all laminar. The flooded river has high speed and large size.

Answer: (c)

9.3 Core

Equation of continuity

Put your thumb over the end of a garden hose and the water shoots out much faster. A river flows lazily where it is wide and races through a narrow gorge. The water has nowhere else to go: the same amount must pass every section each second, so where the passage is narrow the water must move faster.

Derivation. In time Δt, the fluid entering section 1 occupies a length v₁Δt, so its mass is ρ₁A₁v₁Δt. The same mass must leave through section 2 (no fluid crosses the walls of the tube of flow, and none piles up in steady flow): ρ₁A₁v₁ = ρ₂A₂v₂. For an incompressible liquid ρ₁ = ρ₂.

Equation of continuity (conservation of mass)
\[ A_1 v_1 = A_2 v_2 = Q \]
Q = volume flow rate (m³ s⁻¹), constant along the pipe. Mass flow rate = ρQ (kg s⁻¹).
Round pipes
\[ v \propto \frac{1}{A} \propto \frac{1}{d^2}, \qquad \frac{v_1}{v_2} = \left(\frac{d_2}{d_1}\right)^2 \]
Halve the diameter → 4× the speed. Kinetic energy per volume ½ρv² ∝ 1/d⁴.
Branching and many holes
\[ A v = \sum_i A_i v_i, \qquad A_1 v_1 = n\,a\,v_2 \]
Flow in = total flow out. Spray pumps and shower heads: n holes, each of area a.

Why a falling stream of tap water narrows (Test-05 Q25). Once it leaves the tap, water speeds up under gravity: \(v^2 = v_0^2 + 2gh\). Since Av is constant, a larger v means a smaller A, so the stream gets thinner as it falls. This is continuity, not surface tension.

Exam trap

Areas go as the square of diameters (or radii). Test-05 Q22: diameters 6.4 cm and 3.2 cm → area ratio 4 → v_min : v_max = 1 : 4 (not 1 : 2). Q27: diameter ratio 2.5 : 3.75 = 2 : 3 → speed ratio 9 : 4 → KE per volume ratio (9/4)² = 81 : 16.

Worked example · Numerical (like Q19)

A spray pump has a cylindrical tube of cross-section 6 cm² ending in a nozzle with 30 fine holes, each of area 0.5 mm². Liquid moves in the tube at 1.5 m per minute. Find the speed at which it leaves the holes.

Show solution
  1. Convert: A₁ = 6 × 10⁻⁴ m², v₁ = 1.5/60 = 0.025 m s⁻¹, total hole area = 30 × 0.5 × 10⁻⁶ = 1.5 × 10⁻⁵ m².
  2. \(v_2 = \dfrac{A_1 v_1}{n a} = \dfrac{6\times10^{-4}\times0.025}{1.5\times10^{-5}} = 1\) m s⁻¹.

Answer: 1 m s⁻¹ (for Q19's numbers: 8 cm², 20 × 1 mm², 1 m/min → 2/3 m s⁻¹)

Worked example · Branching (like Q44)

Water flows at 2 m s⁻¹ in a pipe of area 3A. It splits into two branches of areas A and 2A. The speed in the first branch is 3 m s⁻¹. Find the speed in the second.

Show solution
  1. Flow in = flow out: \(3A \times 2 = A \times 3 + 2A \times V\).
  2. \(6 = 3 + 2V \Rightarrow V = 1.5\) m s⁻¹.

Answer: 1.5 m s⁻¹ (Q44: 2A·4 = (A/2)·4 + A·V → V = 6 m s⁻¹)

Worked example · Tap stream

Water leaves a tap at 1 m s⁻¹ with cross-section 1 cm². What is the cross-section of the stream 15 cm below the tap? (g = 10 m s⁻²)

Show solution
  1. \(v^2 = 1^2 + 2(10)(0.15) = 4 \Rightarrow v = 2\) m s⁻¹.
  2. \(A = A_0 v_0 / v = 1 \times 1/2 = 0.5\) cm². (The radius shrinks by a factor √2.)

Answer: 0.5 cm²

9.4 Core

Bernoulli's principle

Hold a strip of paper just below your lips and blow over it: it rises. Hang two sheets of paper close together and blow between them: they come together, not apart. A shower curtain gets sucked inward when the shower runs. In each case fast-moving air has lower pressure. Bernoulli's principle explains why, and it is just energy conservation for a flowing fluid.

The idea behind the derivation

Follow a small slug of fluid of volume ΔV and mass Δm = ρΔV along a tube of flow, from section 1 (pressure P₁, speed v₁, height h₁) to section 2 (P₂, v₂, h₂).

  1. The fluid behind pushes it forward: work done = P₁A₁Δx₁ = P₁ΔV. The fluid ahead pushes back: work = −P₂ΔV. Net work by pressure = (P₁ − P₂)ΔV.
  2. Work–energy theorem: this net work = change in KE + change in PE = ½Δm(v₂² − v₁²) + Δm g(h₂ − h₁).
  3. Divide by ΔV (with Δm/ΔV = ρ) and rearrange: P₁ + ½ρv₁² + ρgh₁ = P₂ + ½ρv₂² + ρgh₂.
Bernoulli's equation (along a streamline)
\[ P + \tfrac{1}{2}\rho v^2 + \rho g h = \text{constant} \]
Each term is energy per unit volume (J m⁻³ = Pa): P = pressure energy, ½ρv² = kinetic energy, ρgh = potential energy, all per unit volume. Valid for steady, incompressible, non-viscous, irrotational flow.
Per unit mass
\[ \frac{P}{\rho} + \frac{v^2}{2} + g h = \text{const} \]
Units J kg⁻¹.
Per unit weight ("heads")
\[ \frac{P}{\rho g} + \frac{v^2}{2g} + h = \text{const} \]
Pressure head + velocity head + elevation head, each a length (m).
Exam traps · your Test-05 Q21 and Q42
  • Bernoulli's equation is the law of conservation of energy (Q42). Continuity is conservation of mass. Neither is conservation of linear momentum.
  • Correct form (Q21): \(P + \tfrac12\rho v^2 + \rho g h\). Watch for wrong options like \(P + \tfrac12\rho v + \rho g h\), \(P + \tfrac12 v^2 + gh\) (mixing per-volume and per-mass terms), or \(P + \rho v^2 + \rho g h\).
  • For a fluid at rest (v = 0) it reduces to P + ρgh = const, which is the hydrostatics you already know.

Static, dynamic and stagnation pressure

In a horizontal pipe, P + ½ρv² = const. P is the static pressure (what a gauge moving with the fluid would read). ½ρv² is the dynamic pressure. Where the fluid is brought to rest, at the nose of an object or the mouth of a tube facing the flow, all of it turns into pressure. The stagnation pressure there is P + ½ρv².

An L-shaped (pitot) tube with its open end facing the current therefore holds a column h = v²/2g higher than a plain vertical tube (Test-05 Q39).

Shortcuts
  • Horizontal pipe: narrowest section → maximum speed → minimum pressure; widest section → minimum speed → maximum pressure (Q41).
  • Liquid escaping from a region at pressure P₁ into a region at P₂ through a valve: ½ρv² = P₁ − P₂, so v ∝ √(P₁ − P₂) (Q43).
  • ΔP between two sections of a horizontal pipe = ½ρ(v₂² − v₁²). Find v₂ from continuity first.
Worked example · Numerical

Water flows at 2 m s⁻¹ through a horizontal pipe. At a constriction the area halves. Find the pressure difference between the wide and narrow parts.

Show solution
  1. Continuity: v₂ = 2 × 2 = 4 m s⁻¹.
  2. \(P_1 - P_2 = \tfrac12\rho(v_2^2 - v_1^2) = \tfrac12(1000)(16 - 4) = 6000\) Pa.

Answer: 6.0 × 10³ Pa (higher in the wide part)

Worked example · Numerical with height

Water at pressure 2.0 × 10⁵ Pa flows at 3 m s⁻¹ in a pipe at ground level. The pipe rises 5 m and its area halves. Find the pressure at the upper point. (g = 10 m s⁻²)

Show solution
  1. Continuity: v₂ = 6 m s⁻¹.
  2. \(P_2 = P_1 - \tfrac12\rho(v_2^2 - v_1^2) - \rho g h = 2.0\times10^5 - 500(36 - 9) - 1000(10)(5)\).
  3. \(= 200000 - 13500 - 50000 = 1.365\times10^5\) Pa.

Answer: 1.37 × 10⁵ Pa

Worked example · Pitot tube (like Q39)

A river flows at 2 m s⁻¹. An L-shaped glass tube is dipped in it with its open end facing upstream. How high above the river surface does water rise in the vertical arm? (g = 10 m s⁻²)

Show solution
  1. At the tube's mouth the water is brought to rest, so its kinetic energy per volume ½ρv² becomes extra pressure.
  2. That pressure holds a column h with ρgh = ½ρv² ⇒ h = v²/2g = 4/20 = 0.2 m.

Answer: 20 cm above the surface

9.4.1 Exam

Speed of efflux: Torricelli's law

Poke holes at different heights in a plastic bottle full of water. The lowest hole squirts hardest, because it has the most water above it. But it does not necessarily throw the water farthest: a hole lower down also has less height to fall from. That trade-off is the most-asked graph and range question in this chapter.

Derivation. Apply Bernoulli between a point on the free surface (pressure P₀, speed ≈ 0 because the tank is much wider than the hole) and the hole at depth h below the surface (pressure P₀, since it opens to air, speed v):

\(P_0 + 0 + \rho g h = P_0 + \tfrac12\rho v^2 + 0 \;\Rightarrow\; v = \sqrt{2gh}\).

This is the same speed a stone gains falling freely through h. Here h is measured down from the free surface, not up from the bottom.

Speed of efflux
\[ v = \sqrt{2 g h} \]
h = depth of the hole below the free surface. Independent of the liquid's density and the hole's size.
Extra pressure on the surface (closed tank, piston, load)
\[ v = \sqrt{2gh + \frac{2(P - P_0)}{\rho}} \]
An extra pressure ΔP on the surface adds ΔP/ρg to the effective head (Test-05 Q35).
Horizontal range on the ground
\[ R = v\sqrt{\frac{2y}{g}} = 2\sqrt{h\,y} = 2\sqrt{h(H-h)} \]
h = depth below surface, y = height of hole above the ground, H = h + y (liquid surface above the ground). Maximum R = H when h = y = H/2. Holes at depth h and at depth H − h give the same range.
Time to empty (hole at the bottom)
\[ t = \frac{A}{a}\sqrt{\frac{2H}{g}} \;\propto\; \sqrt{H} \]
A = tank area, a = hole area. From H₁ to H₂: \( t = \frac{A}{a}\sqrt{\frac{2}{g}}\,(\sqrt{H_1} - \sqrt{H_2}) \). The first half of the height drains in only 29% of the total time.
Thrust (reaction) of the jet
\[ F = \rho a v^2 = 2\rho a g h \]
Momentum per second carried out by the jet. This is twice the hydrostatic force ρgh·a on a plugged hole. Two holes on opposite walls at depths h₁, h₂: net force 2ρag(h₂ − h₁) (Q40).
Exam traps · your Test-05 Q30, Q31
  • Maximum range needs the hole at half the height of the liquid surface above the ground (Q30). If the tank stands on a block, measure H from the ground, not from the tank's base.
  • Time to empty ∝ √H, not ∝ H (Q31). Four times the height takes twice the time. The outflow slows down as the level falls.
  • v = √(2gh) uses depth below the surface. If a question gives the hole's height above the base, use h = H − y.
Worked example · Range (like Q34)

A tank stands on a platform. A small hole in its side is 45 cm below the water surface and 80 cm above the ground. How far from the tank does the jet hit the ground?

Show solution
  1. \(R = 2\sqrt{h y} = 2\sqrt{45 \times 80} = 2\sqrt{3600} = 120\) cm.
  2. Q34 is the same shape: hole 70 cm below the surface and 140 cm above the ground → \(2\sqrt{70\times140} = 140\sqrt2\) cm.

Answer: 120 cm = 1.2 m

Worked example · Extra pressure (like Q35)

A tank of water has a hole 0.8 m below the surface. A piston resting on the water adds a pressure of 10⁴ Pa. Find the efflux speed. (g = 10 m s⁻²)

Show solution
  1. \(\tfrac12\rho v^2 = \Delta P + \rho g h\) ⇒ \(v^2 = 2(\Delta P/\rho + gh) = 2(10 + 8) = 36\).
  2. \(v = 6\) m s⁻¹. (Without the piston it would be √16 = 4 m s⁻¹.)

Answer: 6 m s⁻¹

Worked example · Time and ratio (like Q31)

A tank of area 1 m² holds water 5 m deep and drains through a hole of area 10 cm² at its bottom. (a) How long does it take to empty? (b) How long to drain from 5 m to 1.25 m? (g = 10 m s⁻²)

Show solution
  1. (a) \(A/a = 1/10^{-3} = 1000\). \(t = 1000\sqrt{2\times5/10} = 1000\) s.
  2. (b) \(t = 1000\sqrt{2/10}\,(\sqrt5 - \sqrt{1.25}) = 1000 \times 0.447 \times 1.118 = 500\) s.
  3. So three-quarters of the height drains in half the time, and the last 1.25 m takes the other 500 s.

Answer: (a) 1000 s; (b) 500 s

The venturimeter

How do you measure how much water flows through a pipe each second without putting anything in its way? Narrow the pipe smoothly to a throat and back again. In the throat the water speeds up (continuity), so its pressure drops (Bernoulli). Measure that pressure drop with a manometer and you can calculate the flow rate. That device is a venturimeter, and it uses both laws together.

Two equations, two unknowns
\[ A_1v_1 = A_2v_2, \qquad P_1 - P_2 = \tfrac12\rho\,(v_2^2 - v_1^2) \]
Substitute v₂ = v₁A₁/A₂ into Bernoulli and solve for v₁.
Venturimeter result
\[ v_1 = \sqrt{\frac{2\,\Delta P}{\rho\left[(A_1/A_2)^2 - 1\right]}}, \qquad Q = A_1 v_1 = A_1A_2\sqrt{\frac{2\,\Delta P}{\rho\,(A_1^2 - A_2^2)}} \]
ΔP = P₁ − P₂. With vertical tubes of the same liquid, ΔP = ρgΔh. With a U-tube manometer holding a heavier liquid ρ_m (e.g. mercury under water), ΔP = (ρ_m − ρ)gh.
How it is asked
  • "A venturimeter works on the principle of …" → Bernoulli's theorem (together with continuity) (Test-05 Q20).
  • Given A₁, A₂ and the pressure difference (or Δh), find Q. Convert cm² → m² (× 10⁻⁴) before using the formula, then convert back.
  • Ratio form: if Δh is made 4 times larger, Q doubles (Q ∝ √Δh).
Worked example · Numerical (like Q26)

Water flows through a horizontal pipe of cross-section 20 cm² that narrows to 10 cm². The pressure difference between the two sections is 60 Pa. Find the volume flow rate.

Show solution
  1. A₁ = 2 × 10⁻³ m², A₂ = 1 × 10⁻³ m², A₁² − A₂² = 3 × 10⁻⁶ m⁴.
  2. \(Q = A_1A_2\sqrt{\dfrac{2\Delta P}{\rho(A_1^2 - A_2^2)}} = 2\times10^{-6}\sqrt{\dfrac{120}{1000\times3\times10^{-6}}} = 2\times10^{-6}\times200\).
  3. Q = 4 × 10⁻⁴ m³ s⁻¹ = 400 cm³ s⁻¹. (For Q26's 15 Pa, the root is √10⁴ = 100, giving 200 cm³ s⁻¹.)

Answer: 400 cm³ s⁻¹

Worked example · Manometer heights

In a venturimeter (A₁ = 10 cm², A₂ = 5 cm²) carrying water, the vertical tubes over the wide part and the throat differ in height by 15 cm. Find v₁, v₂ and Q. (g = 10 m s⁻²)

Show solution
  1. ΔP = ρgΔh = 1000 × 10 × 0.15 = 1500 Pa. Area ratio = 2.
  2. \(v_1 = \sqrt{\dfrac{2\times1500}{1000\,(4 - 1)}} = 1\) m s⁻¹, so v₂ = 2 m s⁻¹.
  3. Q = A₁v₁ = 10⁻³ m³ s⁻¹ = 1000 cm³ s⁻¹ (1 litre per second).
  4. Check: ½ρ(v₂² − v₁²) = 500 × 3 = 1500 Pa. ✓

Answer: 1 m s⁻¹, 2 m s⁻¹, 1 L s⁻¹
